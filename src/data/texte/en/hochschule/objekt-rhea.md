# Rhea

With a volume-equivalent spherical radius of 763.3 km (semi-axes
$765 \times 763 \times 762\,\mathrm{km}$), Rhea is the second-largest moon of Saturn, but
markedly smaller than [Titan](objekt:titan) (2575 km). Gravity-field and moment-of-inertia
measurements from several close Cassini flybys point to a comparatively homogeneous mixture of
ice and rock, rather than the pronounced differentiation one would expect for such a large icy
moon ([Iess et al. 2007](literatur:iess-2007);
[Anderson and Schubert 2007](literatur:anderson-2007)); a later study combining two flybys leaves
open whether that picture actually holds (see Interior). The surface is old and densely
cratered, with the large impact basin Tirawa and the young, bright-rayed crater Inktomi; on the
trailing hemisphere lies a version of "wispy terrain" made of tectonic ice cliffs, similar to but
fainter than [Dione's](objekt:dione). In 2010 Cassini's mass spectrometer INMS directly detected
a wafer-thin oxygen–carbon dioxide exosphere – the first direct detection of an oxidizing
exosphere at an icy moon other than Europa ([Teolis et al. 2010](literatur:teolis-2010)). A ring
system inferred from plasma measurements in 2008 was disproved two years later by a targeted
imaging search – a cautionary tale about how an indirect measurement can mislead. This text
covers parameters and measurement, interior, surface, atmosphere and magnetosphere, orbit,
rotation and dynamics, and formation and evolution, and closes with what Orrery models of it.

## Parameters and measurement

Cassini's radio tracking (ephemeris SAT441) yields Rhea's mass parameter directly from the
Doppler shift of the spacecraft's radio signal; combined with the volume derived from Cassini
images, this gives a mean density of $1.2372 \pm 0.0029\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ – barely more
than pure water ice ($0.917\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$) ([JPL SSD satellite
data](quelle:jpl-satelliten)). Rhea is slightly triaxial: $765 \times 763 \times
762\,\mathrm{km}$, from the same photogrammetric control-point network over Cassini images of the
prime mission as for the other mid-sized Saturnian moons
([Thomas 2010](literatur:thomas-2010)); the volume-equivalent spherical radius from these three
semi-axes is $\sqrt[3]{765 \cdot 763 \cdot 762} = 763.3\,\mathrm{km}$ (derivation) – the value
also carried in the catalog.

Using the catalog mass gives a density of about $1.24\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$: assuming a mix of
water ice ($917\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$) and silicate rock ($3000\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$), that
corresponds to a rock mass fraction of roughly 38% (derivation, the same simplified
two-component model as for [Dione](objekt:dione) and Tethys). Anderson and Schubert (2007), using
their own material densities (ice $930$, rock $3250\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$) and additionally
accounting for compression of the ice with depth, instead arrive at about one quarter rock – a
difference in assumptions, not a contradiction in the measured density itself. For the moment of
inertia factor and core formation in moons generally, see [Interior structure](thema:innerer-aufbau).

## Interior

A close Cassini flyby on 26 November 2005 provided the first gravity-field and
moment-of-inertia data for Rhea. Two independent analyses of the same tracking data arrived at a
consistent picture: a mean density near $1233\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ and a moment-of-inertia
factor consistent with a largely homogeneous, undifferentiated body – unusual for an icy moon
this size, where a clear separation of rock and ice would normally be expected
([Iess et al. 2007](literatur:iess-2007);
[Anderson and Schubert 2007](literatur:anderson-2007)).

A second close flyby in March 2013 allowed a joint analysis of both encounters with a fully
unconstrained degree-2 gravity field. The result shows a statistically significant departure
from the hydrostatic expectation: the ratio $J_2/C_{22} = 3.9 \pm 0.1$ lies well above the
hydrostatic value $10/3 \approx 3.33$ – much as for [Dione](objekt:dione). Moment-of-inertia
factors estimated separately from $J_2$ and $C_{22}$ give two mutually incompatible,
physically implausible values of about 0.41 and 0.37; the moment of inertia can therefore no
longer be read off the field directly ([Tortora et al. 2016](literatur:tortora-2016)). The
observed gravitational oblateness matches the observed shape if Rhea is uniformly homogeneous;
but because the surface is more likely water ice than rock, a two-layer model of a silicate core
and an ice mantle (mantle density $920\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$) appears physically more plausible.
In that model, the core–mantle boundary must depart from the hydrostatic expectation by roughly
1 km to explain the measured gravity field – a stress of only about 0.3 MPa, readily supported
by cold silicate rock. A wide range of possible moments of inertia therefore remains permitted
([Tortora et al. 2016](literatur:tortora-2016)).

Whether Rhea's low degree of differentiation reflects too little radiogenic and accretional
heat to fully separate rock and ice during formation, or whether a later remixing – through
large impacts or convection, for instance – blended an initially more differentiated interior
back together, remains unresolved (see Open questions).

## Surface

In 1980 Voyager 1 distinguished two terrain types on Rhea: an old plain densely covered with
large craters, and regions with noticeably fewer large craters ([Smith et al.
1981](literatur:smith-1981)). The most prominent single feature is the impact basin Tirawa, about
350 km across, still recognizable as a ring structure with an inner ring of ridges and plateaus
roughly 180 km wide – comparable to the basin Odysseus on [Tethys](objekt:tethys), though less
pronounced; its slightly elliptical shape suggests an oblique impact. Markedly younger is the
complex crater Inktomi, about 47 km in diameter, whose bright ejecta rays extend over 400 km and
expose fresh, spectrally pure water ice; crater counts on the rays themselves mark it as one of
the youngest large craters among the inner Saturnian moons.

On the trailing hemisphere, similar to but fainter than at [Dione](objekt:dione), bright,
network-like streaks run across darker terrain. Voyager 1 interpreted them in 1980 as deposits of
frost or cryovolcanic ash ([Smith et al. 1981](literatur:smith-1981)); today, as at Dione, they
are understood as subsidence graben and fracture zones whose steep walls expose bright, fresh
water ice as darker surface material slides off them – the same shift from the original frost
interpretation to the tectonic one as there.

## Atmosphere and magnetosphere

Rhea carries no permanent atmosphere, but one of the thinnest exospheres ever measured directly
anywhere in the solar system. During three close flybys in 2005, 2007 and 2010 – the last at an
altitude of just 101 km – Cassini's ion and neutral mass spectrometer INMS and its plasma
spectrometer CAPS jointly detected molecular oxygen and carbon dioxide, at a peak-density ratio
of roughly 5 to 2. It was the first direct, in-situ detection of an oxygen-bearing exosphere at
any body other than Earth; the oxygen-rich exospheres of Europa and Ganymede had until then only
been inferred remotely by spectroscopy, not measured directly
([Teolis et al. 2010](literatur:teolis-2010)). The oxygen is thought to arise from radiolysis:
energetic charged particles from [Saturn's](objekt:saturn) magnetosphere strike the surface ice
and split water molecules, a process that slowly but steadily alters Rhea's ice. The source of the
carbon dioxide is less certain: candidates include dry ice trapped from the protoplanetary disk,
the same irradiation acting on organic material in the ice, or carbon-rich material delivered by
meteoritic impacts ([Teolis et al. 2010](literatur:teolis-2010)).

A later study of the same Cassini ultraviolet spectra found a hitherto unexplained broad
absorption feature near 184 nm on both of Rhea's hemispheres. Among the candidate molecules
tested, hydrazine monohydrate fits best and is chemically the easiest to explain – for instance
through irradiation of ammonia in the surface ice or, alternatively, through nitrogen escaping
[Titan](objekt:titan) and transported to Rhea ([Elowitz et al. 2021](literatur:elowitz-2021)). A
detection of hydrogen chloride, which would inevitably accompany the chlorine-bearing alternative
candidates, is however absent.

## Orbit, rotation and dynamics

Rhea orbits Saturn on a nearly circular, barely inclined path in 4.518 days (figures as in
[Saturn's Rings Edge-On](szene:saturn-ringkante)) and rotates synchronously. It lies in the outer
system, between the inner moons out to [Dione](objekt:dione) and the far more distant
[Titan](objekt:titan); unlike those, it is accompanied by no Lagrange or shepherd moons and is
not known to be in any mean-motion resonance with another Saturnian moon. Its apsidal and nodal
lines precess with periods of roughly 33.9 and 35.8 years respectively, markedly slower than for
the inner moons but considerably faster than for Titan or [Iapetus](objekt:iapetus) (more on this
pattern under [Orbital elements](thema:bahnelemente)).

## Formation and evolution

Like the other mid-sized Saturnian moons, Rhea probably formed from Saturn's circumplanetary
disk shortly after the planet itself ([as described for Mimas](objekt:mimas); for its place among
the other origin hypotheses, see [Formation of the Solar System](thema:entstehung)). Its low
degree of differentiation is an important constraint here: accretion models must explain either
a comparatively cool, slow accumulation without enough radiogenic and impact heat, or a mechanism
that later remixed an initially warmer, more differentiated interior.

A 2008 finding, derived from a sharp, symmetric dip in energetic electrons around Rhea together
with ultraviolet-bright spots along its equator, was initially interpreted as evidence for a
broad debris disk with possible individual rings around Rhea
([Jones et al. 2008](literatur:jones-2008)) – the first ring structure ever suspected around a
moon. A targeted imaging search with Cassini's narrow-angle camera from several viewing angles on
the suspected ring plane two years later found no material at all – neither narrow rings nor a
broad disk of anything from dust to larger chunks
([Tiscareno et al. 2010](literatur:tiscareno-2010)). The original electron signature remained
unexplained; it illustrates how carefully an indirect measurement must be interpreted before a
direct observation confirms or refutes it.

## Open questions

- **Why is Rhea so little differentiated?** Neither insufficient accretional heat nor a later
  remixing can be ruled out from the available gravity-field data alone; the two-layer model
  permits a wide range of possible core states without favoring any one of them
  ([Tortora et al. 2016](literatur:tortora-2016)).
- **Where does the exosphere's carbon dioxide come from, and what sustains it?** Unlike the
  oxygen, whose radiolytic origin is well supported, several sources remain open for the carbon
  dioxide, and no quantitative balance between ongoing loss to space and resupply from the
  surface is available ([Teolis et al. 2010](literatur:teolis-2010)).
- **Is the 184-nm absorption feature really hydrazine?** The spectroscopic fit is not unique, a
  direct laboratory confirmation on the original spectrum is missing, and both the in-ice
  formation route and long-range transport from Titan remain mere hypotheses
  ([Elowitz et al. 2021](literatur:elowitz-2021)).

## In the model

- **Orbital reference:** Like the other Saturnian moons, Rhea moves in the `parentEquator`
  reference on [Saturn's](objekt:saturn) equatorial plane, with osculating Horizons elements at
  epoch J2000 ($a = 527\,225.27\,\mathrm{km}$, $e = 0.00080$) – figures and convention as under
  [Orbital elements](thema:bahnelemente).
- **Orbital period and precession:** From `LDot`, $360^\circ/\dot L = 4.518$ days, carried over
  from [Saturn's Rings Edge-On](szene:saturn-ringkante). The data panel instead shows the Kepler
  orbital period computed from semi-major axis and masses (`umlaufzeitTage`), about 0.06% longer.
  The apsidal and nodal periods (33.939 and 35.775 years respectively, header comment in
  `saturn-monde.ts`) give, via
  $\mathrm{lpDot} = (360/P_\mathrm{apsis} - 360/P_\mathrm{knoten}) \cdot 100$ and
  $\mathrm{nodeDot} = -360/P_\mathrm{knoten} \cdot 100$, the rates carried in the data set of
  $54.44^\circ$ and $-1006.29^\circ$ per century respectively (worked out from the code).
- **Rotation and pole:** `rotationPeriodH` matches $360^\circ/\dot L$ to within
  $-0.018 \cdot 10^{-6}$ relative; the angle between the map center and Saturn's direction is
  $5.9^\circ$ at epoch and $5.8^\circ$ a hundred years later (the same figures as under
  [Tidal locking](thema:gebundene-rotation)). Rhea's pole ($40.38^\circ / 83.55^\circ$) is,
  unlike Mimas, Enceladus, Tethys and Dione, its own data-set value, slightly different from
  theirs, and lies only about $0.03^\circ$ from Saturn's own pole
  ([Archinal et al. 2018](literatur:archinal-2018); worked out from the code).
- **Albedo and texture:** The catalog albedo of 0.7 is the fact sheet's geometric albedo; the
  texture is a Cassini mosaic with no separate note about unlit areas (`ASSETS.md`).
- **Shadows:** `waehleOkkluder` selects the four moons with the largest rendered angular radius
  as sphere occluders for Saturn: Titan, Tethys, Dione and Rhea (figures as in
  [Eclipses](thema:finsternis), Saturn) – Rhea therefore casts a shadow on Saturn in the model.
  Its real shadow window is $\arcsin(R_\mathrm{Saturn}/a_\mathrm{Rhea}) \approx 6.3^\circ$
  (worked out from the code, formula as above), well below Saturn's $26.7^\circ$ axial tilt.
- **No ring, no exosphere:** Orrery's renderer knows neither rings around moons nor atmospheres
  or exospheres. That Rhea is therefore shown without a ring happens to match today's
  understanding in the case of the once-suspected, now-disproved ring system – not a deliberate
  modeling result, but a general simplification that affects every other ringless moon as well.
  The same simplification also omits the actually detected oxygen–carbon dioxide exosphere.
- **Scale:** Rhea is a satellite (`isSatellite`, its `parent` is Saturn, not the Sun); its orbital
  radius therefore scales, like its own and Saturn's radius, with `sizeScale`, not with the
  distance compression applied to planetary orbits.
- **Data panel:** The mass carried in the data set ($2.31 \cdot 10^{21}\,\mathrm{kg}$), times $G$
  per CODATA 2018, gives a $GM$ of $154.18\,\mathrm{km^3\,s^{-2}}$, about 1524 ppm (0.15%) above
  the value measured by radio tracking in the table above. Further simplifications:
  [Model limitations](thema:modell).
