# Oberon

Oberon is the outermost of Uranus's five large moons and, at 761.4 km in radius, the second
largest, and the reddest of the five. During Voyager 2's single flyby on 24 January 1986, the
probe saw only Oberon's sunlit southern hemisphere as well
([Smith et al. 1986](literatur:smith-1986)); since then, ground- and space-based telescopes, most
recently Hubble and the James Webb Space Telescope, have provided the only new data – unlike
[Titania](objekt:titania), Oberon has no analysed stellar occultation. This text covers
parameters, interior, surface, atmosphere and magnetosphere, orbit, rotation, and formation, and
finally describes what Orrery models of it.

## Parameters and measurement

Like Umbriel and Titania, Oberon appears spherical within measurement uncertainty in the
Voyager 2 limb-coordinate surveys ([Thomas 1988](literatur:thomas-1988)); a single radius value
suffices. As with the other moons, Oberon's mass parameter comes from a flyby trajectory plus
decades of ground-based astrometry ([Jacobson 2014](literatur:jacobson-2014)).

| Quantity | Value | Uncertainty | Method | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $GM$ | $205.3\,\mathrm{km^3\,s^{-2}}$ | $5.8\,\mathrm{km^3\,s^{-2}}$ | Flyby trajectory and astrometry | [JPL SSD satellite data](quelle:jpl-satelliten) |
| Radius (limb/IAU) | $761.4\,\mathrm{km}$ | $2.6\,\mathrm{km}$ | Limb coordinates | [JPL SSD satellite data](quelle:jpl-satelliten) |
| $\bar\rho$ | $1664\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ | $50\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ | from $GM$ and radius | [JPL SSD satellite data](quelle:jpl-satelliten) |
| Geometric albedo (visual) | $0.23$ | – | Fact sheet | [Uranian satellite fact sheet](quelle:nssdc-uranusmonde) |

With the catalogue mass ($2.88 \cdot 10^{21}\,\mathrm{kg}$) and the radius of 761.4 km, density
works out to about $1.558\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ (derivation) – matching the NSSDC fact-sheet value
(1560 kg/m³); the density computed from $GM$ and radius in the table above,
$1.664\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, rests on a slightly different, more recent mass solution – as with
[Ariel](objekt:ariel) (−3.0%), [Titania](objekt:titania) (−0.6%), and
[Umbriel](objekt:umbriel) (+4.5%), but for Oberon with the largest magnitude of the four
(+6.8%). By catalogue density, Oberon is third densest of the five: Titania (1663 kg/m³) and
Ariel (1587 kg/m³) exceed it slightly, Umbriel (1457) and Miranda (1204) fall below. With a visual
albedo of 0.23, Oberon sits between the darker Umbriel (0.21) and the brighter Titania (0.27) – a
rank HST multi-band photometry confirms independently
([Karkoschka 2001](literatur:karkoschka-2001)). Because Voyager 2 saw only the southern
hemisphere, all surface values apply to a good half of the moon.

## Interior

In the same simplified two-component model of water ice ($917\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$) and silicate
rock ($3000\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, as for [Miranda](objekt:miranda), [Ariel](objekt:ariel),
[Umbriel](objekt:umbriel), and [Iapetus](objekt:iapetus)), Oberon's catalogue density corresponds
to a rock mass fraction of about 59% (derivation) – between Ariel's 60% and Umbriel's 54%, well
above Miranda's one third and just below Titania's 65%. Whether the body is fully differentiated
cannot be decided without a dedicated gravity-field measurement (see
[Interior structure](thema:innerer-aufbau)). Interior-structure models for all five large Uranian
moons rank Oberon with Titania as the most promising candidates for a residual ocean under 50 km
thick, against at most 30 km for Ariel and Umbriel; Miranda is judged too small to still hold
liquid water today ([Castillo-Rogez et al. 2023](literatur:castillo-rogez-2023)). An independent
heat budget agrees: unlike Ariel and Umbriel, radiogenic heating alone suffices for Titania and
Oberon to keep enough heat for a long-lived ocean over billions of years
([Bierson and Nimmo 2022](literatur:bierson-2022)).

A direct test would be measuring the secondary field induced by Uranus's time-varying magnetic
field during a flyby: the driving field, oscillating at the synodic period, is at Oberon about
4 nT, the weakest of the five – against about 300 nT at nearby Miranda –, since field strength
decreases with distance; a sufficiently conductive ocean ($>2\,\mathrm{S\,m^{-1}}$, 3 to 40 km
thick, even under up to 50 km of ice) would still yield a signal above the roughly 1 nT
sensitivity typical of today's magnetometers ([Weiss et al. 2021](literatur:weiss-2021)). A newer
approach looks for the same signal in libration and precession with non-vanishing obliquity,
computed for all five moons ([Baland et al. 2025](literatur:baland-2025)). Unlike Titania's
Messina Chasmata or Ariel's graben system, Oberon's known surface so far offers no tectonic
structure from which heat fluxes could be derived (see Surface).

## Surface

By crater counting densities, Oberon's surface is considered among the oldest in the Uranian
system, along with Miranda, Umbriel, and Titania, unlike the markedly younger Ariel
([Kirchoff et al. 2022](literatur:kirchoff-2022)); an earlier analysis of the same Voyager images,
based only on Umbriel, Titania, and Oberon, reached the same conclusion
([Plescia 1987](literatur:plescia-1987)). The largest known crater, Hamlet, just under 206 km
across, carries on part of its floor an extensive, very dark deposit that may be icy, carbon-rich
material and probably welled up from depth only after the impact; its exact composition and
origin remain unknown ([Plescia 1987](literatur:plescia-1987)). A single graben, Mommur
Chasma, about 537 km long and the longest known fracture on the moon, shows that Oberon's crust
once stretched too – more isolated than Titania's Messina Chasmata or Ariel's graben system; a
finer network comparable to Titania's is considered possible but not established
([Schenk and Moore 2020](literatur:schenk-2020)). On the lower limb of a Voyager 2 image, at
23° S, 140° E, an isolated massif about 45 km wide also rises; an early estimate shortly after
the flyby put its height at about 6 km, while a limb survey published as part of a study of large
impact features on the mid-sized icy moons of Saturn and Uranus instead found 11 km – possibly
the central peak of a heavily viscously relaxed, roughly 375 km impact basin otherwise visible
only as an indistinct semicircle of dark patches ([Moore et al. 2004](literatur:moore-2004)).

Spectroscopically, Oberon is the only one of the four outer classical moons with no detectable CO2
ice, while band strength at Ariel, Umbriel, and Titania decreases with distance from Uranus; the
water-ice pattern seen at the three inner classical moons also reverses – their deeper H2O bands
lie on the leading hemisphere, whereas at Oberon the same study finds them on the trailing side
([Grundy et al. 2006](literatur:grundy-2006)) –, a reversal whose cause is open. Oberon is also
considered the reddest of the five: colour asymmetry between the leading and trailing hemispheres
grows with distance from Uranus, as does overall surface reddening; bright, fresh impact features,
by contrast, appear distinctly bluish, as already at Miranda and Titania
([Buratti and Mosher 1991](literatur:buratti-1991)). For the red material on the leading
hemisphere of Titania and Oberon, complex organics and amorphous pyroxene fit the spectral slopes
best, within the hypothesis that dust from Uranus's outer irregular moons preferentially strikes
there ([Cartwright et al. 2018](literatur:cartwright-2018)) – a hypothesis, not a proven fact.

## Atmosphere and magnetosphere

There is no direct evidence for an atmosphere at Oberon; unlike Titania, no usable stellar
occultation exists (see Parameters and measurement). As for the other four large Uranian moons,
mass and surface temperature are insufficient anyway to retain even CO2 as a gas: the escape
velocity is only about $0.73\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ (derived from $GM$ and radius above).

With a mean orbital radius of 583,550 km, equivalent to 22.8 Uranus radii, Oberon orbits closer to
the edge of Uranus's magnetosphere than any of the other four large moons. A 2024 reanalysis of
the Voyager 2 data finds a mean, multi-model solar-wind-averaged magnetopause distance of
22.2 Uranus radii (about 567,000 km) and, for the unusually compressed state measured during the
single 1986 flyby, only 17.3 Uranus radii (about 442,000 km – a state the solar wind produces only
about 4% of the time); from the full distribution of this distance, the same study puts the
probability that Oberon lies outside the magnetosphere at a given time at under 13% – higher than
for Titania (under 4%), but markedly lower than a bare comparison of orbital radius with the mean
alone would suggest ([Jasinski et al. 2024](literatur:jasinski-2024)). Oberon thus spends most of
its time inside the magnetosphere but, unlike the three inner large moons, repeatedly exits it at
least temporarily.

## Orbit, rotation and dynamics

Oberon's osculating orbit, with $i = 179.812^\circ$, lies only $0.188^\circ$ from the degeneracy at
$180^\circ$ – the largest distance among the four outer large moons after Umbriel (0.056°) and
Titania (0.101°), though far below Miranda's 4.34°; the NSSDC fact sheet instead gives an orbital
inclination of only 0.07°, an unresolved discrepancy of 0.12° – the largest among the five moons
(Miranda 0.09°, Umbriel 0.07°, Titania 0.02°) (convention as under
[Orbital elements](thema:bahnelemente)). Seen from Earth, Oberon's orbit and those of the other
four moons therefore appear almost as a straight line around Uranus; the scene
[Uranus lying on its side](szene:uranus-gekippt) shows how tilted the system appears. Like all 21
moons covered by the model, Oberon rotates synchronously, with the smallest residual deviation of
the five large Uranian moons ($0.006 \cdot 10^{-6}$ relative between rotation and orbital period);
the direction to Uranus therefore stays practically unchanged at 102.6° over a century – Oberon's
libration is the smallest and most damped of the five (numbers as under
[Tidal locking](thema:gebundene-rotation)).

At 13.46 days, Oberon has by far the longest orbital period of the five large moons and is not
currently in a mean-motion resonance; unlike Miranda, Ariel, and Umbriel, its orbit was also never
involved in a resonance crossing under either of the two competing pictures of the system's
classical orbital dynamics ([Tittemore and Wisdom 1990](literatur:tittemore-1990);
[Ćuk et al. 2020](literatur:cuk-2020); basics under
[Orbital resonances](thema:resonanzen)). A newer, system-wide model nonetheless finds a dynamical
role for Oberon: its rotation-axis precession lies close to a resonance with the precession of
Umbriel's orbital plane, excited during the Ariel–Umbriel 5:3 resonance; this spin–orbit
resonance, together with a subsequent Ariel–Umbriel secular resonance, could help explain the
present, nearly vanishing inclinations of Ariel and Umbriel
([Ćuk et al. 2020](literatur:cuk-2020)) – Oberon's own orbit remains free of resonance, but its
rotation axis still affects its neighbours.

## Formation and evolution

Like the other regular moons of the giant planets, Oberon probably formed in a debris disk around
Uranus, whether after a giant impact that could also explain the planet's extreme axial tilt, or
through orderly co-accretion with the planet itself (numbers and discussion under
[Uranus](objekt:uranus) and [Axial tilt](thema:achsneigung)). As the outermost of the five, Oberon
escaped, under both resonance models, the crossings that heated Ariel and Umbriel (see Orbit,
rotation and dynamics); its size and rock fraction, comparable to Titania's, suggest that
radiogenic heat alone could have sufficed for early interior activity and the single graben Mommur
Chasma, without a resonance ([Bierson and Nimmo 2022](literatur:bierson-2022)). Uranus and its
moon system formed within the broader formation history of the outer Solar System, in which
Neptune also migrated outward (context under
[Formation of the Solar System](thema:entstehung)).

## Open questions

- **Does Oberon have a liquid ocean today?** Interior and heat-budget models rank Oberon with
  Titania as the most promising candidate among the five large Uranian moons
  ([Castillo-Rogez et al. 2023](literatur:castillo-rogez-2023);
  [Bierson and Nimmo 2022](literatur:bierson-2022)); whether an original ocean is still liquid or
  has long since frozen is undecided.
- **Where do the red material and the dark deposit in crater Hamlet come from?** Complex organics
  or amorphous pyroxene from delivered dust of the irregular moons
  ([Cartwright et al. 2018](literatur:cartwright-2018)) and material that welled up from the
  interior, possibly by cryovolcanism ([Plescia 1987](literatur:plescia-1987)), stand as competing
  explanations, with neither established.
- **Northern hemisphere unknown.** Voyager 2 saw only the sunlit southern hemisphere in 1986; no
  close-up images exist of the composition, crater density, or possible further graben systems of
  the northern hemisphere.

## In the model

- **Orbital reference:** Like the other four large Uranian moons, Oberon orbits in the
  `parentEquator` reference on [Uranus's](objekt:uranus) equatorial plane, with osculating Horizons
  elements at epoch J2000 ($a = 583549.94\,\mathrm{km}$, $e = 0.00055$, $i = 179.812^\circ$). The
  resulting distance from the degeneracy at $180^\circ$ (0.188°) departs from the NSSDC value
  (0.07°) – the largest unresolved discrepancy among the five (see Orbit, rotation and dynamics).
- **Node rate:** `nodeDot` is zero – as with the other three larger Uranian moons, a case of
  numerical degeneracy of the node at $i \approx 180^\circ$. Horizons' own osculating node drifts
  evenly: 0.7° per year (numbers as under [Orbital elements](thema:bahnelemente)) against 1.9° per
  year from the nodal period $P_\mathrm{node} = 192.798\,\mathrm{a}$ of the JPL table
  ($360/192.798 = 1.8672^\circ$/year, derivation). Against the fixture `monde-horizons.json`, the
  position with `nodeDot = 0` still stays close: 46 to 1935 km at the five reference dates (at
  most 0.053% of the orbital circumference), well below Ariel's maximum of 2378 km.
- **Apsidal rate:** `lpDot` ($40.2565^\circ$ per century) follows the two-term project formula
  $(360/P_\mathrm{apsis} - 360/P_\mathrm{node}) \cdot 100$ with the JPL values
  $P_\mathrm{apsis} = 158.604\,\mathrm{a}$ and $P_\mathrm{node} = 192.798\,\mathrm{a}$:
  $(360/158.604 - 360/192.798) \cdot 100 = 40.2565$ (matches the dataset value exactly).
- **Orbital period:** From `LDot`, $360^\circ/\dot L = 13.4632\,\mathrm{d}$; the data panel shows
  the Kepler period from semi-major axis and both masses, about $13.4675\,\mathrm{d}$, roughly
  0.031% longer. `rotationPeriodH` ($-323.11769\,\mathrm{h}$) matches $360^\circ/\dot L \cdot 24$
  practically exactly – the synchronous rotation from Orbit, rotation and dynamics above.
- **Pole:** $257.43^\circ / -15.10^\circ$ is fixed in the dataset and is – as for Ariel, Umbriel,
  and Titania – a simplification; only Miranda carries a slightly different declination. The axial
  tilt shown in the data panel (`achsneigungDeg`, the angle between this pole and the orbital
  normal from position and velocity at epoch, flipped for a negative rotation period) is
  $0.069^\circ$ (derivation) – the smallest of the five, well below Ariel's $0.140^\circ$,
  Titania's $0.137^\circ$, Umbriel's $0.182^\circ$, and far below Miranda's $4.334^\circ$.
- **Texture:** Oberon carries no texture, but a fallback colour approximating the fact-sheet
  albedo: as for the other four large Uranian moons, the only available USGS/JPL map mosaic
  consists, because of one-sided Voyager illumination, largely of unlit area (`ASSETS.md`); Hamlet
  and Mommur Chasma are not rendered as separate features. As for every body, the material model
  without gloss and fill light reaches only $0.640\,p$ of the full catalogue albedo $p$ (numbers
  as under [Albedo and brightness](thema:photometrie)).
- **Shadows:** Oberon is the reason only four, not all five, large moons are ever available as
  spherical occluders at Uranus (`MAX_OKKLUDER` in `render/shadows.ts`): `waehleOkkluder` sorts
  sibling moons by rendered angular radius, descending, and truncates to `MAX_OKKLUDER`; Oberon's
  angular radius (about 269 arcseconds, derivation) is the smallest of the five – smaller even
  than the far smaller Miranda's (374 arcseconds), because the large distance outweighs the
  larger physical radius (Ariel 625, Umbriel 453, Titania 373 arcseconds). Because `sizeScale`
  scales radius and orbital radius of all Uranian moons alike, no scale changes this ranking;
  Oberon therefore always drops out at Uranus regardless of scale (numbers as under
  [Eclipses](thema:finsternis)).
- **Scale:** Oberon is a satellite (`isSatellite`, `parent` is Uranus, not the Sun); its orbital
  radius scales like its own and Uranus's radius with `sizeScale`, not with the distance
  compression for planetary orbits. Further simplifications: [Limits of the model](thema:modell).
