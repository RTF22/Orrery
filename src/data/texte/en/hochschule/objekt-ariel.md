# Ariel

At 578.90 km in radius, Ariel is the third-largest of Uranus' five large moons, but the brightest
and geologically youngest: an ice world cut by deep rift systems, smooth valley floors, and the
highest fraction of freshly resurfaced terrain among the five. Voyager 2 saw only Ariel's sunlit
southern hemisphere during its single flyby on 24 January 1986
([Smith et al. 1986](literatur:smith-1986)); it has not been observed at close range since, and
more recent insights come from Hubble and the James Webb Space Telescope. This text presents
parameters and measurement, interior, surface, atmosphere and magnetosphere, and orbit, rotation
and formation, and finally describes what Orrery renders of it.

## Parameters and measurement

Ariel's shape is slightly triaxial: $581.1 \times 577.9 \times 577.7\,\mathrm{km}$, determined from
limb coordinates in the Voyager 2 images ([Thomas 1988](literatur:thomas-1988)). The
volume-equivalent spherical radius from these three semi-axes is
$\sqrt[3]{581.1 \cdot 577.9 \cdot 577.7} = 578.90\,\mathrm{km}$ (derivation) – the value the catalog
also carries. As with [Miranda](objekt:miranda), Ariel's mass parameter comes from a single flyby
trajectory plus decades of ground-based astrometry ([Jacobson 2014](literatur:jacobson-2014)).

With the catalog mass ($1.29 \cdot 10^{21}\,\mathrm{kg}$, from the same Voyager-era solution) and
the geometric mean of the three semi-axes, the resulting density is about
$1.59\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ (derivation) – close to the NSSDC fact sheet's $1590\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$;
the density computed from the table's $GM$ and mean radius, $1.54\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, rests on a
slightly different mass solution. That makes Ariel the densest of the five large Uranian moons
after Titania (1660 kg/m³), ahead of Oberon (1560), Umbriel (1460) and Miranda (1200 kg/m³) – a
sign of a substantially higher rock fraction than Miranda's (see Interior). At visual albedo 0.39,
Ariel is the brightest of the five; in the K band (around 2.2 µm), Karkoschka's (2001) Hubble value
of $0.31 \pm 0.025$ is lower, the same photometric signature of water ice seen in the other moons.
Because Voyager 2 saw only the southern hemisphere, all surface values hold for a good half of the
moon; no close-up images of the northern hemisphere exist to this day.

## Interior

In a simple two-component model of water ice ($917\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$) and silicate rock
($3000\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, the same simplified model as for [Miranda](objekt:miranda) and
[Iapetus](objekt:iapetus)), Ariel's density corresponds to a rock mass fraction of about 60 %
(derivation) – substantially more than Miranda's one third, but similar to the two larger, outer
Uranian moons. Whether the body is fully differentiated cannot be settled without its own gravity
field measurement (see [Interior Structure](thema:innerer-aufbau)). Interior models for all five
large Uranian moons conclude that Ariel, Umbriel, Titania and Oberon could host residual oceans,
while Miranda is too small to do so
([Castillo-Rogez et al. 2023](literatur:castillo-rogez-2023)); an independent budget finds that
radiogenic heating alone is insufficient for a long-lived ocean at Ariel and Umbriel, unlike the
larger Titania and Oberon, which could retain enough heat over billions of years
([Bierson and Nimmo 2022](literatur:bierson-2022)). Both works therefore agree that a present-day
ocean at Ariel is less certain than at Titania and Oberon, but cannot be ruled out. A flyby
magnetometer could test this directly: for a sufficiently conductive ocean
($>2\,\mathrm{S\,m^{-1}}$, 0.4 to 6 km thick, even beneath an ice shell up to 50 km thick), models
predict a secondary field induced by Uranus' time-varying magnetic field that exceeds the roughly
1 nT sensitivity typical of spacecraft magnetometers ([Weiss et al. 2021](literatur:weiss-2021)).

Direct evidence of past interior heat comes from the surface: flexure at several rift systems (see
Surface) yields elastic lithosphere thicknesses between 3.8 and 11.4 km and corresponding heat
fluxes of 6 to 92 mW/m², depending on the rift system examined and the assumed ice model; neither
tidal heating nor radiogenic heat from the rocky core alone accounts for the higher end of this
range, leaving the origin of the strongest heating episodes open
([Peterson et al. 2015](literatur:peterson-2015);
[Beddingfield et al. 2022](literatur:beddingfield-2022)). A past orbital resonance is considered
the driver of the overall measured heat (see Orbit, rotation and dynamics,
[Tides and the Roche limit](thema:gezeiten)).

## Surface

Ariel carries the least cratered and thus youngest surface of the five large Uranian moons, marked
by a network of chasmata – wide rift systems, some more than a thousand kilometers long – and the
smooth, apparently subsequently infilled floors between them. Two groups of such rifts, the older
Pixie group (Pixie, Kewpie, Brownie, Kra, Sylph, and one unnamed rift) and the younger Kachina
Chasmata, show the flank uplift used to derive the elastic thicknesses above
([Peterson et al. 2015](literatur:peterson-2015);
[Beddingfield et al. 2022](literatur:beddingfield-2022)). The smooth valley floors point to
cryovolcanism or viscous ice extrusion, with neither interpretation established. Crater counts
confirm this young impression: Ariel may be overall younger than Miranda, with widespread,
geologically recent, internally driven resurfacing ([Kirchoff et al. 2022](literatur:kirchoff-2022)).

Spectroscopically, Ariel carries the most CO2 ice detected anywhere in the Uranian system, with
decreasing amounts outward toward Umbriel and Titania, while Oberon shows none
([Cartwright et al. 2015](literatur:cartwright-2015)). The CO2 is concentrated on the trailing
hemisphere, where it was first detected in the Uranian system in 2002, while the leading hemisphere
shows deeper water-ice absorption bands
([Grundy 2003](literatur:grundy-2003); [Grundy et al. 2006](literatur:grundy-2006)). Near-infrared
spectra additionally show a 2.2 µm band best matched by ammonia hydrates or flash-frozen
ammonia-water solutions, distributed across both hemispheres; because such ammonia-bearing species
are destroyed by irradiation from Uranus' magnetosphere on comparatively short timescales, their
detection points to geologically recent exposure
([Cartwright et al. 2020](literatur:cartwright-2020)). James Webb Space Telescope observations in
2023 found the first carbon monoxide ice detected anywhere in the Uranian system on Ariel, along
with CO2 deposits estimated at 10 mm or more in thickness on the trailing hemisphere, plus a broad
absorption near 4.02 µm that may point to carbonate minerals; the authors consider it possible that
a substantial fraction of these carbon compounds originates from an interior water ocean rather
than forming solely through surface radiation chemistry
([Cartwright et al. 2024](literatur:cartwright-2024)).

## Atmosphere and magnetosphere

Ariel carries no atmosphere; its mass and surface temperature are insufficient to bind CO2
permanently – even in the comparatively cold orbit around Uranus, some twenty times farther from
the Sun than Earth, carbon dioxide readily turns to gas and is lost. The moon lies within
Uranus' magnetosphere, whose charged particles continually irradiate its surface. Two positions
compete to explain the origin of the CO2: one holds that it forms through radiation chemistry from
water ice and carbon trapped within it, driven by magnetospheric particles
([Cartwright et al. 2015](literatur:cartwright-2015)); the other, supported by the JWST findings,
holds that at least part of it originates in the interior and is subsequently deposited at or
chemically altered on the surface ([Cartwright et al. 2024](literatur:cartwright-2024)). The two
mechanisms are not necessarily mutually exclusive.

## Orbit, rotation and dynamics

Ariel orbits only 0.003° from the degeneracy at $i = 180^\circ$, lying practically exactly in Uranus'
(tilted) equatorial plane – markedly closer to that plane than Miranda's 4.34°
(see [Miranda](objekt:miranda)). Like all 21 moons captured in the model, Ariel rotates
synchronously, with a residual deviation between rotation and orbital period of only
$0.066 \cdot 10^{-6}$ relative; the direction toward Uranus therefore drifts only from 46.7° at the
epoch to 46.6° a century later – practically no measurable drift (figures as in
[Synchronous Rotation](thema:gebundene-rotation)).

Ariel no longer sits in a mean-motion resonance with any neighboring moon today, but the classical
picture of the system's dynamics places Ariel itself as a resonance partner twice: first in a 5:3
resonance with Miranda, then in a 2:1 resonance with [Umbriel](objekt:umbriel), during which the
eccentricities of both participating moons could fluctuate considerably through the chaotic passage
([Tittemore and Wisdom 1990](literatur:tittemore-1990); basic concepts under
[Orbital Resonances](thema:resonanzen)). A newer modeling effort spanning the whole system instead
places the youngest and most consequential resonance of the system first: the 5:3 passage between
Ariel and Umbriel themselves, during which the inclinations of all five moons were raised, before
subsequent secular resonances restored Ariel's and Umbriel's present, nearly vanishing inclinations
([Ćuk et al. 2020](literatur:cuk-2020)) – which of the two pictures better matches the system's
actual history remains undecided (see Open questions). Under either reading, tidal friction during
the resonance passages supplies the heating now visible in Ariel's young surface
([Tides and the Roche limit](thema:gezeiten)).

## Formation and evolution

Like the other regular moons of the giant planets, Ariel most likely formed in a debris disk around
Uranus, whether after a giant impact that could also explain the planet's extreme axial tilt, or
through orderly co-accretion with the planet itself (figures and discussion under
[Uranus](objekt:uranus) and [Axial Tilt](thema:achsneigung)). Resonance passages – such as the
Miranda-Ariel and Ariel-Umbriel passages described above – are considered the central driver of the
moon system's further evolution, heating orbits, raising inclinations, and releasing the moons into
their present, no-longer-resonant configuration ([Ćuk et al. 2020](literatur:cuk-2020)). The
strength of the tidal friction within Uranus required for this bounds its dissipation factor $Q$;
different approaches arrive at values between roughly 15,000 and 39,000
([Tittemore and Wisdom 1989](literatur:tittemore-1989); [Ćuk et al. 2020](literatur:cuk-2020)) – a
hint that the Uranian system as a whole could be younger than the planet's own age would suggest.
Uranus and its moon system formed within the broader formation history of the outer solar system,
in which Neptune also migrated outward (context under
[Formation of the Solar System](thema:entstehung)).

## Open questions

- **Does Ariel host a present-day ocean?** Interior models consider a residual ocean possible but
  less certain than at Titania and Oberon
  ([Castillo-Rogez et al. 2023](literatur:castillo-rogez-2023);
  [Bierson and Nimmo 2022](literatur:bierson-2022)); a magnetic-field measurement during a future
  flyby is considered the most promising direct test
  ([Weiss et al. 2021](literatur:weiss-2021)).
- **Where does the CO2 come from?** Radiation chemistry acting on water ice and trapped carbon
  ([Cartwright et al. 2015](literatur:cartwright-2015)) and an at least partly interior origin with
  subsequent deposition ([Cartwright et al. 2024](literatur:cartwright-2024)) stand as competing
  explanations; whether carbonate minerals are actually present is not yet firmly established from
  the single JWST observation so far.
- **Which resonance explains the system's orbital history?** The classical sequence of
  Miranda-Umbriel, Miranda-Ariel and Ariel-Umbriel passages
  ([Tittemore and Wisdom 1990](literatur:tittemore-1990)) and the newer assignment of the decisive
  role to the youngest Ariel-Umbriel 5:3 resonance
  ([Ćuk et al. 2020](literatur:cuk-2020)) disagree in cause, not merely in modeling detail.
- **How old is the most recent resurfacing?** The elastic thicknesses derived from the rift flanks
  point to heating episodes that differ in time and location, without allowing a single, precise
  age for the most recent resurfacing to be derived
  ([Peterson et al. 2015](literatur:peterson-2015);
  [Beddingfield et al. 2022](literatur:beddingfield-2022)).
- **Northern hemisphere unknown.** Voyager 2 saw only the sunlit southern hemisphere in 1986; no
  close-up images exist of the composition, crater density, or possible further chasmata of the
  northern hemisphere.

## In the model

- **Orbital reference:** Like the other four large Uranian moons, Ariel orbits with reference frame
  `parentEquator` on [Uranus'](objekt:uranus) equatorial plane, using osculating Horizons elements
  at epoch J2000 ($a = 190941.35\,\mathrm{km}$, $e = 0.00152$). The dataset's inclination
  ($i = 179.997^\circ$) lies only $0.003^\circ$ from the degeneracy at $180^\circ$ – Ariel's orbit in the model is
  practically coincident with Uranus' equatorial plane (convention as under
  [Orbital Elements](thema:bahnelemente)).
- **Node rate:** `nodeDot` is zero. Unlike Miranda, this is not an empirical choice fitted against
  a fixture here, but follows directly from the source itself: the JPL table of mean orbital
  elements lists Ariel's nodal period as $0.000\,\mathrm{a}$, explicitly undefined. Horizons' own
  osculating node also wanders so irregularly over the decades that it deviates from a fitted line
  by a root-mean-square of 40° (figures as in Orbital Elements) – a case of numerical degeneracy at
  $i \approx 180^\circ$, as with the other three larger Uranian moons.
- **Apsidal rate:** `lpDot` ($1245.6316^\circ$ per century) follows from the two-term project formula
  $(360/P_\mathrm{apsis} - 360/P_\mathrm{node}) \cdot 100$, which reduces to its first term because
  $P_\mathrm{node} = 0$: $360/28.901 \cdot 100 = 1245.6316$ (the derivation matches the dataset value
  exactly) – the same JPL table column that also sets `nodeDot` to zero, but here for a different,
  independently tabulated quantity.
- **Orbital period:** From `LDot`, $360^\circ/\dot L = 2.520379\,\mathrm{d}$; the data panel shows the
  Kepler orbital period computed from semi-major axis and masses, about $2.52071\,\mathrm{d}$, some
  0.013 % longer. `rotationPeriodH` ($-60.48910\,\mathrm{h}$) matches $360^\circ/\dot L \cdot 24$
  practically exactly – the synchronous rotation from Orbit, rotation and dynamics above.
- **Pole:** $257.43^\circ / -15.10^\circ$ is fixed in the dataset and – as a simplification – is
  the same value used for Umbriel, Titania and Oberon; only Miranda carries a slightly different
  declination of $-15.08^\circ$. The data panel's axial tilt (`achsneigungDeg`, the angle between
  this pole and the orbital normal formed from position and velocity at the epoch, flipped for a
  negative rotation period) is $0.140^\circ$ (derivation) – Ariel's own rotation pole thus lies
  almost exactly on its orbital normal, much closer than Miranda's $4.334^\circ$
  (see [Miranda](objekt:miranda)).
- **Texture:** Ariel carries no texture, but a fallback color approximating the fact-sheet albedo:
  as with the other four large Uranian moons, the only available USGS/JPL map mosaic consists to a
  substantial extent of unlit area because of Voyager's one-sided illumination (`ASSETS.md`).
- **Shadows:** Ariel is among the up to four simultaneously drawn sphere occluders
  (`MAX_OKKLUDER`) when it has the largest rendered angular radius among the Uranian moons; among
  the five large moons, Oberon, as the smallest, always drops out (figures as in
  [Eclipses](thema:finsternis)).
- **Scale:** Ariel is a satellite (`isSatellite`, its `parent` is Uranus, not the Sun); its orbital
  radius therefore scales with `sizeScale` like its own and Uranus' radius, not with the distance
  compression applied to planetary orbits. Further simplifications:
  [Limits of the model](thema:modell).
