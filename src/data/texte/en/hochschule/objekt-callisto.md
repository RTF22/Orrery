# Callisto

Callisto is the outermost of the four Galilean moons and, at 2410.3 km mean radius, almost as large as
the planet [Mercury](objekt:mercury) (4820.6 against 4879.4 km in diameter, 98.8%), at only about a
third of its mass. Unlike [Io](objekt:io), [Europa](objekt:europa) and
[Ganymede](objekt:ganymede), it orbits outside the Laplace resonance and therefore carries no forced
eccentricity and hardly any tidal heating; its surface is the most densely cratered in the Solar
System, and its interior is, by present understanding, only partly separated into rock and ice. An
induced magnetic field nevertheless points to a salty, liquid layer beneath the old crust. This text
presents the gravity field and interior, the surface, the atmosphere and magnetosphere, and the orbit,
rotation and formation, and describes at the end what Orrery models of all this.

## Parameters and measurement

Today's picture of Callisto's interior comes, as for [Io](objekt:io) and [Europa](objekt:europa), from
radio-Doppler data of several flybys of the Galileo spacecraft. Anderson et al. derived from these the
shape, mean radius and gravity field and, assuming hydrostatic equilibrium, determined a moment-of-inertia
factor of $C/(Ma^2) = 0.3549 \pm 0.0042$ — much closer to 0.4 (a homogeneous sphere) than for Io or
Ganymede, but still noticeably below it: any rocky core would be at most 600 km in radius, at a density
of 3.1 to 3.6 g/cm³ ([Anderson et al. 2001a](literatur:anderson-2001a)). Whether this hydrostatic
assumption actually holds for Callisto is open (see below).

The derived mean density, about 1834 kg/m³, agrees with the independently listed fact-sheet value of
1830 kg/m³ ([Jovian Satellite Fact Sheet](quelle:nssdc-jupitermonde)) and lies between the density of
water ice and of rock — consistent with a mixture of the two that has only partly separated, rather
than the fully layered structure found for [Ganymede](objekt:ganymede)
([Interior structure](thema:innerer-aufbau)).

## Interior

Anderson et al. interpreted the measured moment-of-inertia factor as the signature of a body in which
ice and rock have separated only partly, not completely: a mixture whose rock fraction increases with
depth, rather than a sharply layered core-mantle structure like Ganymede's
([Anderson et al. 2001a](literatur:anderson-2001a)). The Radau-Darwin approximation, by which this value
follows from shape and gravity field, assumes hydrostatic equilibrium; for a slowly rotating body like
Callisto in particular (orbital and rotation period 16.7 days), this assumption alone can carry an
error of up to 10% in the derived moment-of-inertia factor, as a comparison of the approximation with
exact, non-hydrostatic models for [Titan](objekt:titan), Callisto and Enceladus shows — Titan, too, is
considered only partially differentiated by its own, similarly high moment-of-inertia factor
([Interior structure](thema:innerer-aufbau)); non-hydrostatic contributions could be large enough to
reconcile the measured gravity field with complete differentiation of Callisto, even though the
observed gravity anomalies rather favour a smaller correction
([Gao and Stevenson 2013](literatur:gao-2013)). How differentiated Callisto really is therefore remains
open (see below).

Independently of the gravity field, the Galileo spacecraft's magnetometer provides a second finding:
while flying through Jupiter's background field, which changes periodically with Jupiter's roughly
ten-hour rotation and Callisto's orbital position, it measured a time-varying, induced field — strong
evidence for a well-conducting layer near the surface, such as a salty ocean beneath the ice crust
would provide ([Khurana et al. 1998](literatur:khurana-1998)). A more detailed modelling of the same
measurements confirmed the induced character of the field at Europa and Callisto together and
constrained the depth and conductivity of the presumed oceans
([Zimmer et al. 2000](literatur:zimmer-2000)). That a barely heated, only partially differentiated body
should carry an ocean that is still liquid today is not easily reconciled with simple thermal evolution
models: a model that reproduces Callisto's slow, multi-billion-year differentiation from accretional
heat, radioactive decay and solid-state convection in the ice does find a path to a partially
differentiated end state, but whether enough heat remains near the surface to keep water liquid to this
day depends sensitively on the assumed initial conditions
([Nagel et al. 2004](literatur:nagel-2004)). The existence and depth of the ocean therefore also remain
open (see below).

## Surface

"Callisto is the most heavily cratered object in our solar system" — Callisto's old, barely renewed
crust has accumulated more impacts than any other known surface
([Jupiter moons at NASA](quelle:nasa-jupitermonde)). By far the largest basin is Valhalla: a structure
officially listed as a "Large ringed feature", made up of concentric fracture rings around a brighter
centre at 14.70° latitude and 56.00° longitude, with 3000 km in diameter as its officially mapped
extent ([USGS Astrogeology Science Center 2026a](literatur:usgs-gazetteer-2026a)); individual
rings can still be traced considerably further out in images. Such multi-ring basins form when an
impact into a thin, brittle crust over a more yielding layer triggers concentric fractures that
propagate far beyond the crater itself.

Because Callisto releases hardly any internal heat, it lacks the drive for volcanism or tectonic crustal
movement that shape [Europa](objekt:europa) or [Ganymede](objekt:ganymede); small craters are not lost
to flooding but slowly through sublimation of their icy walls, which grow shallower and lose their
rims while dark, non-volatile material remains behind on the floor. Together with carbon dioxide and
organic traces in the regolith, these eroded craters and extensive dark deposits point to a surface
depleted in volatiles and increasingly enriched in non-icy material over time (see Atmosphere and
magnetosphere).

## Atmosphere and magnetosphere

Callisto carries an extremely thin atmosphere consisting mainly of carbon dioxide: spectra from the
Galileo spacecraft showed an absorption band consistent with a surface pressure of about
$7.5 \cdot 10^{-12}$ bar and a temperature around 150 K ([Carlson 1999](literatur:carlson-1999)).
Because such a thin atmosphere is continually depleted by photodissociation and by bombardment with
charged particles from Jupiter's magnetosphere, a source must keep resupplying it; a more recent model
of this thin, still collisional atmosphere accounts for exactly such loss and resupply processes
([Carberry Mogan et al. 2021](literatur:carberry-mogan-2021)). Observations with the Hubble Space
Telescope also detected an oxygen atmosphere, similar to that of [Ganymede](objekt:ganymede) and
[Europa](objekt:europa) and likewise explained by radiolysis of surface ice: energetic particles and
photons split water ice, and the light hydrogen escapes faster than the heavier oxygen
([Cunningham et al. 2015](literatur:cunningham-2015)).

Callisto has no magnetic field of its own generated by a dynamo; the measured field is, as at Europa, an
induced one (see above). Callisto lies outside Jupiter's strongest radiation belts and thus in a
markedly weaker radiation environment than the three inner Galilean moons — a circumstance that has
long made it a candidate site for a base for future crewed missions into the Jupiter system (see
below).

## Orbit, rotation and dynamics

With an orbital period of 16.689017 days, Callisto lies well outside the Laplace chain: the ratio to
Ganymede's orbital period (7.154553 days) is 2.33, not the 2.00 of a 2:1 resonance
([Orbital resonances](thema:resonanzen)). Without a resonant perturbation, Callisto lacks the forced
eccentricity that keeps up tidal heating at Io and Europa; its small eccentricity of 0.007 is therefore
not the equilibrium value of a continuous drive but largely an undisturbed remnant, and the role that
[tides](thema:gezeiten) play for the three inner moons is essentially absent for Callisto. The scene
[Galilean Shadow Play](szene:galileisches-schattenspiel) shows how Io, Europa and Ganymede eclipse and
occult one another; Callisto does not appear in it, because the scene's subject is precisely the three
resonantly coupled moons. Like the other three moons, Callisto rotates
[synchronously](thema:gebundene-rotation): its rotation and orbital periods agree exactly, and Callisto
permanently shows Jupiter the same face.

Because Callisto experiences hardly any tides, an astrometric measurement of its orbital evolution
comparable to those for Io, Europa and Ganymede is not quantified in the works consulted here; this
fits the picture of a moon whose orbit has been little disturbed since its formation. Because of its
lower radiation exposure, the NASA study "Human Outer Planet Exploration" (HOPE) examined Callisto as a
site for a possible later crewed base in the Jupiter system, with boundary conditions such as a launch
around 2045 or later, a transfer time of under five years, and a crew of three for at least 30 days on
the surface; given the distance and the residual radiation, such an undertaking would remain a distant
goal even there ([Troutman et al. 2003](literatur:troutman-2003)).

## Formation and evolution

The Galilean moons probably formed in a "starved" circumjovian gas disc that contained little solid
mass at any one time and was replenished over millions of years by inflowing material; this picture
fits best with the ice fraction that increases from the inside outward across the four moons and with
Callisto's presumably only partially differentiated interior
([Nimmo et al. 2026](literatur:nimmo-2026)). Within this disc, Callisto formed farthest from Jupiter and
so where the disc was coldest, cold enough for a high ice fraction combined with little heating after
formation — one of three explanations for why its interior, unlike that of the only slightly smaller
Ganymede, never fully melted (see below). The broader context of planet formation is presented under
[Formation of the Solar System](thema:entstehung).

**The Ganymede-Callisto dichotomy.** Why Ganymede is fully differentiated — iron in the core, rock in
the mantle, ice in the shell — while the only slightly smaller and similarly composed Callisto, judged
by its moment-of-inertia factor, probably remains only partially differentiated, is one of the major
open questions about the Galilean moons. Three explanations stand side by side. According to the
first, only the later capture into the Laplace resonance released enough tidal heat to melt Ganymede
completely, while Callisto, orbiting outside the resonance, stayed cold
([Showman and Malhotra 1997](literatur:showman-1997)). According to the second, the Late Heavy
Bombardment delivered different amounts of impact heat to the two moons: model calculations show that
the impact energy of that time sufficed to push Ganymede past the threshold for complete
differentiation, but not Callisto ([Barr and Canup 2010](literatur:barr-2010)). According to the
third, no additional event is needed at all: an accretion model that assumes a slow build-up lasting
more than two million years for both moons finds that differing disc temperatures and final radii at
their respective formation sites explain why Callisto can remain unmelted despite an impactor
contribution of up to 30% of its accreted mass, while Ganymede could still fully differentiate
([Bennacer et al. 2025](literatur:bennacer-2025)). Which of these explanations is correct — or whether
several acted together — is not decided.

Since April 2023 the European spacecraft JUICE has been under way to Jupiter; after several gravity
assists at Earth and Venus, including one in September 2026, it is planned to enter Jupiter orbit in
July 2031 and to characterise all three icy moons — Europa, Callisto and Ganymede — during flybys,
before finally entering orbit around Ganymede itself
([Grasset et al. 2013](literatur:grasset-2013); [Juice (ESA)](quelle:esa-juice)).

## Open questions

- **Degree of differentiation:** shape and gravity field give, under the assumption of hydrostatic
  equilibrium, a moment-of-inertia factor of 0.3549 ± 0.0042, consistent with a body that has separated
  only partly ([Anderson et al. 2001a](literatur:anderson-2001a)); for a body rotating as slowly as
  Callisto in particular, non-hydrostatic contributions could distort this value by up to 10%, so that
  the gravity field would also be consistent with more complete differentiation, even though the
  measured gravity anomalies rather favour a smaller correction
  ([Gao and Stevenson 2013](literatur:gao-2013)).
- **Existence and depth of the ocean:** the induced magnetic field from the Galileo flybys argues for a
  well-conducting, salty layer beneath the crust
  ([Khurana et al. 1998](literatur:khurana-1998); [Zimmer et al. 2000](literatur:zimmer-2000)); how a
  barely heated, only partially differentiated body can sustain an ocean that is still liquid today is,
  by contrast, not easily reconciled with simple thermal evolution models
  ([Nagel et al. 2004](literatur:nagel-2004)).
- **Cause of the Ganymede-Callisto dichotomy:** resonance passage
  ([Showman and Malhotra 1997](literatur:showman-1997)), impacts during the Late Heavy Bombardment
  ([Barr and Canup 2010](literatur:barr-2010)), and differing disc temperatures and final radii under
  an otherwise similar, slow accretion history ([Bennacer et al. 2025](literatur:bennacer-2025)) stand
  side by side as explanations (see above).

## In the model

- **Orbital reference:** like the other Jupiter moons, Callisto moves in the `parentEquator` reference
  on Jupiter's equatorial plane (pole 268.057°/64.495° from [Jupiter](objekt:jupiter)), with
  $i = 0.3^\circ$, $e = 0.007$ (`eDot` 0) and the angles $L = 80.3^\circ$, $\varpi = 352.9^\circ$,
  $\Omega = 309.1^\circ$ at the epoch. A check against `positionInParentFrame` at the epoch shows that
  the node value noticeably determines the position: with a node of 0° substituted, the position shifts
  by a total of about 2413 km, of which −2413 km is in $z$, but only −15 km in $x$ and 13 km in $y$.
- **Nodal period:** from `nodeDot` ($-62.3631^\circ$ per century) follows a period of
  $360/62.3631 \times 100 = 577.264$ years, regressing — the direction expected for the nodal
  regression of a satellite under the oblateness of its parent body
  ([Murray and Dermott 2000](literatur:murray-2000)).
- **Perijove period:** from $\dot\varpi$ ($67.1701^\circ$ per century) follows a period of
  $360/\dot\varpi \times 100 = 535.953$ years, prograde. Unlike for Io and Europa
  ([Orbital resonances](thema:resonanzen)), this is not a departure from theory here: because
  $\dot\varpi$ carries both nodal and apsidal precession together, and pure apsidal precession for a
  satellite below the critical inclination runs prograde under its parent body's oblateness
  ([Murray and Dermott 2000](literatur:murray-2000)), a regressing nodal rate (−62.3631°/century) and a
  still stronger prograde apsidal rate (+129.52°/century, from the element table's separately listed
  "periapsis precession period" of 277.921 years) add up here to a prograde remainder, exactly as theory
  predicts for a moon without resonant forcing.
- **Orbital period of the data set:** from $\dot L$ (787,883.432559° per century) follows
  $36525/(\dot L/360) = 16.689017$ days — exactly the value with which Callisto actually orbits in the
  model, and which the fact sheet also gives. Unlike for Io and Europa, the table's own column P is not
  flagged as erroneous for Callisto (header comment in `jupiter-monde.ts`); an independent Kepler
  calculation with Jupiter's `GM` and Callisto's mass (`umlaufzeitTage` from `sim/orbit.ts`) gave
  16.690128 days, only 0.0067% (about 96 s) above the model value — at the one decimal place the data
  panel shows, both values read "16.7 days".
- **Rotation ratio:** `rotationPeriodH` (400.53643 h) matches the sidereal period computed from
  $\dot L$ (400.536408 h) to $0.055 \cdot 10^{-6}$ relative, and the angle between the map centre and
  the direction to Jupiter is 101.2° at the epoch and 102.5° after a hundred years (figures as in
  [Tidal locking](thema:gebundene-rotation)).
- **Pole and Laplace plane:** the stored pole (268.72°/64.83°, IAU rotation elements/SPICE kernel) is
  fixed at the epoch and deviates from Jupiter's own pole by 0.439° — more than for the three inner
  moons. The element table additionally lists a Laplace pole of its own for Callisto (268.7°/64.8°)
  with a "tilt" of 0.4° against Jupiter's equatorial plane; this is a real property of Callisto's orbital
  plane, not a modelling quantity. The model error that follows because Orrery uses Jupiter's equatorial
  plane uniformly instead of this Laplace plane of Callisto's own is the deviation between Jupiter's
  stored pole and this separately listed Laplace pole: computed at 0.411°, the largest among the four
  moons (Io and Europa each 0.019°, Ganymede 0.122°); the orbital plane on which Callisto moves in the
  model is thus misoriented by this amount.
- **Obliquity:** the check with `achsneigungDeg` gives $0.736^\circ$ — more than the pure pole
  difference to Jupiter (0.439°) or the orbital inclination $i = 0.3^\circ$ alone, because at the epoch
  the two effects do not oppose but partly add to one another. The data panel rounds to one decimal
  place and therefore shows 0.7°.
- **Shadows and occluder selection:** Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto are simultaneously occluders of
  Jupiter and of one another (`MAX_OKKLUDER` 4 in `render/shadows.ts`); `waehleOkkluder` sorts the
  sibling moons by displayed angular radius in descending order and only then truncates to
  `MAX_OKKLUDER`. Seen from Jupiter, Callisto has the smallest angular radius of the four, about 0.073°
  (Io 0.247°, Ganymede 0.141°, Europa 0.133°) — were a fifth candidate ever added, Callisto would be
  the first to be dropped; but with exactly four Galilean moons in the catalogue and `MAX_OKKLUDER` at
  4, this truncation never actually applies, and Callisto always counts among the four occluders. A
  moon at distance $r$ casts its shadow onto a sphere of radius $R$ only if the Sun's elevation above
  the orbital plane stays below $\arcsin(R/r)$; with Jupiter's radius of 69,911 km ($R$) and Callisto's
  orbital radius of 1,882,700 km ($r$) this gives $\arcsin(69911/1882700) = 2.128^\circ$, rounding to
  the 2.1° from [Eclipses](thema:finsternis). Because Jupiter's obliquity, at 3.1200° (computed) or
  3.13° (fact sheet), exceeds this, Callisto — unlike the three inner moons — casts a shadow on Jupiter
  only some of the time: whenever the Sun is closer than 2.1° to Jupiter's equatorial plane, that is,
  near the equinoxes of its not quite twelve-year orbit around the Sun. As with Jupiter's own shadow,
  this one, too, carries no umbra colour.
- **Albedo and texture:** the albedo of 0.19 is the fact sheet's geometric albedo, matched exactly. The
  texture is a USGS global mosaic (`ASSETS.md`) and is normalised to the catalogue albedo like every
  body's ([Albedo and brightness](thema:photometrie)); when fitted to the target resolution its height
  was stretched by 2.8%, a small latitude error on this simple-cylindrical map. Valhalla and the other
  impact structures are not drawn separately in it; `appearance` in `jupiter-monde.ts` carries only an
  albedo texture.
- **Scale:** Callisto is a satellite (`isSatellite`, `parent` is Jupiter, not the Sun); its orbital
  radius around Jupiter therefore scales, like its own and Jupiter's radius, with `sizeScale`, not with
  the distance compression of the planetary orbits (`scaledPositionAt`).
- **Data panel:** the mass carried in the data set ($1.0759 \cdot 10^{23}\,\mathrm{kg}$), multiplied by
  $G$ from CODATA 2018, gives a $GM$ of $7180.879\,\mathrm{km^3\,s^{-2}}$, about 222 ppm above the value
  measured by orbit tracking in the table above. Further simplifications:
  [Limits of the model](thema:modell).
