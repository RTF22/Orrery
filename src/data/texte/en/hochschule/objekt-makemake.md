# Makemake

Makemake is the second-brightest known body beyond Neptune, after Pluto, and the largest
known classical Kuiper belt object; as with [Eris](objekt:eris) and
[Haumea](objekt:haumea), almost everything known about it comes from stellar occultations,
light and thermal curves, spectroscopy and, since 2016, the orbit of a single known moon,
not from a flyby. This text presents parameters, surface, atmosphere, orbit and formation,
and closes with what Orrery models of all this, in particular the origin of the mass and
the stand-in axis that replaces the missing measured pole direction.

## Parameters and measurement

A stellar occultation on 23 April 2011 gave Makemake projected axes of
$1430 \pm 9\,\mathrm{km}$ and $1502 \pm 45\,\mathrm{km}$ — a noticeably elongated outline —
along with a geometric albedo of $0.77 \pm 0.03$ and, from an assumed mass derived via a
volatile-retention model, a density of $1.7 \pm 0.3\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$
([Ortiz et al. 2012](literatur:ortiz-2012)). A later, purely photometric reanalysis of the
same occultation data shows, however, that Makemake's very small light-curve amplitude (see
below) is only compatible with a nearly pole-on viewing geometry; under that assumption, an
equatorial diameter of $1434 \pm 14\,\mathrm{km}$ and a polar diameter of
$1422 \pm 14\,\mathrm{km}$ follow — a body close to spherical rather than the previously
assumed ellipsoid — together with a higher albedo of $0.81^{+0.01}_{-0.02}$. The same work
also shows that the previously reported density rested on an invalid application of the
retention model: the occultation data alone provide no meaningful constraint on the density
([Brown 2013](literatur:brown-2013)). The catalog's radius value, $715\,\mathrm{km}$, is the
geometric mean of Brown's nearly spherical axes.

As with [Eris](objekt:eris) and [Haumea](objekt:haumea), the mass follows from the orbit of
a moon: in 2016, the Hubble Space Telescope discovered a small, very dark companion,
S/2015 (136472) 1, informally called "MK2", $7.80 \pm 0.04\,\mathrm{mag}$ fainter than
Makemake ([Parker et al. 2016](literatur:parker-2016)). The discovery data alone did not
constrain a mass; the orbit was bounded only to a period above 12.4 days and a semi-major
axis above roughly 21,000 km. A 2025 preprint, not yet published in a peer-reviewed journal,
based on thirteen Hubble observation epochs between April 2015 and February 2019, gives a
near-circular orbit with period $18.023 \pm 0.017\,\mathrm{d}$, semi-major axis
$22250 \pm 780\,\mathrm{km}$, and an orbital plane tilted by $83.7^\circ \pm 1.0^\circ$
against the line of sight, that is, nearly edge-on. Assuming that essentially the entire
system mass resides in Makemake (size estimates put the moon's contribution below 0.2 %)
and using the radius $715 \pm 15\,\mathrm{km}$ ([Brown 2013](literatur:brown-2013)), this
gives a system mass of $(2.69 \pm 0.20) \times 10^{21}\,\mathrm{kg}$ and a density of
$1.76 \pm 0.17\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ ([Bamberger 2025](literatur:bamberger-2025)) — exactly
the value the dataset carries (origin check, see In the model). An earlier, likewise
unpublished orbit solution using fewer observation epochs had given a somewhat higher
density of about $2.1\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$; both orbit solutions are therefore preliminary,
but mutually consistent. Using the catalog's mass and radius gives the same density by
calculation, $1.757\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ (derivation) — close to, but with no substantive
connection to, Ortiz's older, methodologically superseded value of $1.7\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$.

Three independent determinations — Brown's photometric reanalysis, a thermal model fit to
Herschel data, and a photometric recalibration of the absolute magnitude — agree on a higher
albedo than the original occultation analysis: $0.81$
([Brown 2013](literatur:brown-2013)), a range of $0.7$ to $0.9$ from a two-terrain thermal
model ([Lim et al. 2010](literatur:lim-2010)), and $0.82 \pm 0.02$ in the V filter,
explicitly described as "more similar to Brown (2013) and Lim et al. (2010)"
([Hromakina et al. 2019](literatur:hromakina-2019)). The dataset nonetheless carries Ortiz's
original value of $0.77$ (see In the model).

## Surface

Near-infrared spectra show deep absorption bands on Makemake, shifted relative to pure
methane ice, placing the body among the most methane-ice-rich known objects alongside
Pluto, Eris, Triton and Sedna ([Licandro et al. 2006](literatur:licandro-2006)). A closer
analysis shows the methane occurs in large, roughly centimeter-sized grains — markedly
larger than the roughly $100\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ grains on Pluto — together with ethane as an
irradiation product; the authors attribute the differences from Pluto mainly to a depletion
of nitrogen ice ([Brown, Barkume et al. 2007](literatur:brown-barkume-2007)). James Webb
Space Telescope spectroscopy, as with [Eris](objekt:eris), detected the heavy isotopologues
of methane for the first time and found a D/H ratio of $(2.9 \pm 0.6) \times 10^{-4}$ and a
${}^{13}\mathrm{C}/^{12}\mathrm{C}$ ratio of $0.010 \pm 0.003$ in Makemake's methane ice; the
authors read this, as for Eris, as evidence for internal geochemical activity in a warm or
hot rocky core ([Grundy et al. 2024](literatur:grundy-2024)). A later modeling study shows,
however, that the same elevated D/H ratio can also be explained primordially, without
internal activity ([Mousis et al. 2025](literatur:mousis-2025)); which reading is correct
remains open (see Open questions).

James Webb observations in the mid-infrared also reveal a prominent thermal excess at
$18$–$25\,\mathrm{\mu m}$, indicating temperatures around $150\,\mathrm{K}$ — far warmer
than sunlight alone could produce at Makemake's distance. The authors discuss two
explanations: a small, warm, possibly cryovolcanically active region of about
$350\,\mathrm{km^2}$ (about 1 % of the surface, powered by the warm interior described
above), or an as-yet undetected ring of very small, carbonaceous dust grains at a radius of
about $4300\,\mathrm{km}$, near the 3:1 spin-orbit resonance of the shorter rotation period;
which of the two explanations is correct is undecided
([Kiss et al. 2024](literatur:kiss-2024)). Direct JWST spectroscopy in 2025 detected
fluorescence from gaseous methane over Makemake for the first time — making it, after Pluto,
the second trans-Neptunian object with confirmed volatile release in the gas phase; the D/H
ratio newly measured in the methane ice from the same observation (not in the gas itself),
$(3.98 \pm 0.34) \times 10^{-4}$, agrees within $2\sigma$ with the value found by
[Grundy et al. 2024](literatur:grundy-2024) — the paper does not report a separate D/H
determination for the gaseous methane
([Protopapa et al. 2025](literatur:protopapa-2025)).

## Atmosphere and magnetosphere

The same occultation that gave the radius and albedo rules out a global, Pluto-like
nitrogen atmosphere down to an upper limit of $4$ to $12\,\mathrm{nbar}$ ($1\sigma$)
([Ortiz et al. 2012](literatur:ortiz-2012)) — despite a Pluto-like surface of methane and
nitrogen ice, Makemake thus lacks a comparable, bound gas envelope; part of the explanation
may lie in the depletion of the more readily sublimating nitrogen ice found by Brown,
Barkume et al. (2007), the very ice that sustains Pluto's atmosphere. The 2025 detection of
methane fluorescence is consistent with this: it points either to an expanding, comet-like
coma with a rovibrational temperature around $35\,\mathrm{K}$, or to a thin, gravitationally
bound layer with gas temperatures near $40\,\mathrm{K}$ and a surface pressure of about
$10\,\mathrm{pbar}$ — some three orders of magnitude below the occultation limit and thus
consistent with it ([Protopapa et al. 2025](literatur:protopapa-2025)). Whether this is a
persistent, localized outgassing process or a transient event remains open (see Open
questions).

## Orbit, rotation and dynamics

Makemake's orbit has a semi-major axis of $a = 45.571\,\mathrm{AU}$, an eccentricity of
$e = 0.159$, and an inclination of $i = 29.0^\circ$ to the ecliptic. From this follow a
perihelion distance of $38.33\,\mathrm{AU}$ and an aphelion distance of
$52.81\,\mathrm{AU}$ (derivation); the orbital period is about 307.6 years (checked in
code, consistent with Kepler's third law for the heliocentric semi-major axis). On
17 September 2026, Makemake is about $52.7\,\mathrm{AU}$ from the Sun (checked in code),
near aphelion — consistent with the statement that Makemake currently lies close to its
farthest orbital point ([Parker et al. 2016](literatur:parker-2016)). At
$a = 45.57\,\mathrm{AU}$, Makemake lies between the Kuiper belt's known Neptune resonances
and, like Eris, is locked into none of them; it belongs to the classical,
non-resonant Kuiper belt objects ([Parker et al. 2016](literatur:parker-2016)) and, because
of its inclination being high compared with the "cold" population ($i < 5^\circ$), to the
dynamically "hot" classical subgroup
([Lykawka and Mukai 2007](literatur:lykawka-mukai-2007)).

Makemake's rotation period is not conclusively settled: long-term photometry between 2006
and 2017 shows a very small light-curve amplitude of only $0.032 \pm 0.005\,\mathrm{mag}$ in
the R filter, compatible either with a nearly spherical shape or with a nearly pole-on
viewing geometry; a possible light-curve asymmetry points to a double-peaked solution of
$P = 22.8266 \pm 0.0001\,\mathrm{h}$, while the same data also allow a single-peaked
solution at half that value, $11.4133\,\mathrm{h}$
([Hromakina et al. 2019](literatur:hromakina-2019)). A more recent analysis of light-curve
data from the TESS and Gaia space telescopes confirms the single-peaked 11.4-hour solution;
the double-peaked TESS light curve at $22.8\,\mathrm{h}$ shows no significant asymmetry
between its two half-periods, so the double period cannot be confirmed as the true rotation
period. The authors therefore adopt $P = 11.4\,\mathrm{h}$ as the default value, while still
using $22.8\,\mathrm{h}$ in some calculations — the ambiguity is thus not conclusively
resolved, but the newer data favor the shorter period
([Kiss et al. 2024](literatur:kiss-2024)). The dataset and the JPL Small-Body Database
(drawing on the Light Curve Database, LCDB) still carry the double-peaked value of
$22.8266\,\mathrm{h}$ (see In the model).

The 2025 preprint orbit solution for MK2 described above is nearly edge-on; this opens the
possibility of mutual occultations and eclipses between Makemake and its moon in the near
future ([Bamberger 2025](literatur:bamberger-2025)). If Makemake's spin axis is assumed to
coincide with MK2's orbital plane — an unconfirmed but, given the body's fast rotation and
expected flattening, plausible assumption — a very high obliquity against its own
heliocentric orbit follows, of $46^\circ$ to $78^\circ$, and the present, nearly edge-on
configuration would mean that Makemake is currently viewed nearly equator-on, close to an
equinox ([Parker et al. 2016](literatur:parker-2016)) — the only axis information derived
for Makemake so far, under the same kind of additional assumption as for
[Eris'](objekt:eris) Dysnomia orbit pole (context under
[Axial tilt](thema:achsneigung)).

## Formation and evolution

Makemake was discovered in 2005 and named in 2008 after the creator god of the Rapa Nui
people of Easter Island, as presented in the school-level text (same as school-level text).
Its size and low surface temperature place it among the few Kuiper belt objects — alongside
Pluto, Eris and Sedna — able to retain methane against loss to space over the age of the
Solar System, while smaller or warmer objects have long since lost their volatiles
([Schaller and Brown 2007](literatur:schaller-brown-2007); context under
[Dwarf planets](thema:zwergplaneten)). As a non-resonant member of the dynamically hot
classical population, Makemake, like the other hot classicals, was likely scattered onto its
present, moderately inclined orbit during Neptune's migration, rather than being captured
into a protective resonance like Pluto (context under
[Formation of the Solar System](thema:entstehung)).

## Open questions

- **How fast does Makemake actually rotate?** The light curve is equally compatible with a
  double-peaked solution of $22.8266\,\mathrm{h}$ and a single-peaked one of
  $11.4133\,\mathrm{h}$; newer TESS and Gaia data favor the shorter period but cannot rule
  out the longer one
  ([Hromakina et al. 2019](literatur:hromakina-2019); [Kiss et al. 2024](literatur:kiss-2024)).
- **What are the true mass and density?** The only available determination comes from a 2025
  preprint, not yet peer-reviewed, orbit solution for MK2; an earlier, likewise unpublished
  orbit solution gave a noticeably different density
  ([Bamberger 2025](literatur:bamberger-2025)).
- **Where does Makemake's spin axis point?** The only known value is a tilt range of
  $46^\circ$ to $78^\circ$ against its own orbit, derived under the unconfirmed assumption
  that the spin axis and MK2's orbit pole coincide; an actual right ascension and
  declination are missing ([Parker et al. 2016](literatur:parker-2016)).
- **Does Makemake have an atmosphere?** A global, bound nitrogen atmosphere is excluded down
  to a few nanobar, yet 2025 brought the detection of methane fluorescence; whether a thin,
  gravitationally bound layer or a transient, comet-like coma is present remains open
  ([Ortiz et al. 2012](literatur:ortiz-2012); [Protopapa et al. 2025](literatur:protopapa-2025)).
- **Where do the hydrocarbons come from, and what produces the mid-infrared thermal excess?**
  The elevated D/H ratio in the methane ice can be explained either by internal geochemical
  activity or primordially
  ([Grundy et al. 2024](literatur:grundy-2024); [Mousis et al. 2025](literatur:mousis-2025));
  whether the observed thermal excess comes from a cryovolcanically active region or an
  undetected dust ring is likewise undecided
  ([Kiss et al. 2024](literatur:kiss-2024)).

## In the model

- **Pole as the orbital normal, not a measurement:** The dataset carries Makemake's pole at
  $317.9202^\circ/50.0297^\circ$ — exactly its own heliocentric orbital normal from $i$ and
  the node, independently checked in code and matching the catalog value to four decimal
  places. This stand-in axis contradicts the only derived axis information, the tilt range
  of $46^\circ$ to $78^\circ$ from MK2's orbital plane
  ([Parker et al. 2016](literatur:parker-2016); the same kind of additional assumption as for
  Eris' Dysnomia orbit pole, context under
  [Axial tilt](thema:achsneigung)): the orbital normal itself corresponds to a tilt of
  $0^\circ$, so it can be wrong by as much as $78^\circ$. The axial tilt recomputed in the
  data panel with `achsneigungDeg` accordingly gives only $0.0000384^\circ$ — the panel
  rounds this to one decimal place and shows $0.0^\circ$, far from the derived tilt range.
- **Rotation period, both solutions:** `rotationPeriodH` is set to $22.8266$, the
  double-peaked solution; at $365.25$ days per Earth year, that gives $384.03$ full
  rotations per Earth year (derivation). For the single-peaked solution of
  $11.4133\,\mathrm{h}$, it would be exactly double that, $768.05$ — the dataset opts for
  the longer, explicitly flagged as uncertain, period without taking into account the newer
  analysis of TESS and Gaia data that favors the shorter one
  ([Kiss et al. 2024](literatur:kiss-2024)).
- **`massKg` checked at the source:** The value $2.69 \times 10^{21}\,\mathrm{kg}$ exactly
  matches the system mass reported in the 2025 preprint orbit solution for MK2
  ([Bamberger 2025](literatur:bamberger-2025)) — contrary to what the dataset's terse
  comment might initially suggest, this is not an unsupported figure but a real, if not yet
  peer-reviewed, source.
- **MK2 is missing from the catalog:** Unlike Pluto and Charon, Orrery does not model
  Makemake's moon; the orbit and mass values above are text-only.
- **Albedo and fallback color:** The dataset carries the albedo $0.77$ from the original
  occultation analysis ([Ortiz et al. 2012](literatur:ortiz-2012)), while the fallback color
  used while the texture loads or on failure follows the higher albedo of $0.82$, later
  confirmed by three independent methods
  ([Hromakina et al. 2019](literatur:hromakina-2019)) — visible only while the texture is
  still loading or if it fails to load. Per `ASSETS.md`, the surface texture itself is an
  artistic approximation explicitly labeled "fictional" by Solar System Scope, not a real
  image (checked in code: `ASSETS.md`).
- **Orbit and Kepler solver:** Osculating heliocentric SBDB elements at epoch
  JD 2461200.5 (19 June 2026 TDB), all rates except $\dot L$ zero (as in
  [Orbital elements](thema:bahnelemente)); Makemake's eccentricity of $e = 0.159$ lies far
  below the limiting cases considered there, $e = 0.99$ and $e = 0.999$, so the Kepler
  solver converges correspondingly quickly.
- **Scale:** Makemake is a heliocentric dwarf planet (`parent: 'sun'`) and therefore scales
  like a planet with the distance compression of the display, not like a moon with
  `sizeScale` (`sim/scale.ts`), as [with Eris](objekt:eris). Further simplifications:
  [Limits of the model](thema:modell).
