# Dione

At 561.3 km in radius, Dione is the fourth-largest of Saturn's seven large moons and, at
around 1.48 g cm⁻³, markedly denser than water ice – one of the more rock-rich among them. On
the hemisphere facing away from its orbital motion, bright, net-like streaks cross darker
terrain – the "wispy terrain", which Voyager 1 took for frost deposits in 1980
([Smith et al. 1981](literatur:smith-1981)), but which Cassini showed from 2004 onward to be
ice cliffs hundreds of metres high along tectonic fracture zones
([Stephan et al. 2010](literatur:stephan-2010)). With [Enceladus](objekt:enceladus), Dione is
locked in a 2:1 orbital resonance that permanently sustains that moon's eccentricity and thus
its tidal heating; on its own orbit it is accompanied, as [Tethys](objekt:tethys) is by Telesto
and Calypso, by the small Lagrange moons Helene (L4) and Polydeuces (L5). This text presents
parameters and measurement, interior, surface, atmosphere and magnetosphere, and orbit,
rotation and formation, and finally describes what Orrery renders of it.

## Parameters and measurement

Cassini's radio tracking (ephemeris SAT441) yields Dione's mass parameter directly from the
Doppler shift of the spacecraft's radio signal; combined with the volume derived from Cassini
images, this gives a mean density of $1.4781 \pm 0.0032\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ – a good 50 %
more than pure water ice ($0.917\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$)
([JPL SSD satellite data](quelle:jpl-satelliten)). Dione is slightly triaxial:
$563 \times 561 \times 560\,\mathrm{km}$, from the same photogrammetric control-point network
built from Cassini images of the nominal mission used for the other mid-sized Saturn moons
([Thomas 2010](literatur:thomas-2010)); the volume-equivalent spherical radius from these three
semi-axes is $\sqrt[3]{563 \cdot 561 \cdot 560} = 561.3\,\mathrm{km}$ (worked out from the
code) – the value the catalog also carries, close to the mean radius of 561.40 km measured
directly from radio tracking.

At around $1478\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, Dione is markedly denser than
[Tethys](objekt:tethys) ($984\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$) and carries a significant rock
fraction alongside its water ice: a homogeneous mix of water ice ($917\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$)
and silicate rock (around $3000\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$) would, at the measured density, imply a
rock mass fraction of around 55 % (worked out from the code) – against only a tenth for Tethys
(same calculation as for Tethys); classification of the moment-of-inertia
factor and core formation in moons in general is under
[Interior structure](thema:innerer-aufbau).

## Interior

Unlike [Enceladus](objekt:enceladus), whose gravity field three close Cassini flybys in
2010/2012 measured with a clear hydrostatic result, only three of Dione's five Cassini flybys
yielded usable radio-Doppler data for the degree-2 gravity field:
$J_2 = 1496 \pm 11 \cdot 10^{-6}$ and $C_{22} = 364.8 \pm 1.8 \cdot 10^{-6}$. Their ratio
$J_2/C_{22} = 4.102 \pm 0.044$ departs by about 17 standard deviations from the value $10/3$
that a hydrostatically relaxed body in slow, synchronous rotation should show – so Dione's
moment of inertia cannot be read off the field directly the way Enceladus's can
([Zannoni et al. 2020](literatur:zannoni-2020)). A joint analysis of gravity and topography
instead favours Airy-type isostasy – an outer ice shell floating on a denser, less viscous
layer, such as an ocean would be –, with the best-fitting models tending toward thick ice
shells and thin oceans ([Zannoni et al. 2020](literatur:zannoni-2020)).

Independently, working from shape and topography alone under the assumption of minimum stress
("minimum stress isostasy"), a study of Dione's and Enceladus's ice shells arrives at a similar
picture: an ice shell around 100 km thick over an ocean several tens of kilometres deep,
surrounding a large rocky core ([Beuthe et al. 2016](literatur:beuthe-2016)). The ocean
hypothesis stands on markedly weaker ground for Dione than for Enceladus, though: while
Enceladus's forced libration was measured directly ($0.120^\circ \pm 0.014^\circ$, see
Enceladus), the libration the same model predicts for Dione falls below
what Cassini's control-point network could resolve
([Beuthe et al. 2016](literatur:beuthe-2016)) – an ocean at Dione is plausible but, unlike at
Enceladus, not directly demanded by any single measurement. Whether a liquid layer still
exists today or the interior has since frozen completely remains open (see Open questions);
more on the moment-of-inertia factor for moons in general is under
[Interior structure](thema:innerer-aufbau).

## Surface

Dione's most striking feature is the "wispy terrain" on the hemisphere facing away from its
orbital motion (the trailing hemisphere): bright, net-like streaks over darker ground, which
Voyager 1 took in 1980 for deposits of frost or cryovolcanic ash
([Smith et al. 1981](literatur:smith-1981)). Cassini's December 2004 flyby disproved that: the
streaks are bright ice cliffs, up to several hundred metres high, along tectonic fracture zones
(chasmata), where darker surface material has been stripped away to expose spectrally pure
water ice beneath ([Stephan et al. 2010](literatur:stephan-2010)). A global crater mapping
reveals an at-first-unexpected pattern besides this: the old, densely cratered landscape is
concentrated on the same trailing hemisphere as the tectonically younger "wispy terrain", while
the leading hemisphere mostly shows smoother, less-cratered plains – the reverse of the naive
expectation that a synchronously rotating moon should collect more impacts on its leading side,
which faces into its orbital motion
([Kirchoff and Schenk 2015](literatur:kirchoff-2015)). One possible reading is a large-scale
reorientation of Dione during the era of heavy bombardment, driven by one or more sufficiently
large impacts that spun the moon about its axis before today's bound rotation finally settled
in; the crater map itself dates this early phase without pinning down the exact trigger
([Kirchoff and Schenk 2015](literatur:kirchoff-2015); see Open questions).

## Atmosphere and magnetosphere

Dione carries no permanent atmosphere. During a 2010 flyby, the Cassini Plasma Spectrometer
detected, from the density of $\mathrm{O_2^+}$ pickup ions in the moon's wake, a very thin,
only indirectly inferred oxygen exosphere, with a column density estimated at
$0.9$ to $7 \cdot 10^{11}\,\mathrm{cm^{-2}}$
([Tokar et al. 2012](literatur:tokar-2012)) – unlike at [Rhea](objekt:rhea), where a similar
method additionally found carbon dioxide (details there). The moon lies within
[Saturn's](objekt:saturn) magnetosphere and E ring; unlike Enceladus's, its own contribution to
the system stays purely gravitational.

## Orbit, rotation and dynamics

Dione orbits Saturn in 2.737 days (figures as in
[Saturn's rings edge-on](szene:saturn-ringkante)) at a distance of 6.49 Saturn radii, on a
nearly circular orbit, and rotates in a bound state. On its own orbit it is accompanied by the
far smaller Lagrange moons Helene (L4, 60° ahead) and Polydeuces (L5, 60° behind), which
Orrery's catalog does not render separately – the same pattern shown by
[Tethys](objekt:tethys) with Telesto and Calypso.

With Enceladus, Dione is locked in a 1:2 mean-motion resonance with the resonant angle
$2\lambda_\mathrm{Di} - \lambda_\mathrm{En} - \varpi_\mathrm{En}$, whose libration stays below
$1^\circ$ ([Ćuk et al. 2024](literatur:cuk-2024); derivation under
[Orbital resonances](thema:resonanzen)). Because Dione is about ten times more massive than
Enceladus (worked out from the catalog masses), the same resonance drives mostly Enceladus's
orbit toward a permanent, heat-generating eccentricity, while Dione itself retains only a small
forced eccentricity of around 0.002 (JPL's orbital-element table, averaged over decades) – too
weak to drive noticeable tidal heating within Dione itself.

## Formation and evolution

Like the other mid-sized Saturn moons, Dione probably formed out of the Saturn disc shortly
after the planet itself
([as described for Mimas](objekt:mimas); classification alongside the other origin hypotheses
under [Formation of the Solar System](thema:entstehung)). The crater map shows a surface that
is old over large areas but, especially in the "wispy terrain", considerably younger in
places, pointing to sustained, if weaker, geological activity than at Enceladus
([Kirchoff and Schenk 2015](literatur:kirchoff-2015)). That Dione appears so much less active
than Enceladus despite belonging to the same resonance family fits the mass ratio derived
above: a body ten times more massive needs only a fraction of the eccentricity – and thus of
the tidal heating – to stay in dynamical equilibrium at the same resonance strength.

## Open questions

- **Does a residual liquid ocean layer still exist beneath Dione's ice shell, or has the
  interior since frozen completely?** Gravity and topography favour a thick ice shell over a
  thin, denser layer, without being able to identify the latter securely as a liquid ocean
  ([Zannoni et al. 2020](literatur:zannoni-2020)); the independent shape analysis reaches the
  same picture, but the libration it predicts falls below Cassini's detection threshold
  ([Beuthe et al. 2016](literatur:beuthe-2016)).
- **How old are the ice cliffs of the "wispy terrain", and exactly how did they form?** The
  crater map dates parts of the region markedly younger than the rest of the old surface,
  without pinning down the exact formation mechanism of the fracture zones themselves
  ([Kirchoff and Schenk 2015](literatur:kirchoff-2015); [Stephan et al. 2010](literatur:stephan-2010)).
- **Why is Dione so much less geologically active than Enceladus despite belonging to the same
  resonance family?** The mass ratio offers a plausible order-of-magnitude reason why the
  resonance heats mostly Enceladus (see Orbit, rotation and dynamics); whether, and how
  strongly, Dione was itself more strongly heated in its past than today's forced eccentricity
  suggests remains unanswered by this.

## In the model

- **Orbital reference:** Like the other Saturn moons, Dione moves in the `parentEquator`
  reference on [Saturn's](objekt:saturn) equatorial plane, with osculating Horizons elements at
  epoch J2000 ($a = 377\,652.18\,\mathrm{km}$, $e = 0.00293$) – figures and convention as under
  [Orbital elements](thema:bahnelemente).
- **Fixed node (table special case):** `nodeDot` is set to 0, the same special case as for
  Enceladus (header comment in `saturn-monde.ts`, lines 104–114). At an inclination of only
  $i = 0.029^\circ$, the ascending node loses almost all meaning as a geometric feature – the
  same singularity that Orbital elements describes in general for
  $I \to 0$. The JPL mean-elements table accordingly gives no nodal precession period for
  Dione; Orrery's data set follows that instead of inventing a rate that is barely defined at
  such vanishing inclination anyway. `lpDot` ($3077.4491^\circ$ per century) then directly
  gives the apsidal period $360/3077.4491 \cdot 100 = 11.698$ years (worked out from the
  code) – exactly the value independently given in the header comment.
- **Orbital period:** From `LDot` follows $360^\circ/\dot L = 2.737$ days, adopted as in
  [Saturn's rings edge-on](szene:saturn-ringkante). The data panel instead shows the Kepler
  orbital period computed from the semi-major axis and the masses (`umlaufzeitTage`),
  $2.740$ days, about 0.13 % longer. `rotationPeriodH` ($65.68598\,\mathrm{h}$) matches
  $360^\circ/\dot L$ to $-0.061 \cdot 10^{-6}$ relative; the angle between the map centre and
  the direction to Saturn stands at $130.5^\circ$ at epoch and at $130.0^\circ$ after a hundred
  years (the same figures as under [Bound rotation](thema:gebundene-rotation)).
- **Pole:** $40.66^\circ / 83.52^\circ$ from the IAU rotation elements – identical to the data
  sets for Mimas, Enceladus and Tethys, because the underlying SPICE kernel gives no individual
  precession for any of the four, only a shared pole fixed at epoch
  ([Archinal et al. 2018](literatur:archinal-2018)).
- **Albedo and texture:** The catalog albedo of 0.7 is the fact sheet's geometric albedo; the
  texture is a Cassini mosaic with no separate note of unlit areas (`ASSETS.md`).
- **Shadow:** `waehleOkkluder` picks Saturn's four moons with the largest rendered angular
  radius as spherical occluders: Titan, Tethys, Dione and Rhea (figures as in
  [Eclipses](thema:finsternis), Saturn) – Dione therefore casts a shadow on
  Saturn in the model. Its real shadow window is
  $\arcsin(R_\mathrm{Saturn}/a_\mathrm{Dione}) \approx 8.9^\circ$ (worked out from the code,
  formula as above), well under Saturn's $26.7^\circ$ axial tilt;
  seen from Dione itself, Saturn is always used as the occluder.
- **Helene and Polydeuces are absent:** Neither Lagrange moon is carried in the orbital catalog
  (`saturn-monde.ts`); Orrery renders nothing at Dione's L4 and L5 points.
- **Scale:** Dione is a satellite (`isSatellite`, its `parent` is Saturn, not the Sun); its
  orbital radius therefore scales, like its own and Saturn's radius, with `sizeScale` rather
  than with the distance compression applied to planetary orbits.
- **Data panel:** The mass carried in the data set ($1.10 \cdot 10^{21}\,\mathrm{kg}$), times
  $G$ per CODATA 2018, gives a $GM$ of $73.4173\,\mathrm{km^3\,s^{-2}}$, about 4120 ppm
  (0.41 %) above the value measured by radio tracking in the table above – markedly less than
  for Mimas (1.04 %), more than for Tethys (0.08 %) or Enceladus (−0.03 %). Further
  simplifications: [Limits of the model](thema:modell).
