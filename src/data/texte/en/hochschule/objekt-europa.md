# Europa

Europa is the smallest of the four Galilean moons and, counted from Jupiter, the second innermost:
at a radius of 1560.8 km slightly smaller than Earth's Moon (1737.151 km). Together with
[Io](objekt:io) and [Ganymede](objekt:ganymede) it stands in the Laplace resonance around
[Jupiter](objekt:jupiter); unlike the volcanically active Io, the tidal heating here shows up mainly
as a thin, tectonically active ice shell over a presumed global ocean of salty water. Together with
chemical energy and possible organic compounds, this makes Europa one of the most promising places
to search for life beyond Earth ([Jupiter moons at NASA](quelle:nasa-jupitermonde)). This text
presents the gravity field and interior, the surface, the atmosphere and magnetosphere, and the
orbit, rotation and formation, and finally describes what Orrery renders of it.

## Parameters and measurement

As with Io, today's picture of Europa's interior comes from the same Galileo flybys: from
radio-Doppler data of four encounters, Anderson et al., assuming hydrostatic equilibrium, derived a
model with a metallic core, a rock mantle and an outer water/ice shell. Depending on the assumed
composition the core occupies up to about half the radius, and the outer layer of ice and liquid
water is probably 80 to 170 km thick ([Anderson et al. 1998](literatur:anderson-1998)); this much
dense mass in the core lowers the moment-of-inertia factor below the value 0.4 of a homogeneous
sphere.

## Interior

The gravity field and hydrostatic figure alone only show that Europa is layered; whether the outer
water/ice shell is partly liquid does not follow directly from this
([Anderson et al. 1998](literatur:anderson-1998)). The independent evidence came from the Galileo
magnetometer: during the flyby of 3 January 2000, in an orbital geometry that could distinguish an
induced from a permanent dipole, it measured time-varying field components of the kind expected from
a current-carrying, planet-wide layer beneath the icy surface; the very fact that Europa's field
varies in time strengthens the case for a liquid ocean beneath the present-day surface
([Kivelson et al. 2000](literatur:kivelson-2000)). Such conductivity requires dissolved salts, as
suggested by traces of sodium chloride on the surface (see Surface, below).

The heat that keeps the ocean liquid comes, as with Io, from the orbital eccentricity forced by the
Laplace resonance. Because the heating rate depends on the eccentricity, and the eccentricity itself
is forced by the resonance with Io and Ganymede, Europa's heat budget is tightly coupled to the
orbital dynamics of the three moons: if the strength of the resonance changes over geological
timescales, the heating rate changes with it, and potentially the freezing state of the crust and
ocean; a coupled thermal-orbital model of this feedback for Io and Europa was presented by Hussmann
and Spohn ([Hussmann and Spohn 2004](literatur:hussmann-2004)). How a resonance similarly sustains
tidal heating at [Enceladus](objekt:enceladus) is presented under
[Tides and the Roche limit](thema:gezeiten); more on the moment-of-inertia factor and core formation
in moons in general is under [Interior structure](thema:innerer-aufbau).

## Surface

Europa's surface is among the smoothest in the Solar System: long, often paired cracks (lineae) in
characteristic cycloidal arcs, and chaos terrains where ice blocks appear displaced, tilted and
refrozen, point to a thin, mobile crust over a yielding layer
([Pappalardo et al. 1999](literatur:pappalardo-1999)). The cycloidal shape of many cracks follows
the direction of tidal stress rotating over the course of one orbit, as produced by the eccentric
orbit (diurnal tides): if the stress direction moves faster than a crack propagates, its path
curves — a clock that lets individual cracks be dated relative to one another.

Impact craters are rare on Europa, a strong argument for a geologically young, continually renewed
surface ([Pappalardo et al. 1999](literatur:pappalardo-1999)); models of impact rates in the outer
Solar System that translate such crater densities into surface ages were presented by Zahnle et al.
([Zahnle et al. 2003](literatur:zahnle-2003)). How young individual craters are can be read from the
progressive darkening caused by bombardment with energetic particles from Jupiter's magnetosphere:
for the two bright ray craters Pwyll and Manannán, this clock gives an age of only about 1 to 20
million years ([Davis and Brown 2024](literatur:davis-2024)) — younger than practically any
comparable feature on Earth's Moon.

Spectra also show that the surface is not pure water ice. The Hubble Space Telescope detected sodium
chloride in material likely sourced from Europa's interior
([Trumbo et al. 2019](literatur:trumbo-2019)) — a direct clue to the composition of the presumed
ocean. The James Webb Space Telescope mapped four absorption bands of carbon-dioxide ice in 2022,
concentrated in the geologically young region Tara Regio; a simultaneous search for plumes of water,
carbon monoxide, methanol, ethane or methane yielded no detection
([Villanueva et al. 2023](literatur:villanueva-2023)). The isotope ratios of the carbon at the same
location suggest that it originates from within Europa rather than being supplied from outside,
although formation at the surface itself cannot be excluded
([Trumbo and Brown 2023](literatur:trumbo-2023)).

Whether Europa also shows episodically active water-vapour plumes is disputed; the evidence for and
against is presented under Open questions, below.

## Atmosphere and magnetosphere

Europa carries an extremely thin oxygen atmosphere, which the Hubble Space Telescope detected in
1995 as far-ultraviolet airglow ([Hall et al. 1995](literatur:hall-1995)). It arises through
radiolysis: energetic particles from Jupiter's magnetosphere and photons split water ice at the
surface, the light hydrogen escapes, and the heavier oxygen accumulates — a mechanism with no
volcanic origin at all, unlike Io's sulphur-dioxide atmosphere.

Europa has no magnetosphere of its own: the field measured by Kivelson et al. is, as described
above, an induced one, not one generated by its own dynamo
([Kivelson et al. 2000](literatur:kivelson-2000)). Instead, Europa sits in the middle of Jupiter's
intense radiation belt, closer to it than Ganymede or Callisto; the Europa Clipper spacecraft avoids
the resulting high radiation dose by orbiting Jupiter rather than Europa itself, observing the moon
during 49 close flybys at typically 25 to 100 km distance instead of remaining permanently nearby
([Pappalardo et al. 2024](literatur:pappalardo-2024)).

## Orbit, rotation and dynamics

Io, Europa and Ganymede satisfy, on average, the Laplace resonance with the mean-motion ratio 4:2:1
and the librating angle $\varphi_\mathrm{L} = \lambda_1 - 3\lambda_2 + 2\lambda_3 = 180^\circ$; the
derivation and the two-body angles are presented under [Orbital resonances](thema:resonanzen). The
resonance forces Europa's orbital eccentricity on average to 0.009 (Showman and Malhotra 1997,
cited after [Ershkov and Leshchenko 2021](literatur:ershkov-2021)), more than twice the 0.0041
forced on Io; without this constant drive, tidal friction would circularise the orbit within a short
time and switch off the heating itself — just as at [Enceladus](objekt:enceladus), whose forced
eccentricity is sustained by the resonance with Dione. Whether this coupling remains stable over
very long timescales is itself the subject of current research
([Ershkov and Leshchenko 2021](literatur:ershkov-2021)). The scene
[Galilean shadow play](szene:galileisches-schattenspiel) shows how Io, Europa and Ganymede eclipse
and occult one another in the process.

Whether Europa's crust rotates synchronously with its orbit or turns slightly faster — decoupled
from the rocky interior by the ocean — has been disputed since the first Galileo images. From the
orientation and distribution of lineaments, Geissler et al. concluded that Europa rotates faster
than synchronous, or did so in the past ([Geissler et al. 1998](literatur:geissler-1998)). A more
recent analysis of the hemispheric colour dichotomy — the pattern produced by the uneven bombardment
of the trailing hemisphere with energetic particles, which can only persist if the discolouration
acts much faster than rotation away from the synchronous state — instead finds no detectable
signature of non-synchronous rotation in the Voyager data
([Burnett and Hayne 2021](literatur:burnett-2021)). The question remains open (see below);
classification and model figures for all moons of the Solar System are given under
[Bound rotation](thema:gebundene-rotation).

Europa's orbit itself also evolves: an astrometric analysis of the positions of all four Galilean
moons over more than a century finds a relative change in Europa's mean motion of
$-0.43 \pm 0.10$ in units of $10^{-10}$ per year — Europa, unlike Io, is thus migrating slightly
outward on average ([Lainey et al. 2025](literatur:lainey-2025)); its place within Jupiter's tidal
dissipation is presented under [Tides and the Roche limit](thema:gezeiten).

## Formation and evolution

Like the other Galilean moons, Europa probably formed in a "starved" circumjovian gas disc that
contained little solid mass at any one time and was replenished over millions of years by inflowing
material; this picture fits best with the ice fraction that increases from the inside outward across
the four moons and with Callisto's presumably only partially differentiated interior
([Nimmo et al. 2026](literatur:nimmo-2026)). Within this disc, Europa formed farther from Jupiter
than Io: cold enough for water ice to condense and survive, yet still close enough that tides and
the later capture into the Laplace resonance heated the interior enough to keep an ocean liquid,
rather than letting it freeze completely as at the moons farther out
([Nimmo et al. 2026](literatur:nimmo-2026)). The broader context of planet formation is presented
under [Formation of the Solar System](thema:entstehung).

How long this ocean has already existed, and whether it has been continuously liquid since
formation, depends on the same thermal-orbital coupling that also sets today's heating rate: if
Europa's orbital eccentricity changed substantially over time, the tidal heating changed with it,
and potentially the freezing state of the ocean
([Hussmann and Spohn 2004](literatur:hussmann-2004)). Later evolution — tides, impacts, ongoing
crustal motion — has overprinted the original signatures of formation, which are today only partly
accessible ([Nimmo et al. 2026](literatur:nimmo-2026)).

Two missions are set to examine Europa more closely in the coming years. NASA's Europa Clipper
spacecraft, launched in October 2024, is due to enter Jupiter orbit in 2030 and study Europa during
49 close flybys at typically 25 to 100 km distance with remote-sensing instruments and in-situ
sensors, addressing habitability, composition, geology and ongoing activity
([Pappalardo et al. 2024](literatur:pappalardo-2024)). ESA's JUICE spacecraft, under way since April
2023, is primarily designed for Ganymede but will characterise the Jupiter system as a whole on the
way there, with Europa as one of its targets
([Grasset et al. 2013](literatur:grasset-2013); [Juice (ESA)](quelle:esa-juice)).

## Open questions

- **A thin or a thick ice shell?** The mobile, often tilted ice blocks of the chaos terrains and the
  tight radii of curvature of some cycloidal cracks have, since the first Galileo images, suggested
  a thin, mechanically easily deformed crust
  ([Pappalardo et al. 1999](literatur:pappalardo-1999)). A thermal-equilibrium model that propagates
  the uncertainty of about 20 parameters through around ten million independent simulations instead
  yields an asymmetric distribution with a most likely value of 24.3 km and a range of about 23 to
  47 km — thick shells are markedly more likely than thin ones in this model
  ([Howell 2021](literatur:howell-2021)). The two approaches average over very different time and
  length scales, and which thickness actually best explains the observations is not settled.
- **Are there active plumes?** Ultraviolet observations with Hubble found a statistically
  significant emission signal at the south pole, consistent with two water-vapour plumes about
  200 km high ([Roth et al. 2014](literatur:roth-2014)); a re-analysis of the magnetometer and
  plasma-wave data from a 1997 Galileo flyby found signatures likewise consistent with a plume
  ([Jia et al. 2018](literatur:jia-2018)). A targeted search with the James Webb Space Telescope in
  2022, however, found no detection of water, carbon monoxide, methanol, ethane or methane emission
  ([Villanueva et al. 2023](literatur:villanueva-2023)). Since plumes appear, on present evidence,
  to occur episodically and locally, a single observing window without a detection does not rule out
  earlier ones; whether and how often Europa actually vents matter is open.
- **Does the crust rotate non-synchronously?** Lineaments argued for it
  ([Geissler et al. 1998](literatur:geissler-1998)), the colour dichotomy finds no signature
  ([Burnett and Hayne 2021](literatur:burnett-2021)); details and the comparison with other moons
  are under [Bound rotation](thema:gebundene-rotation).
- **Is Europa habitable?** Liquid water, chemical energy from tidal heating and radiolysis, and
  possible organic compounds would coincide there; whether the conditions are actually sufficient
  and whether material is exchanged between the ocean and the surface are questions Europa Clipper
  and JUICE have yet to settle ([Pappalardo et al. 2024](literatur:pappalardo-2024);
  [Grasset et al. 2013](literatur:grasset-2013)).

## In the model

- **Orbital reference:** Like the other Jovian moons, Europa moves in the `parentEquator` reference
  on Jupiter's equatorial plane (pole 268.057°/64.495° from [Jupiter](objekt:jupiter)). Unlike Io
  ($i = 0.0^\circ$), the orbital inclination $i = 0.5^\circ$ is not negligible: recomputing
  `positionInParentFrame` at the epoch shows that the node value (184.0°) genuinely determines the
  orientation — substituting a trial node of 0° shifts the $z$ coordinate alone by about 6172 km,
  instead of cancelling out numerically as it does for Io. The eccentricity is fixed at
  $e = 0.009$ (`eDot` 0), exactly matching the eccentricity of 0.009 forced by the resonance on
  average (Showman and Malhotra 1997, cited after
  [Ershkov and Leshchenko 2021](literatur:ershkov-2021)).
- **Nodal period:** From `nodeDot` ($-1191.9740^\circ$ per century) follows a period of
  $360/1191.974 \times 100 = 30.202$ years, regressing; this matches exactly the "node precession
  period" of the underlying element table (header comment in `jupiter-monde.ts`), so it mainly
  confirms the calculation by which `nodeDot` itself was derived from that table. An independent,
  freely accessible literature source giving the same figure could not be opened in this session;
  the order of magnitude (a few decades, markedly shorter than Ganymede's roughly 137.8 and
  Callisto's roughly 577.3 years) is familiar from the celestial mechanics of the Galilean moons.
- **Perijove period:** From $\dot\varpi$ (24,632.9901° per century) follows a period of
  $360/\dot\varpi \times 100 = 1.46$ years; as with Io, the perijove thus advances forward instead
  of, as theory would predict, regressing (figures as in
  [Orbital resonances](thema:resonanzen)).
- **Orbital period of the data set:** From $\dot L$ (3,702,711.858393° per century) follows
  $36525/(\dot L/360) = 3.551181$ days — exactly the value with which Europa actually orbits in the
  model, and which the fact sheet also gives. The orbital-element table's own column P instead lists
  3.525463 days, 0.72 % below that; as with Io, this column of the table is not plausible for Europa
  (header comment in `jupiter-monde.ts`). Independent cross-checks agree well with the model value:
  Kepler's third law with the $GM$ of the Jupiter system gives 3.551988 days, a direct fit of
  position vectors 3.550798 days.
- **Rotation ratio:** `rotationPeriodH` (85.22835 h) matches the sidereal period computed from
  $\dot L$ (85.228344 h) to $0.070 \cdot 10^{-6}$ relative; the angle between the map centre and the
  direction to Jupiter is 31.5° at the epoch and 31.2° after a hundred years (figures as in
  [Bound rotation](thema:gebundene-rotation)).
- **Pole:** 268.08°/64.51° from the IAU rotation elements (SPICE kernel), fixed at the epoch; close
  both to Jupiter's own pole and to Europa's Laplace pole listed separately in the orbital-element
  table (268.1°/64.5°, tilt 0.0°; recomputed deviation from the stored Jupiter pole 0.019°, as for
  Io).
- **Albedo and texture:** The albedo 0.68 is the fact sheet's geometric albedo, matched exactly. The
  texture is a Voyager mosaic from Caltech/JPL/USGS (`ASSETS.md`) and is normalised to the catalogue
  albedo like every body's ([Albedo and brightness](thema:photometrie)); neither the lineae and
  chaos structures nor possible plumes are drawn separately in it, `appearance` in
  `jupiter-monde.ts` carries only an albedo texture.
- **Shadows:** Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto are simultaneously occluders of Jupiter and of one
  another (`MAX_OKKLUDER` 4 in `render/shadows.ts`); each of the four moons is therefore shadowed by
  Jupiter and the three others. A moon at distance $r$ casts its shadow onto a sphere of radius $R$
  only if the solar elevation above the orbital plane stays below $\arcsin(R/r)$; with Jupiter's
  radius of 69,911 km ($R$) and Europa's orbital radius of 671,100 km ($r$) this gives about
  $5.98^\circ$ — well above Jupiter's axial tilt, whether recomputed against the orbit normal
  stored in the data set as 3.1200° or taken from the fact sheet as 3.13° (both figures are meant
  here). Europa therefore always casts a shadow, like Io, unlike Callisto with only 2.1° of
  permissible solar elevation.
- **Scale:** Europa is a satellite (`isSatellite`, its `parent` is Jupiter, not the Sun); its
  orbital radius around Jupiter therefore scales, like its own and Jupiter's radius, with
  `sizeScale` rather than with the distance compression applied to planetary orbits
  (`scaledPositionAt`).
- **Data panel:** The mass carried in the data set ($4.800 \cdot 10^{22}\,\mathrm{kg}$), multiplied
  by $G$ from CODATA 2018, gives a $GM$ of $3203.664\,\mathrm{km^3\,s^{-2}}$, about 297 ppm above the
  value measured by orbit tracking in the table above. The data panel computes the orbital period
  from Kepler's law with $a$ and $G\,(M_\mathrm{Jup} + M)$ (`umlaufzeitTage`) as 3.552022 days,
  0.024 % above the sidereal period with which Europa actually moves in the model. Further
  simplifications: [Limits of the model](thema:modell).
