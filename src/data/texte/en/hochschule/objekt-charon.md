# Charon

At just over half of Pluto's radius, Charon is by far the largest of the five known Pluto moons
and carries the largest known mass ratio of a moon to its primary among the dwarf planets
recognized by the International Astronomical Union (see Parameters and measurement). James
Christy discovered it in 1978 at the US Naval Observatory as a recurring bulge on photographic
plates of Pluto; on 14 July 2015 New Horizons also flew past Charon and revealed for the first
time a geologically distinct world, quite different from Pluto
([Stern et al. 2015](literatur:stern-2015); [Moore et al. 2016](literatur:moore-2016)). This text
presents parameters, interior, surface, atmosphere, orbit and formation, and closes with what
Orrery models of all this, in particular the missing barycentric orbit and an axial tilt that
reads very differently for a doubly synchronous moon than for a planet. Pluto's own parameters and
the system's barycenter appear under [Pluto](objekt:pluto).

## Parameters and measurement

New Horizons images yielded a mean radius of $606.0 \pm 1.0\,\mathrm{km}$ from the same limb-fit
method used for Pluto ([Nimmo et al. 2017](literatur:nimmo-2017)); neither body shows a
measurable flattening. The combined orbit solution for all moons known at the time gives
$GM_\mathrm{Charon} = 106.1 \pm 0.3\,\mathrm{km^3\,s^{-2}}$
([Brozović et al. 2015](literatur:brozovic-2015)); with the radius, $\bar\rho = 3\,GM/(4\pi G
R^3)$ gives a mean density of about $1.70\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ (derivation) — clearly below
Pluto's roughly $1.85\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ ([Pluto's parameters](objekt:pluto)) and a first hint
of a smaller rock fraction. The same $GM$ pair gives the mass ratio
$M_\mathrm{Charon}/M_\mathrm{Pluto} = GM_\mathrm{Charon}/GM_\mathrm{Pluto} \approx 0.122$
(derivation) — the largest known ratio of a moon to its primary among the five dwarf planets
officially recognized by the IAU, and larger than for any of the eight planets (Earth–Moon, the
largest of those, is about 0.0123). Among transneptunian bodies not yet officially classified as
dwarf planets, the Orcus–Vanth system now exceeds this value: radio-interferometric orbit
tracking with ALMA found a mass ratio there of $0.16 \pm 0.02$, the highest measured to date for
any planet or dwarf planet ([Brown and Butler 2023](literatur:brown-butler-2023)). Charon's
geometric albedo ranges across its surface from about 0.4 to 0.6, with a few brighter crater
ejecta patches and a LORRI full-disk mean of 0.41
([Buratti et al. 2017](literatur:buratti-2017)); the catalog value of 0.42 is a rounded NSSDC
full-disk mean and matches this photometrically determined value more closely than Pluto's does
(see In the model).

## Interior

A density of about $1.70\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ requires, as for Pluto, a differentiated body with
a rock core beneath an ice shell, though with a smaller rock fraction than Pluto. Charon's surface
itself carries the evidence in the form of extensive graben and faults: a global mapping of the
New Horizons images finds an extensional areal strain of about one percent early in Charon's
history, consistent with a former global ocean beneath an ice crust that expanded on freezing and
stretched and cracked the crust ([Beyer et al. 2017](literatur:beyer-2017)); the most conspicuous
of these features, Serenity Chasma, is part of a belt of graben and faults that separates the
rugged, old northern hemisphere from the smoother, younger plains of Vulcan Planitia in the south
(see Surface). A thermal evolution model that jointly tracks radiogenic heat sources, ammonia as
a freezing-point depressant in the ice, and the thermal conductivity of the layers finds a
two-stage history: an early, extensive ocean froze completely about 1.7 to 2.5 billion years ago,
with accompanying crustal extension that could explain Serenity Chasma and the broadly younger
appearance of Vulcan Planitia; subsequent radiogenic heat buildup then produced a second, smaller
ocean that froze about 0.5 to 1.7 billion years ago and, in doing so, could have enabled
cryovolcanism ([Desch and Neveu 2017](literatur:desch-2017)). No liquid water is expected today:
without a continuing heat input from tidal friction — which ends in the doubly synchronous
Pluto–Charon system once mutual locking is reached (see Orbit, rotation and dynamics) — a body
this small should long since have frozen to the core. Exactly how the transition from one ocean
to the other and the final freezing proceeded in time depends strongly on the thermal and tidal
model used and remains open (see Open questions).

## Surface

Charon's surface is mostly water ice, to which New Horizons spectra add ammonia and ammonia
hydrates: mapping with the infrared spectrometer LEISA detects this characteristic absorption
feature over much of the surface, concentrated in particular around the young, smooth crater
Organa ([Dalle Ore et al. 2018](literatur:dalle-ore-2018)); because ammonia is broken down by
sunlight and cosmic rays on geologically short timescales, its presence is taken as a sign of
comparatively young, possibly cryovolcanically resurfaced ground. The terrains differ from north
to south along exactly this contrast: the older, densely faulted northern hemisphere — with
Serenity Chasma as the largest single graben of the belt system (see Interior) — borders the
markedly smoother, younger and less cratered Vulcan Planitia to the south. Within Vulcan Planitia
lie isolated, dome-shaped rises with a central depression such as Kubrick Mons, whose unusual
morphology suggests a cryovolcanic origin, though a purely tectonic formation cannot be excluded
(see Open questions); the two-stage freezing model described above links such an event in time to
the freezing of the second, smaller ocean ([Desch and Neveu 2017](literatur:desch-2017)). A
systematic crater count on Pluto and Charon together found, compared with models of Kuiper belt
collisional evolution, markedly fewer small craters than expected — evidence independent of Pluto
that small Kuiper belt objects are rarer than equilibrium models predict
([Singer et al. 2019](literatur:singer-2019)).

The most striking feature seen from afar is Charon's reddish-brown north polar cap, informally
named Mordor Macula. It does not originate on Charon itself but from methane escaping Pluto's
thin atmosphere: some of it is captured by Charon's gravity, bounces across the surface until it
escapes or lands on the pole, which is especially cold during polar winter, freezes there as a
thin methane-ice film, and is converted (photolyzed) by sunlight and cosmic rays into heavier,
reddish hydrocarbons and tholins; the original methane ice itself sublimates away quickly again
in spring, while the heavier reaction products remain and build up over many Charon years
([Grundy et al. 2016](literatur:grundy-2016)).

## Atmosphere and magnetosphere

Charon has no detectable atmosphere. During the flyby, New Horizons instruments searched with
solar UV occultation, UV airglow measurements and a high-phase look-back for scattered light from
atmospheric haze; for 14 possible atomic and molecular species, the resulting upper limits were
consistently below $0.3\,\mathrm{nbar}$, for a pure nitrogen atmosphere the upper limit was only
$4.2\,\mathrm{pbar}$ ($3\sigma$), and for haze the upper brightness limit was
$I/F = 2.6\times10^{-5}$ ([Stern et al. 2017](literatur:stern-2017)) — several orders of
magnitude tighter than the much coarser limits from an earlier ground-based stellar occultation
($110\,\mathrm{nbar}$ for a pure nitrogen, $15\,\mathrm{nbar}$ for a pure methane atmosphere,
each $3\sigma$). Unlike Pluto, Charon is too small and too cold to hold a sublimation-driven
atmosphere against escape for long; the methane trapped on its surface (see Surface) stays bound
rather than feeding an atmosphere of its own. Charon has no magnetic field of its own and, like
Pluto, sits unshielded in the solar wind (values and context for the system under
[Pluto](objekt:pluto)).

## Orbit, rotation and dynamics

Charon orbits Pluto on a nearly circular path ($e \approx 0.0002$) with a semi-major axis of
about $19\,596\,\mathrm{km}$ (Horizons orbit solution "plu060_merged", essentially matching the
roughly $19\,595.8\,\mathrm{km}$ of the JPL mean-elements table for the Pluto moons) in Pluto's
equatorial plane, with an orbital period of $6.387\,\mathrm{d}$
($153.29333\,\mathrm{h}$, JPL mean-elements table). Pluto and Charon are the best-known doubly
synchronous pair in the solar system: both rotation periods match this orbital period, an end
state that tidal calculations reproduce starting from Charon on an eccentric, close orbit with
full conservation of angular momentum ([Cheng et al. 2014](literatur:cheng-2014); context under
[Tidal locking](thema:gebundene-rotation)). The angle between the map center of Charon's surface
model and the direction to Pluto is correspondingly fixed at $48.9^\circ$ and drifts by only
$0.3^\circ$ per century (value as in [Tidal locking](thema:gebundene-rotation)) — the map center
does not point at Pluto, but Charon still keeps the same face toward Pluto permanently. The
system's common barycenter lies, as derived under [Pluto](objekt:pluto), about
$2126\,\mathrm{km}$ (1.79 times Pluto's radius) from Pluto's center, outside Pluto's surface but
well within Charon's own orbit; Charon itself orbits this point at a distance of about
$17\,464\,\mathrm{km}$ (derivation), a much larger amplitude than Pluto's, but with the same
period.

From 1985 to 1990, Pluto and Charon, seen from Earth, repeatedly occulted and eclipsed each other
— so far the only series of this kind observed for this pair. This series yielded the discovery
of water ice on Charon, the first useful radii for both bodies, and the surprisingly high mean
density of the system of about $2\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ (context and source under
[Eclipses and transits](thema:finsternis)). The photometric method used to analyze that series
also underlies newer predictions: a systematic search for comparable event windows among other
transneptunian binary systems forecasts ongoing or imminent event series in the 2020s and 2030s
for five of them — among them (38628) Huya and (58534) Logos-Zoe — though a new Pluto-Charon
window is not among them
([Proudfoot et al. 2026](literatur:proudfoot-2026)).

## Formation and evolution

Charon most likely formed in an oblique impact of a body nearly the size of Pluto early in the
system's history (see [Pluto](objekt:pluto)). In the older, hydrodynamic interpretation, such an
impact, given a suitable geometry, leaves an intact, water-rich debris body on a close orbit that
circularizes and migrates outward to become Charon ([Canup 2005](literatur:canup-2005)); a more
recent simulation that accounts for the material strength of cold bodies instead finds a brief
"kiss", in which Pluto and the impactor merge momentarily into a single rotating body, before both
partners separate again ahead of complete merging and remain behind as a separate, bound pair — a
mechanism that leaves both bodies largely intact
([Denton et al. 2025a](literatur:denton-2025a)). Both scenarios predict different internal heat
budgets for the young Charon and thus different starting conditions for the ocean described under
Interior. The impact initially left Charon on an eccentric, close orbit; tidal friction in both
bodies removed angular momentum and eccentricity and carried Charon outward until Pluto and
Charon reached today's doubly synchronous end state
([Cheng et al. 2014](literatur:cheng-2014)). The same impact is thought to be the likely origin
of the four small moons Styx, Nix, Kerberos and Hydra as well, which accreted further out from the
debris disk of the event (context under [Pluto](objekt:pluto)).

## Open questions

- **How exactly did Charon form — through a classical impact followed by circularization, or
  through "kiss and capture"?** Both mechanisms are consistent with the roughly known orbit and
  density values ([Canup 2005](literatur:canup-2005); [Denton et al. 2025a](literatur:denton-2025a)),
  but predict different thermal histories.
- **When did Charon's ocean or oceans freeze through, and do any remnants survive?** The
  two-stage model with freezing times around 1.7–2.5 and 0.5–1.7 billion years ago rests on
  assumptions about ammonia content and thermal conductivity that cannot be tested independently
  and directly ([Desch and Neveu 2017](literatur:desch-2017)); whether any liquid water still
  exists today is open.
- **Where do the ammonia hydrates come from, and how old is Vulcan Planitia really?** The
  ammonia signature points to geologically young material, but an absolute age from crater counts
  for the smooth plains themselves is not available
  ([Dalle Ore et al. 2018](literatur:dalle-ore-2018)).
- **How long did the tidal evolution to double locking take, and by what path?** The outcome
  depends strongly on the tidal model used and on the ratio of dissipation in Charon to that in
  Pluto; different assumptions lead to different timescales and intermediate states
  ([Cheng et al. 2014](literatur:cheng-2014); context under
  [Tidal locking](thema:gebundene-rotation)).

## In the model

- **Orbit:** Charon runs in the `parentEquator` frame referenced to Pluto's equatorial plane,
  with osculating Horizons elements at epoch J2000 ($a = 19\,595.762\,\mathrm{km}$,
  $e = 0.000161$, $i = 0.0841^\circ$ against Pluto's equator) and no precession (values as in
  [Orbital elements](thema:bahnelemente)); the small inclination reflects that Charon runs
  essentially exactly in Pluto's equatorial plane, as mutually tidally locked rotation physically
  requires.
- **Pluto at the origin, Charon around Pluto's center instead of the barycenter:** As derived
  under [Pluto](objekt:pluto), the true system barycenter lies about 2126 km (1.79 Pluto radii)
  from Pluto's center; Orrery instead keeps Pluto fixed at the origin of its system and lets
  Charon visibly orbit Pluto's center rather than this barycenter. This makes no difference to
  Charon's own, relative orbital shape — the displayed ellipse is exactly the true relative orbit
  — only the reference point of the motion differs from reality; the scene
  ["Pluto and Charon, a double world"](szene:pluto-charon) accordingly shows only Charon in
  visible motion.
- **Rotation and pole:** Charon carries the same pole direction ($132.993^\circ/-6.163^\circ$)
  and the same positive rotation period $153.29335\,\mathrm{h}$ as Pluto (values as in
  [Tidal locking](thema:gebundene-rotation)). The axial tilt independently recomputed with
  `achsneigungDeg` gives only $0.0841^\circ$ for Charon — practically identical to the raw orbital
  inclination $i$ against Pluto's equator above, because Charon's own pole coincides with the
  pole that defines the reference plane of its orbit (Pluto's pole). That is a very different
  picture from Pluto's own axial tilt of $119.614^\circ$, measured against the heliocentric
  orbital normal ([Pluto's axial tilt](objekt:pluto)): both numbers are correctly computed with
  the same formula but measure different things — the quantity that is actually meaningful for
  the double lock is not Charon's axial tilt but the near-constant angle of $48.9^\circ$ between
  map center and the direction to Pluto noted above.
- **Orbital period in the data panel against the dataset and rotation:** The Kepler orbital
  period computed by the data panel from the semi-major axis and the catalog masses
  $G(M_\mathrm{Pluto}+M_\mathrm{Charon})$ gives $153.286\,\mathrm{h}$; the value
  $360^\circ/\dot L$ computed from the dataset's orbital rate $\dot L$ gives $153.2933\,\mathrm{h}$,
  matching the JPL mean-elements table; both lie less than 0.005 % below the rotation period
  of $153.29335\,\mathrm{h}$ shared by Pluto and Charon — deviations far below display precision
  that keep the double lock internally consistent in the model.
- **Albedo:** The catalog value of 0.42 is an NSSDC full-disk mean and matches the
  photometrically measured LORRI mean of 0.41 ([Buratti et al. 2017](literatur:buratti-2017))
  more closely than Pluto's does (0.52 against a measured 0.62); each body carries its albedo
  normalized to the slider range ([Albedo and brightness](thema:photometrie)).
- **Polar cap as texture only:** Mordor Macula appears only as part of the static New Horizons
  global-mosaic texture (`textures/charon/albedo-1024.ktx2`, source
  `assets-quellen/texturen/charon/albedo.jpg`, `ASSETS.md`), not as its own physical
  model; the model has no methane transport from Pluto, no freeze-out and no photolysis, and the
  hemisphere the probe never imaged (about a third of the surface) stays unlit and black.
- **Shadows:** Charon is a satellite (`isSatellite`, `parent` is Pluto); `waehleOkkluder`
  therefore always adds Pluto first as the parent-body occluder for Charon, and because Charon is
  the only moon in Pluto's system, no further candidate remains — Pluto is consequently the only
  possible shadow caster on Charon, mirroring Pluto, for which only Charon qualifies
  (`MAX_OKKLUDER = 4` in `render/shadows.ts` goes unused here, value as in
  [Eclipses and transits](thema:finsternis)).
- **Scale:** Charon is a satellite; its orbital radius therefore scales with `sizeScale` like
  Pluto's own radius, not with the distance compression applied to the planetary orbits
  (`sim/scale.ts`). Further simplifications: [Limits of the model](thema:modell).
