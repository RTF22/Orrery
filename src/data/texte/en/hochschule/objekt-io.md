# Io

Io is the innermost of the four Galilean moons and, at a radius of 1821.5 km, slightly larger
than Earth’s [Moon](objekt:moon). Together with [Europa](objekt:europa) and
[Ganymede](objekt:ganymede) it stands in the Laplace resonance around [Jupiter](objekt:jupiter);
the proximity to Jupiter and the resonance together heat its interior so strongly that Io is the
most volcanically active body in the Solar System. This text presents the gravity field and
interior, the surface, the atmosphere and magnetosphere, and the orbit, rotation and formation,
and finally describes what Orrery renders of it.

## Parameters and measurement

Four flybys of the Galileo spacecraft between 1995 and 1999 delivered Io’s first own gravity
field; the Doppler shift of the orbit yielded a potential Love number
$k_\mathrm{f} = 1.2924 \pm 0.0027$ describing the hydrostatic figure
([Anderson et al. 2001](literatur:anderson-2001)). The principal axes derived from it,
$1830.0 \pm 0.5$, $1819.2 \pm 0.5$ and $1815.6 \pm 0.5\,\mathrm{km}$, match an ellipsoid in
hydrostatic equilibrium: Io is therefore, as expected for a body of its size and heat production,
soft enough to adjust its shape to rotation and to Jupiter’s tides. Combined with the gravity
field this yields models with a metallic core, 350 to 650 km in radius for pure iron or 550 to
900 km for an iron–sulphur alloy, depending on the assumed composition
([Anderson et al. 2001](literatur:anderson-2001)); such a core lowers the
[moment-of-inertia factor](thema:innerer-aufbau) below the value 0.4 of a homogeneous sphere.
More than 25 years later, two close Juno flybys in December 2023 and February 2024, combined
with Galileo data and astrometry, measured the real part of the dissipative Love number
directly for the first time: $k_2 = 0.125 \pm 0.047$ at a quality factor $Q = 11.4 \pm 3.6$
(1σ; [Park et al. 2025](literatur:park-2025)). The ratio $k_2/Q = 0.011$ is close to the older,
purely astrometric value $k_2/Q = 0.015 \pm 0.003$
([Lainey et al. 2009](literatur:lainey-2009)).

A globally averaged heat flow of about 2.5 W/m² over the spherical surface
($4\pi R^2 \approx 4.17 \cdot 10^{13}\,\mathrm{m^2}$) gives a total power of about 104 TW — close
to the roughly 100 TW with which [Tides and the Roche limit](thema:gezeiten) compares Io’s
measured and $k_2/Q$-derived heat output.

## Interior

The gravity field alone does not sharply distinguish a homogeneous body from a layered one;
only together with the hydrostatic shape does it require a dense, metallic core beneath a
silicate mantle ([Anderson et al. 2001](literatur:anderson-2001)). The small measured real part
of $k_2$ fits an overwhelmingly solid mantle better than an extensive liquid layer near the
surface ([Park et al. 2025](literatur:park-2025)). An older, independent observation points the
other way: during Galileo’s passages through Io’s penumbra, the magnetometer measured an
induction response indicating a well-conducting, contiguous layer near the surface — most simply
explained by a global magma ocean of partially molten silicate rock
([Khurana et al. 2011](literatur:khurana-2011)). The two findings do not necessarily contradict
each other: Park et al. rule out a *shallow* global magma ocean under a crust of at most 50 km,
but not a deeper, thinner melt zone below about 318 km
([Park et al. 2025](literatur:park-2025)) — how strongly and where exactly Io is molten inside
therefore remains open (see below). How this tidal heating is distributed inside and what drives
it is treated under [Tides and the Roche limit](thema:gezeiten); more on core formation and
moment of inertia in moons in general is under [Interior structure](thema:innerer-aufbau).

## Surface

Infrared surveys have identified more than 75 individual active hot spots (see below); Io is
thus the most volcanically active body known in the Solar System. Voyager 1 discovered in 1979 the first example
of active volcanism beyond Earth, a high-rising plume at the volcano Pele
([Morabito et al. 1979](literatur:morabito-1979)). Galileo later found at least a dozen sites
with lava hotter than any terrestrial basaltic eruption; at the volcano Pillan Patera the
temperature exceeded 1700 K and possibly reached 2000 K, consistent with ultramafic,
magnesium-rich rock ([McEwen et al. 1998](literatur:mcewen-1998)). New Horizons observed a
particularly large eruption of the volcano Tvashtar in 2007 on its flyby toward Pluto
([Spencer et al. 2007](literatur:spencer-2007)). The most persistent infrared source on the
surface is the lava lake Loki, which for decades has carried the largest share of Io’s observed
heat flow; a decade of ground-based infrared measurements found it regularly brighter than any
other single source, with high-temperature “outbursts” in about 4 % of the nights observed
([Veeder et al. 1994](literatur:veeder-1994)). 271 nights of adaptive optics from 2013 to 2018
yielded nearly 1000 detections at more than 75 individual hot spots, with especially bright
eruptions occurring preferentially on the trailing hemisphere
([de Kleer et al. 2019](literatur:dekleer-2019)).

Sulphur and sulphur dioxide in various crystal forms and purities give the surface its yellow,
orange, red and white colours; the dark, often steaming areas around active centres are freshly
deposited silicate or sulphurous material. Io’s highest mountains, such as the several-kilometre
Euboea Montes, are not volcanic edifices but crustal blocks thrust up along faults that
subsequently spread and flow downslope over large areas
([Schenk and Bulmer 1998](literatur:schenk-1998)). Impact craters are practically absent because
deposition and erosion continually bury them: during the five years of the Galileo mission, at
least 83 % of the surface remained externally unchanged despite more than 80 documented surface
changes, four previously unknown explosive centres newly appeared, and repeated eruptions of the
smaller, SO₂-rich plumes — which typically leave bright rings 150 to 200 km across, compared with
barely half a dozen much larger plumes reaching 500 to 550 km — appear to contribute the largest
share to resurfacing ([Geissler et al. 2004](literatur:geissler-2004)).

## Atmosphere and magnetosphere

Io carries a thin atmosphere consisting mostly of sulphur dioxide that is largely in equilibrium
with frozen SO₂ frost on the surface rather than stemming solely from volcanic gases.
High-resolution infrared spectroscopy in the 19 µm band showed, during a Jupiter eclipse, how the
absorption band collapsed from $2.5 \pm 0.08$ % before the eclipse to $0.18 \pm 0.16$ % after
40 minutes in shadow, corresponding to a decrease in column density by roughly a factor of
$5 \pm 2$ — a clear sign that sublimation and freezing of the frost sustain the atmosphere once
the Sun is absent ([Tsang et al. 2016](literatur:tsang-2016); shadow geometry:
[Eclipses](thema:finsternis)).

Material ejected volcanically and by sputtering from charged particles forms a ring of neutral
particles along Io’s orbit, whose ionisation is the principal source of plasma in Jupiter’s
magnetosphere; from there charged particles stream along field lines into Jupiter’s ionosphere
and create an auroral footprint that travels with Io
([Bagenal and Dols 2020](literatur:bagenal-2020)). This coupling acts through the magnetosphere
far out into interplanetary space.

## Orbit, rotation and dynamics

Io, Europa and Ganymede stand in the Laplace resonance with the mean-motion ratio 4:2:1 and the
librating angle $\varphi_\mathrm{L} = \lambda_1 - 3\lambda_2 + 2\lambda_3 = 180^\circ$; the
derivation, the two-body angles and the resulting forced eccentricity of Io of 0.0041 are
presented under [Orbital resonances](thema:resonanzen). This forced eccentricity sustains the
tidal heating, which would otherwise circularise the orbit within a short time and switch off
the heating itself. The scene [Galilean shadow play](szene:galileisches-schattenspiel) shows how
Io, Europa and Ganymede eclipse and occult one another in the process.

The orbital evolution that follows from this resonance is itself an active field of research.
Astrometric observations from 1891 to 2007 showed surprisingly strong tidal dissipation: Io is
currently migrating inward, not outward as the simple expectation of a moon accelerated by
Jupiter would suggest, and all three inner Galilean moons are moving away from the exact
resonance ([Lainey et al. 2009](literatur:lainey-2009)). A model posted in 2026 that jointly fits
astrometry, heat flow, tidal response and moment of inertia to an orbital-thermal evolution
model interprets the present state not as an endpoint but as a phase within a limit cycle that
has existed for about 0.8 to 2.0 billion years, with a repeat period of roughly 95 to 150 million
years, in which Io migrates inward for a time while Europa and Ganymede migrate outward
([Bagheri et al. 2026](literatur:bagheri-2026)).

Io rotates in a [bound](thema:gebundene-rotation) state: its rotation and orbital periods agree,
and Io permanently shows Jupiter the same face. Historically, it was precisely Io’s eclipses by
Jupiter’s shadow that in 1676 gave the first hint that light takes time to travel, when observed
timings systematically deviated from expectation
([Bobis and Lequeux 2008](literatur:bobis-2008)); details of the shadow geometry and of mutual
occultations among the Galilean moons are likewise presented under
[Eclipses](thema:finsternis).

## Formation and evolution

The Galilean moons probably formed in a “starved” circumjovian gas disc that contained little
solid mass at any one time and was replenished over millions of years by inflowing material;
this picture fits best with the ice fraction that increases from the inside outward across the
four moons and with Callisto’s presumably only partially differentiated interior
([Nimmo et al. 2026](literatur:nimmo-2026)). Io formed closest within it to the young, still hot
Jupiter: in this zone the temperature was not low enough for water ice to condense or survive,
whereas farther out, where Europa, Ganymede and Callisto formed, it was cold enough for ice — an
explanation for why Io alone among the four is practically free of water
([Nimmo et al. 2026](literatur:nimmo-2026)). Whether tides subsequently heated Io further and
captured it into the Laplace resonance with Europa and Ganymede, or whether this resonance
instead arose primordially through migration within the gas disc, is an open dispute addressed
under [Orbital resonances](thema:resonanzen). The authors of the cited review explicitly note
that later evolution — tides, impacts, and in Io’s case ongoing volcanism — has overprinted the
original signatures of formation, which are today only partly accessible; six open questions are
meant to be addressed by the upcoming JUICE, Europa Clipper and Tianwen-4 missions
([Nimmo et al. 2026](literatur:nimmo-2026)). The broader context of planet formation is presented
under [Formation of the Solar System](thema:entstehung).

## Open questions

- **A magma ocean, or a mostly solid mantle?** The induction response from Galileo’s
  magnetometer data points to a well-conducting, contiguous melt layer near the surface
  ([Khurana et al. 2011](literatur:khurana-2011)); the small measured real part of $k_2$ from the
  Juno flybys instead fits an overwhelmingly solid mantle better and rules out a shallow global
  magma ocean under a crust of at most 50 km, but not a deeper melt zone below about 318 km
  ([Park et al. 2025](literatur:park-2025)). The two findings are therefore better described as
  differently sensitive than as strictly contradictory.
- **Where is the tidal energy dissipated?** A model that places the heating in a global magma
  ocean or a partially molten asthenosphere near the surface predicts a different distribution of
  heat flow across the surface than a model with dissipation in the deeper, solid mantle
  ([Tyler et al. 2015](literatur:tyler-2015)); the small real part of $k_2$ from the Juno
  measurements favours the second possibility
  ([Park et al. 2025](literatur:park-2025)). Which distribution actually best explains the
  observed hot-spot pattern is not conclusively settled.
- **In which direction does the orbit really evolve?** Io’s present inward migration is well
  established astrometrically ([Lainey et al. 2009](literatur:lainey-2009)); whether this is a
  lasting trend or merely one of recurring phases of a long-term limit cycle of coupled orbital
  and thermal evolution is the subject of a model only proposed in 2026 and not yet tested by
  further work ([Bagheri et al. 2026](literatur:bagheri-2026)).

## In the model

- **Orbital reference:** Like the other Jovian moons, Io moves in the `parentEquator` reference
  on Jupiter’s equatorial plane (pole 268.057°/64.495° from [Jupiter](objekt:jupiter)), not on
  its own Laplace plane, which is inclined only 0.019° against it (header comment in
  `jupiter-monde.ts`). The orbital-element table itself lists $i = 0.0^\circ$ and
  $\Omega = 0.0^\circ$ for Io: at vanishing inclination the node is geometrically undefined. A
  check of `positionInParentFrame` confirms that it is indeed inconsequential at $i = 0$ — two
  trial node values (0° and 137.284°) yielded the same position to within
  $3 \cdot 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{km}$, numerical noise. `nodeDot` is therefore set to 0 rather than to
  a value computed from the table’s (here meaningless) node-precession period.
- **Orbital period of the data set:** From $\dot L$ (7,432,433.196280° per century) follows
  $36525/(\dot L/360) = 1.769138$ days — exactly the value with which Io actually orbits in the
  model, and which the fact sheet also gives. The orbital-element table’s own column P instead
  lists 1.762732 days, 0.36 % below that; an independent Kepler calculation with Jupiter’s `GM`
  and Io’s mass gave $1.769905$ days, close to the model value but not exactly equal (0.043 %
  above) — both cross-checks independently confirm that the table’s column P is wrong for Io,
  without adopting the BEFUND comment in `jupiter-monde.ts`. The data block shows the second,
  $GM$-and-mass-based figure (`umlaufzeitTage`), which rounded to one decimal likewise reads
  “1.8 days”, the same as the actually simulated motion.
- **Perijove period:** From $\dot\varpi$ (27,006.7517° per century) follows a period of
  $360/\dot\varpi \times 100 = 1.33$ years; because the sign in the data set is positive rather
  than negative, the perijove advances forward in the model instead of, as theory would predict,
  regressing (figures as in [Orbital resonances](thema:resonanzen)).
- **Rotation ratio:** `rotationPeriodH` (42.45931 h) matches the sidereal period computed from
  $\dot L$ to $-0.047 \cdot 10^{-6}$ relative; the angle between the map centre and the direction
  to Jupiter is 162.8° at the epoch and 163.1° after a hundred years (figures as in
  [Bound rotation](thema:gebundene-rotation)).
- **Axial tilt:** Recomputing with `achsneigungDeg` gives $0.0058^\circ$, essentially zero: both
  the orbital inclination ($i = 0$) and the small offset of Io’s own rotation pole
  (268.05°/64.50°) from Jupiter’s pole contribute to this. The data block rounds this to one
  decimal and therefore shows 0.0°.
- **Eccentricity:** `e` is fixed at 0.004 (`eDot` 0) — close to the eccentricity of 0.0041 forced
  by the resonance.
- **Pole:** 268.05°/64.50° from the IAU rotation elements (SPICE kernel), fixed at the epoch;
  close both to Jupiter’s own pole and to Io’s Laplace pole listed separately in the
  orbital-element table (268.1°/64.5°, tilt 0.0°) — two conceptually different but nearly
  identical directions.
- **Albedo and texture:** The albedo 0.62 is the fact sheet’s geometric albedo, matched exactly.
  The texture is a Galileo/Voyager mosaic from the U.S. Geological Survey (`ASSETS.md`) and is
  normalised to the catalogue albedo like every body’s
  ([Albedo and brightness](thema:photometrie)); volcanic plumes are not included, `appearance` in
  `jupiter-monde.ts` carries only an albedo texture, no separate plume layer. The fallback colour
  (sulphur yellow) remains as a backstop.
- **Shadows:** Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto are simultaneously occluders of Jupiter and of
  one another (`MAX_OKKLUDER` 4 in `render/shadows.ts`); each of the four moons is therefore
  shadowed by Jupiter and the three others. At Jupiter’s 3.12° axial tilt, Io, because of its
  proximity, always casts a shadow, whereas Callisto, because of its greater distance, sometimes
  casts none at all (9.5° versus 2.1° permissible solar elevation above the orbital plane,
  [Eclipses](thema:finsternis)); like Jupiter’s own shadow, this one too carries no umbra colour.
- **Scale:** Io is a satellite (`isSatellite`, its `parent` is Jupiter, not the Sun); its orbital
  radius around Jupiter therefore scales, like its own and Jupiter’s radius, with `sizeScale`
  rather than with the distance compression applied to planetary orbits (`scaledPositionAt`).
  Further simplifications: [Limits of the model](thema:modell).
