# Iapetus

Iapetus is the outermost of the large, roughly spherical moons of [Saturn](objekt:saturn) and,
at 734.5 km in radius, markedly smaller than [Titan](objekt:titan) (2575 km) and somewhat
smaller than Rhea (763.3 km), but larger than Dione, Tethys, Enceladus and Mimas. Two features
make it unique among Saturn's moons: one of the strongest known brightness contrasts between the
two hemispheres of a single body in the Solar System, between its dark, leading hemisphere
(Cassini Regio) and its bright trailing hemisphere, and a mountain ridge up to about 20 km high that runs
for just over 110° of longitude almost exactly along the equator, giving Iapetus its
walnut-like shape. Both features are tied to its large orbital distance of about 3.56 million
km, just over 61 times Saturn's radius: that distance permits a rotation slow enough to produce large
day–night temperature differences, and it places the orbital plane under the Sun's influence
rather than Saturn's equatorial bulge alone. This text covers parameters and measurement,
interior, surface, atmosphere and magnetosphere, orbit, rotation and dynamics, and formation and
evolution, and closes with what Orrery models of it.

## Parameters and measurement

Unlike the inner mid-sized Saturnian moons, no gravity-field measurement from a close,
radio-tracked flyby exists for Iapetus; its mass parameter comes from the much wider-ranging
tracking of ephemeris SAT441
([JPL SSD satellite data](quelle:jpl-satelliten)). Its triaxial shape
$746 \times 746 \times 712\,\mathrm{km}$ comes, as for the other mid-sized Saturnian moons, from
the same photogrammetric control-point network over Cassini images of the prime mission
([Thomas 2010](literatur:thomas-2010)); the volume-equivalent spherical radius from these three
semi-axes is $\sqrt[3]{746 \cdot 746 \cdot 712} = 734.5\,\mathrm{km}$ (derivation) – the value
also carried in the catalog, close to the independently determined
$734.30\,\mathrm{km}$ from radio tracking and the IAU report in the table below.

| Radius (radio tracking/IAU) | $734.30\,\mathrm{km}$ | $2.80\,\mathrm{km}$ | Archinal et al. 2018 | [JPL SSD satellite data](quelle:jpl-satelliten) |

Using the catalog mass ($1.81 \cdot 10^{21}\,\mathrm{kg}$) and catalog radius gives a density of
about $1.09\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ (derivation) – barely more than pure water ice
($0.917\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$) and practically identical to the value derived from radio tracking
and the IAU radius in the table. In a simple two-component model of water ice
($917\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$) and silicate rock ($3000\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$), that corresponds to
a rock mass fraction of only about 8% (derivation, the same simplified model as for
[Rhea](objekt:rhea), Dione and Tethys) – markedly less than for the inner mid-sized moons.
Whether that reflects the actual composition or whether an unusually porous, loosely packed
structure additionally lowers the density cannot be decided without a dedicated gravity-field
measurement (see Interior).

## Interior

Unlike [Rhea](objekt:rhea), Dione or Titan, Iapetus has no measurement from a close,
gravimetrically evaluated flyby; its interior can only be constrained indirectly, through shape
and rotational history. Iapetus rotates synchronously today, in 79.33 days (see Orbit, rotation
and dynamics), yet its shape matches the hydrostatic equilibrium figure of a body rotating with a
period of about 16 hours – nearly 120 times faster than today. This "frozen-in" shape can only be
preserved if a sufficiently thick, rigid lithosphere formed early, before tidal friction slowed
the rotation to today's value over several hundred million years; short-lived radioactive
isotopes — mainly ²⁶Al, to a lesser extent ⁶⁰Fe — supply the necessary early heat (Iapetus
accordingly accreted 2.5 to 5.0 million years after the formation of the calcium–aluminum
inclusions, early enough for significant ²⁶Al heating), without warming the body enough to fully
separate into ice and rock
([Castillo-Rogez et al. 2007](literatur:castillo-rogez-2007)). That fits the overall very low
density, barely above that of pure water ice (see Parameters and measurement): a largely
undifferentiated, ice-rich body without pronounced core–mantle separation is consistent with this
picture, although a reliable statement about the actual degree of differentiation would require a
future gravity-field measurement.

## Surface

The most striking feature is the two-tone coloring: the leading hemisphere, Cassini Regio, has a
bolometric Bond albedo of only $0.06 \pm 0.01$, the trailing hemisphere $0.25 \pm 0.03$
([Blackburn et al. 2011](literatur:blackburn-2011); figures as in
[Albedo and brightness](thema:photometrie)) – one of the strongest known albedo contrasts of a
single body in the Solar System
([Spencer and Denk 2010](literatur:spencer-2010); [Iapetus at NASA Science](quelle:nasa-iapetus)).
In high-resolution images, the brightest material reaches up to
$0.38 \pm 0.04$, because the coarse hemispheric averages include darker individual patches
([Blackburn et al. 2011](literatur:blackburn-2011)). Repeated imaging since 2004 and the single
close flyby on September 10, 2007
([Cassini flyby of Iapetus](quelle:nasa-iapetus-vorbeiflug)), additionally revealed a global
color dichotomy: both the dark
and the bright material of the leading side are noticeably redder than the corresponding material
of the trailing side, and individual craters show darker equator-facing and brighter pole-facing
walls – evidence that the same temperature-controlled water-ice sublimation that shapes the
global contrast also produces local albedo patterns
([Denk et al. 2010](literatur:denk-2010)).

According to current understanding, the contrast begins with dark, exogenic dust that
preferentially lands on the leading side: the distant, retrograde moon Phoebe feeds a huge,
extremely tenuous dust ring extending from at least 128 to 207 Saturn radii (optical depth of
order $2 \cdot 10^{-8}$; [Verbiscer et al. 2009](literatur:verbiscer-2009)). Poynting–Robertson
drag slowly carries its grains inward, and because Iapetus moves prograde through this
retrograde-infalling dust, it predominantly strikes its leading hemisphere; capture-probability
models quantify the effect: 10-µm grains on retrograde orbits are captured almost completely,
1-µm grains at 10 to 40%, while prograde grains are captured only at a few tenths of a percent up
to at most 20–30%; a radar-based estimate of the layer thickness meanwhile found only a few
decimeters to meters instead of the previously assumed kilometers, cutting the total amount of
dark material required to a few $10^{18}\,\mathrm{g}$
([Tosi et al. 2010](literatur:tosi-2010)). The dark areas warm up more – subsolar, the respective
Bond albedos give about 125.5 K on the dark and 118.6 K on the bright side (figures as in
Albedo and brightness) –, water ice preferentially sublimates there and
condenses on the colder, already brighter areas; this self-reinforcing feedback ran for around
2.4 billion years and explains why a comparatively small dust input turned into such an extreme
contrast ([Spencer and Denk 2010](literatur:spencer-2010)).

Along the equator, almost exactly on the zero line, a mountain ridge runs for just over 110° of
longitude, or about 1300 km, with elevations up to 20 km above the surrounding terrain
([Ip 2006](literatur:ip-2006)) – a feature unique in the Solar System (for its origin, see Open
questions).

## Atmosphere and magnetosphere

No atmosphere or exosphere has been detected at Iapetus. Unlike the considerably closer
mid-sized moons – for instance the directly measured oxygen–carbon dioxide exosphere of
[Rhea](objekt:rhea) – no published in-situ measurement exists so far; the single close flyby in
2007 served mainly for imaging and spectroscopy of the surface
([Denk et al. 2010](literatur:denk-2010)). Given Iapetus' low mass and its large distance from
Saturn's magnetospheric activity, only a very thin exosphere would be expected in any case.

## Orbit, rotation and dynamics

Iapetus orbits Saturn in 79.33 days and rotates synchronously (see Interior); its orbit, inclined
by 15.47° to Saturn's equator, is the most strongly tilted of the classical Saturnian moons. The
cause is its large distance: close-in moons are held in Saturn's equatorial plane by its
equatorial bulge, but far out the Sun's pull takes over that role, and the Laplace plane about
which the orbit slowly precesses tips from Saturn's equator toward the ecliptic. Iapetus' own
Laplace plane is inclined by 14.8° to Saturn's equator, and its orbit by 7.6° to that Laplace
plane, with a nodal period of 3130 years; its Laplace radius lies at $59\,R_\mathrm{p}$, well
beyond Saturn's own $48.4\,R_\mathrm{p}$ (figures as in [Orbital elements](thema:bahnelemente);
[Tremaine et al. 2009](literatur:tremaine-2009)) – Iapetus thus sits clearly on the outer,
ecliptic-dominated side of this transition (see
[The tilted orbit of Iapetus](szene:iapetus-schief)).

That a moon orbiting this far out is tidally locked at all is remarkable: the timescale of tidal
despinning grows with the sixth power of the distance
([Tidal locking](thema:gebundene-rotation)), so at Iapetus' nearly 61 Saturn radii it is many
orders of magnitude slower than for the inner moons. Only because the early, radiogenically
heated phase provided enough time (see Interior) could the despinning nonetheless run to
completion. The orientation of the spin axis is likewise a special case: in the classification of
Cassini states, Iapetus could lie in either state 1 or state 2 depending on its actual
flattening, unlike most other moons, for which the assignment is unambiguous (Tidal locking).

## Formation and evolution

Like the other mid-sized Saturnian moons, Iapetus probably formed from Saturn's circumplanetary
disk shortly after the planet itself (for its place among the other origin hypotheses, see
[Formation of the Solar System](thema:entstehung)); its accretion 2.5 to 5.0 million years after
the Solar System's oldest solids was early enough to benefit from the heat of short-lived
radionuclides ([Castillo-Rogez et al. 2007](literatur:castillo-rogez-2007)).

The surface color contrast, by comparison, is not a closed episode but an ongoing development:
the Phoebe ring continually supplies dark, exogenic dust that Poynting–Robertson drag carries to
Iapetus
([Verbiscer et al. 2009](literatur:verbiscer-2009); [Tosi et al. 2010](literatur:tosi-2010)), and
the thermal feedback that shaped today's albedo dichotomy from it ran over a substantial part of
the Solar System's history – how large the original share was remains open (see Open questions)
([Spencer and Denk 2010](literatur:spencer-2010)).

## Open questions

- **How much of today's contrast is primordial deposition, and how much is thermally amplified?**
  Tosi et al. find that the amount of water ice increases with distance from the center of the
  dark region – consistent with thermal segregation, but in their own reading also a possible
  sign of ongoing or only recently completed deposition; the question is not settled either way
  ([Tosi et al. 2010](literatur:tosi-2010)). Spencer and Denk, by contrast, show that a
  comparatively small primordial trigger suffices once the feedback operates over billions of
  years ([Spencer and Denk 2010](literatur:spencer-2010)); how large the original share was
  relative to the amplified one is therefore not separately measurable.
- **How did the equatorial ridge form?** One interpretation sees it as a relic of the early,
  roughly 16-hour rotation, frozen into an already-rigid crust as the body despun
  ([Castillo-Rogez et al. 2007](literatur:castillo-rogez-2007)); another sees it as the collapsed
  remnant of a ring that once orbited within Iapetus' large Hill sphere and settled onto the
  equator – with a predicted repose angle of the deposited debris of about 25° for rounded
  grains up to 45° for snow-like, hail-mixed material
  ([Ip 2006](literatur:ip-2006)). Both scenarios are consistent with the ridge's present position
  close to the equator; neither has been ruled out.
- **Is the ridge material endogenous or infalling?** The rotational-relic hypothesis fundamentally
  requires the body's own, tectonically uplifted crustal material
  ([Castillo-Rogez et al. 2007](literatur:castillo-rogez-2007)), while the ring-collapse
  hypothesis requires infalling debris originally in circum-Iapetian orbit
  ([Ip 2006](literatur:ip-2006)); no geochemical or spectral test distinguishing the two
  possibilities at the ridge itself has been carried out.

## In the model

- **Orbital reference:** Iapetus moves in the `parentEquator` reference with osculating Horizons
  elements at epoch J2000; the inclination in the dataset ($i = 15.47013984^\circ$) is already
  the value actually to be used against Saturn's equator, with no detour via the Laplace pole
  needed (header comment in `saturn-monde.ts`). The dataset nonetheless precesses Iapetus' node
  about Saturn's pole rather than the Laplace pole; that deviates from Horizons by 0.71° in 2050
  and 1.11° in 2076 (figures as in [Orbital elements](thema:bahnelemente)).
- **Orbital period:** From `LDot` follows $36\,525 \cdot 360/165\,748.568258 = 79.331$ days (worked
  out from the code), practically identical to the sidereal period of the JPL mean-elements table
  (79.331002 days) – this is the starting point for the orbital and angular figures of the scene
  [The tilted orbit of Iapetus](szene:iapetus-schief). The data panel instead shows the Kepler
  orbital period computed from the semi-major axis and masses (`umlaufzeitTage`), about 0.085%
  longer. The apsidal and nodal periods (1662.900 and 3130.302 years respectively, header comment
  in `saturn-monde.ts`) give, by the same formula used for the other Saturnian moons, the rates
  carried in the dataset of $10.1484^\circ$ and $-11.5005^\circ$ per century respectively (worked
  out from the code).
- **Rotation and pole:** `rotationPeriodH` (1903.94405 h) matches $360^\circ/\dot L$ to
  $0.001 \cdot 10^{-6}$ relative – even tighter than for the other moons in this stage, the tidal
  lock here is coupled to the orbital period down to practically the last decimal place given.
  Iapetus' pole ($318.16^\circ /
  75.03^\circ$) is, unlike the four inner mid-sized moons, its own, individually measured dataset
  value and does not follow Saturn's pole.
- **Albedo and texture:** The catalog albedo of 0.275 is the arithmetic mean of the fact-sheet
  hemisphere values 0.05 and 0.5 and is used as a single scalar for lighting; the real two-tone
  pattern shows only in the texture, whose hemisphere means are 0.053 around the darkest viewing
  direction and 0.373 around the brightest (figures as in
  [Albedo and brightness](thema:photometrie)).
- **Never an occluder:** `waehleOkkluder` chooses, for Saturn, the four moons with the largest
  displayed angular radius as sphere occluders (`MAX_OKKLUDER` 4): Titan, Tethys, Dione and Rhea.
  Iapetus' own angular radius as seen from Saturn, given its large distance, is only
  $\arcsin(734.5/3\,562\,568.1) \approx 0.012^\circ$ (worked out from the code) – well below that
  of the four chosen moons and even below the Sun's mean angular size as seen from Saturn
  ($0.028^\circ$) –, which is why Iapetus never casts a shadow on Saturn in the model, even
  though real solar elevations would occasionally allow it (figures as in
  [Eclipses](thema:finsternis)).
- **Scale:** Iapetus is a satellite (`isSatellite`, with `parent` set to Saturn); its orbital
  radius therefore scales, like its own and Saturn's radius, with `sizeScale`, not with the
  distance compression applied to the planetary orbits.
- **Data panel:** The mass carried in the dataset ($1.81 \cdot 10^{21}\,\mathrm{kg}$), multiplied
  by $G$ per CODATA 2018, gives a $GM$ of $120.80\,\mathrm{km^3\,s^{-2}}$, about 2404 ppm above
  the value measured by radio tracking in the table above – of the same order as for
  [Rhea](objekt:rhea) (+1524 ppm) and Dione (+4120 ppm). Further simplifications:
  [Limits of the model](thema:modell).
