# Tethys

At 530.6 km in radius, Tethys is the fifth-largest of Saturn's seven large moons and, close
to pure water ice, one of the least dense large moons in the Solar System. The huge
impact crater Odysseus and the trough system Ithaca Chasma, which wraps around it almost
concentrically, dominate the surface; whether the two are linked or formed independently
remains an open question. With [Mimas](objekt:mimas), Tethys is locked in a 4:2
mean-motion resonance, and on its own orbit it is accompanied by the far smaller
Lagrange moons Telesto and Calypso, 60° ahead and behind. This text presents parameters
and measurement, interior, surface, atmosphere and magnetosphere, and orbit, rotation and
formation, and finally describes what Orrery renders of it.

## Parameters and measurement

Cassini's radio tracking (ephemeris SAT441) yields Tethys's mass parameter directly from
the Doppler shift of the spacecraft's radio signal; combined with the volume derived
from Cassini images, this gives a mean density of $0.9840 \pm 0.0033\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$
– barely more than that of water ice ($0.917\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$)
([JPL SSD satellite data](quelle:jpl-satelliten)). Tethys is markedly triaxial:
$538 \times 528 \times 526\,\mathrm{km}$, derived from the same photogrammetric
control-point network built from Cassini images of the nominal mission used for the
other mid-sized Saturn moons ([Thomas 2010](literatur:thomas-2010)); the
volume-equivalent spherical radius from these three semi-axes is 530.6 km – the value
the catalog also carries.

At around $984\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, Tethys is among the least dense large moons in the
Solar System, well below Mimas's ($1150\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$) and
[Dione's](objekt:dione) ($1485\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$) denser interiors
([Ćuk et al. 2024](literatur:cuk-2024)); classification alongside all Saturn moons under
[Interior structure](thema:innerer-aufbau).

## Interior

The low density leaves little room for a significant rock fraction in Tethys: if the
moon were a homogeneous mix of water ice ($917\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$) and silicate rock
(around $3000\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$), the measured density would imply a rock mass
fraction of only about one tenth. Unlike [Enceladus](objekt:enceladus) or Rhea, no close
Cassini flyby with radio-Doppler tracking has ever measured Tethys's gravity field
directly – a secure moment-of-inertia model has therefore remained unavailable
([Gyalay and Nimmo 2023](literatur:gyalay-2023)). A 2023 analysis of Tethys's
long-wavelength topography works around this gap: assuming that variations in tidal
heating show up as variations in ice-shell thickness or temperature and thus in surface
shape, the best-fitting models require Pratt isostasy and a heating pattern following
tides raised by Tethys's orbital inclination; they yield a normalized moment of inertia
of 0.340 to 0.345 – only a little below the value 0.4 of a homogeneous body – and a
present-day mean surface heat flux of just 1 to 2 mW m⁻². The best-fitting model
therefore shows no ocean today ([Gyalay and Nimmo 2023](literatur:gyalay-2023)).

This does not rule out an earlier ocean (see Formation and evolution); it only means
that Tethys's interior today, unlike the active Enceladus's or Mimas's presumably still
young one, appears cold and largely frozen. More on the core and moment-of-inertia
factor for moons in general under [Interior structure](thema:innerer-aufbau).

## Surface

Tethys's most striking feature is the impact crater Odysseus: 445 km in diameter, a good
two fifths of Tethys's own diameter (1061 km), with a strongly subsided, flat floor – a
sign that the ice shell was less able to bear load at the time of impact than the
present, densely cratered surface would suggest
([Moore et al. 2004](literatur:moore-2004)). Almost concentric with Odysseus runs Ithaca
Chasma, a trough system roughly 2000 km long and up to 100 km wide that wraps around
nearly three quarters of the moon's circumference
([Giese et al. 2007](literatur:giese-2007)). Whether Chasma and crater are causally
linked or formed independently is an open question (see Open questions).

Away from these two features, Tethys shows an old, densely cratered landscape typical of
the inner Saturn moons; crater statistics across the whole moon family are placed in
context under [Interior structure](thema:innerer-aufbau).

## Atmosphere and magnetosphere

Tethys carries no atmosphere of its own; its small mass and low surface temperature rule
out a bound gas envelope. The moon lies within [Saturn's](objekt:saturn) magnetosphere
and E ring, which spans roughly 180,000 to 480,000 km – Tethys's orbital radius of
295,000 km places it within that span closer to [Enceladus](objekt:enceladus), the
ring's source, than to its outer edge. Unlike the active Enceladus, Tethys feeds no ring
material of its own; its contribution to the system stays purely gravitational (see
Orbit, rotation and dynamics).

## Orbit, rotation and dynamics

Tethys orbits Saturn in 1.888 days (figures as in
[Saturn's rings edge-on](szene:saturn-ringkante)), on a nearly circular orbit
($e = 0.00097$ – smaller only for Rhea, $e = 0.00080$, among all the large Saturn
moons), and rotates in a bound state. On its own orbit it is accompanied by the far
smaller Lagrange moons Telesto (at the leading Lagrange point L4, 60° ahead) and Calypso
(L5, 60° behind), which Orrery's catalog does not render separately; the same pattern is
shown by [Dione](objekt:dione) with Helene and Polydeuces.

With Mimas, Tethys is locked in a second-order 4:2 mean-motion resonance: the angle
$2\lambda_\mathrm{Mi} - 4\lambda_\mathrm{Te} + \Omega_\mathrm{Mi} + \Omega_\mathrm{Te}$
librates around 0° with an amplitude of 95° and a period of 70 years; because Tethys is
about sixteen times more massive than Mimas, the resonance raises essentially only
Mimas's orbital inclination, not that of Tethys
([Ćuk et al. 2024](literatur:cuk-2024); derivation alongside every orbital resonance in
the Solar System under [Orbital resonances](thema:resonanzen)). Unlike Enceladus and
Dione, Tethys is not locked with any other large Saturn moon in a presently active
eccentricity resonance; its own orbit is practically circular.

## Formation and evolution

Like the other mid-sized Saturn moons, Tethys probably formed out of the Saturn disc
shortly after the planet itself (classification alongside the other origin hypotheses
for the Saturn moons under [Formation of the Solar System](thema:entstehung)). Crater
counts on the flanks of Ithaca Chasma date its formation to roughly 4 billion years ago
and thus slightly before Odysseus – a finding that complicates the idea that the chasma
is a direct consequence of the impact, as first proposed
([Moore et al. 2004](literatur:moore-2004); age from
[Giese et al. 2007](literatur:giese-2007)). A model of the chasma's flexure requires, at
the time of formation, an elastic lithospheric thickness of only 5 to 7 km (mechanically
16 to 20 km) and a heat flux of 18 to 30 mW m⁻² – far above the present-day value of 1
to 2 mW m⁻² estimated from topography (see Interior)
([Giese et al. 2007](literatur:giese-2007)).

A possible trigger for this elevated early heating is a now-defunct 3:2 mean-motion
resonance between Tethys and Dione: a forced eccentricity of between 0.001 and 0.02 –
depending on the assumed internal structure – would have sufficed to heat Tethys via
tidal friction enough to form an ocean; its later freezing would have generated
expansion stresses of 1 to 2 MPa consistent with the observed flexure
([Chen and Nimmo 2008](literatur:chen-2008)). This reading remains compatible with the
frozen, ocean-free picture from the topography study today, but replaces one unresolved
earlier resonance with another assumption that likewise lacks independent confirmation
(see Open questions).

## Open questions

- **Is Ithaca Chasma linked to the Odysseus impact, or did it form from the freezing of
  an early ocean?** The original reading treated the chasma as a direct consequence of
  the impact ([Moore et al. 2004](literatur:moore-2004)); that crater counts date the
  chasma slightly older than Odysseus argues instead for independent events rather than
  a shared origin ([Giese et al. 2007](literatur:giese-2007)). The alternative – an
  ocean layer heated by a past Tethys–Dione resonance and later frozen – matches the
  measured flexure numerically, but requires a resonance that can only be inferred
  backward from today's picture, not demonstrated directly
  ([Chen and Nimmo 2008](literatur:chen-2008)).
- **How dense, and how strongly differentiated, is Tethys's core really?** Without a
  moment of inertia measured from the gravity field, every statement about the core
  rests on a topography model with an assumed isostasy and heating pattern; the
  normalized moment of inertia of 0.340 to 0.345 is a model result, not a direct
  measurement ([Gyalay and Nimmo 2023](literatur:gyalay-2023)).

## In the model

- **Orbital reference:** Like the other Saturn moons, Tethys moves in the
  `parentEquator` reference on [Saturn's](objekt:saturn) equatorial plane, with
  osculating Horizons elements at epoch J2000 ($a = 294\,980.26\,\mathrm{km}$,
  $e = 0.00097$) – figures and convention as under
  [Orbital elements](thema:bahnelemente).
- **Node and apsidal precession:** `nodeDot` ($-7226.0136^\circ$ per century) gives a
  retrograde nodal period of 4.982 years. From `lpDot` ($+7\,192\,773.9864^\circ$ per
  century) follows an orbital period for the longitude of pericentre of 0.005 years,
  around 1.826 days – shorter than Tethys's own orbit (worked out from the code); the
  header comment in `saturn-monde.ts` attributes this strikingly short period to
  Tethys's extremely small free eccentricity, a connection not independently verified
  here.
- **Orbital period:** From `LDot` follows $360^\circ/\dot L = 1.8878$ days, adopted as in
  [Saturn's rings edge-on](szene:saturn-ringkante). The data panel instead shows the
  Kepler orbital period computed from the semi-major axis and the masses
  (`umlaufzeitTage`), $1.8917$ days, about 0.21 % longer. `rotationPeriodH`
  ($45.30725\,\mathrm{h}$) matches $360^\circ/\dot L$ to $0.044 \cdot 10^{-6}$ relative;
  the angle between the map centre and the direction to Saturn stands at $140.8^\circ$
  at epoch and at $141.0^\circ$ after a hundred years (the same figures as under
  [Bound rotation](thema:gebundene-rotation); an independent recalculation confirms the
  first figure).
- **Pole:** $40.66^\circ / 83.52^\circ$ from the IAU rotation elements – identical to
  the data sets for Mimas, Enceladus and Dione, because the underlying SPICE kernel
  gives no individual precession for any of the four, only a shared pole fixed at epoch
  ([Archinal et al. 2018](literatur:archinal-2018)).
- **Albedo and texture:** The catalog albedo of 0.8 is the fact sheet's geometric
  albedo; the texture is a seamless Cassini mosaic with no unlit areas (`ASSETS.md`).
- **Shadow:** `waehleOkkluder` picks Saturn's four moons with the largest rendered
  angular radius as spherical occluders: Titan, Tethys, Dione and Rhea – unlike Mimas,
  Tethys does cast a shadow on Saturn in the model. Its real shadow window is
  $\arcsin(R_\mathrm{Saturn}/a_\mathrm{Tethys}) \approx 11.4^\circ$, well under Saturn's
  $26.7^\circ$ axial tilt (figures as in [Eclipses](thema:finsternis)); seen from Tethys
  itself, Saturn is always used as the occluder.
- **No ring relevance:** Saturn's ring in the model is a single, static textured disc
  with no density profile (`render/rings.ts`); Tethys's real position within the E ring
  (see Atmosphere and magnetosphere) has no counterpart there, and no moon can cast a
  shadow on the ring in the model.
- **Scale:** Tethys is a satellite (`isSatellite`, its `parent` is Saturn, not the Sun);
  its orbital radius therefore scales, like its own and Saturn's radius, with
  `sizeScale`, not with the distance compression applied to the planetary orbits.
- **Data panel:** The mass carried in the data set ($6.18 \cdot 10^{20}\,\mathrm{kg}$)
  gives, times $G$ per CODATA 2018, a $GM$ of $41.2472\,\mathrm{km^3\,s^{-2}}$, about
  816 ppm (0.08 %) above the value measured by radio tracking in the table above –
  markedly less than for Mimas (1.04 %), on the order of Enceladus. Further
  simplifications: [Limits of the model](thema:modell).
