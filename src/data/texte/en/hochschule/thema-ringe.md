# Ring systems

All four giant planets carry rings, but only [Saturn](objekt:saturn)'s are bright and massive enough
to stand out even in a small telescope ([Esposito 2010](literatur:esposito-2010);
[Cuzzi et al. 2010](literatur:cuzzi-2010)). This text covers the structure, particles and dynamics of
the Saturn system, its mass and the associated dispute over age and origin, its photometry and the
ring-plane crossings, and sets it against the rings of Uranus, Neptune and Jupiter and the rings of
small bodies beyond the giant planets discovered only in the last decade. Saturn itself, its
gravity-field and rotation measurements, orbit and formation have their own text; tides and the Roche
limit, ring seismology and photometry are covered in depth under
[Tides](thema:gezeiten), [Interior structure](thema:innerer-aufbau) and
[Albedo and brightness](thema:photometrie).

## Structure of the Saturn system

Moving outward, the faint D ring is followed by the denser C, B and A rings, separated by the Cassini
Division, and further out by the narrow F and G rings and the extended, extremely tenuous E ring.
Radius and normal optical depth $\tau$ – the fraction of radially transmitted starlight that is
absorbed or scattered on passing through the ring plane – grow and shrink across several orders of
magnitude:

| Ring/gap | Radius (km) | Optical depth $\tau$ |
|---|---|---|
| D ring | 66,900–74,491 | $10^{-5}$ to $10^{-3}$ |
| C ring | 74,491–91,975 | $0.05$ to $0.35$ |
| B ring | 91,975–117,570 | $0.4$ to $5$ |
| Cassini Division | 117,500–122,050 | $0$ to $0.2$ |
| A ring | 122,050–136,770 | $0.4$ to $1$ |
| Encke gap | 133,423–133,745 | $\approx 0$ |
| Keeler gap | 136,487–136,522 | $\approx 0$ |
| F ring | 139,826–140,612 | $0.01$ to $0.2$ |
| G ring | 166,000–173,200 | $\approx 10^{-6}$ |
| E ring | 180,000–480,000 | $\approx 5 \cdot 10^{-6}$ |

(all values [PDS Ring-Moon Systems Node](quelle:pds-rings)). Within the Cassini Division lie several
nearly empty gaps (Huygens, Herschel, Russell, Jeffreys, Kuiper, Laplace, Bessel, Barnard), of which
Huygens (optical depth 1 to 2) and Laplace (optical depth near 1) carry narrow, markedly denser
ringlets than the other, nearly empty gaps (optical depth near 0 to 0.1). The E
ring is continually fed by the ice plumes of [Enceladus](objekt:enceladus), whose particles are what
turn it into the extended, fine dust veil it forms today
([Cassini mission](quelle:nasa-cassini)). A flight through the dense rings at particle scale is shown
in the scene [Ring flythrough](szene:ringdurchflug).

## Particles, thickness and self-gravity wakes

The rings consist, by most estimates, of more than 95% water ice overall
([Esposito 2010](literatur:esposito-2010); [Cuzzi et al. 2010](literatur:cuzzi-2010)); how strongly
they are contaminated by non-icy material depends strongly on the ring. The optically thin,
low-mass C ring shows, in Cassini microwave radiometry, mostly 1 to 2%, and in the middle C ring up
to 11%, non-icy content at a particle porosity of 70 to 75%
([Zhang et al. 2017a](literatur:zhang-2017a)); the same method, applied to the denser B ring and the
Cassini Division, yields a radially varying exposure age from this – the more mass a ring has, the
more it dilutes infalling foreign material and the "younger" it appears
([Zhang et al. 2017b](literatur:zhang-2017b)). The particles themselves range from dust-grain size up
to a few metres; most of the ring mass sits in a continuum from about 1 cm to 10 m with a comparatively
shallow power-law index $q \approx 2.75$ ([Tiscareno 2013](literatur:tiscareno-2013)). Despite a
diameter of more than 270,000 km, the main rings are only about ten metres thick
([Cassini mission](quelle:nasa-cassini)): inelastic collisions damp any motion perpendicular to the
ring plane until only a wafer-thin disk remains.

Within this disk, the particles' own gravity balances the differential rotation: Cassini UVIS stellar
occultations of the dense A and B rings reveal elongated clumps within them, tilted slightly against
the direction of motion – self-gravity wakes on the metre-to-tens-of-metres scale, whose orientation
and spacing are set by the very shear that creates them
([Colwell et al. 2007](literatur:colwell-2007)). Because such wakes block ring light to different
degrees depending on viewing direction, the ring's apparent brightness depends on the opening angle
to Earth – an azimuthal asymmetry that was only understood once self-gravity wakes had been
identified as its cause.

## Dynamics: shear, resonances, shepherd moons and propellers

Every ring particle orbits Saturn on its own Keplerian orbit, faster on the inside than on the
outside; this differential shear is the basic force that keeps the rings flat and continually pulls
them apart, while collisions and viscosity transport angular momentum outward and mass inward,
slowly spreading the rings radially. Where a moon perturbs a particle in resonance – say, in a 2:1
orbital ratio – that perturbation grows into a standing density or bending wave that propagates
outward as a spiral from the resonance location; unlike the orbital resonances among Saturn's moons
themselves (see [Resonances](thema:resonanzen)), such a Lindblad resonance needs no second orbit of
comparable mass, a single moon suffices. The 2:1 resonance with [Mimas](objekt:mimas) coincides with
the outer edge of the B ring and hence the inner edge of the Cassini Division: there it clears the
material almost completely and marks the sharpest edge in the whole system
([Esposito 2010](literatur:esposito-2010); [Cuzzi et al. 2010](literatur:cuzzi-2010)).

Small moons embedded in gaps shape the rings directly: Pan keeps the Encke gap in the A ring open,
Daphnis, on an orbit inclined against the ring plane, keeps the narrower Keeler gap open with an edge
its passage visibly ripples, and Prometheus and Pandora, as shepherd moons, confine the narrow,
eccentric F ring from both sides ([Porco et al. 2007](literatur:porco-2007)). Moons too small to clear
a gap of their own leave instead a characteristic propeller-shaped pattern of two adjacent brighter
streaks; the first such objects found, in the middle A ring, had estimated diameters around 100 m
([Tiscareno et al. 2006](literatur:tiscareno-2006)) – too small ever to be imaged individually, but
large enough to produce their own perturbation.

## The Roche limit and ring moons

Inside the Roche limit, a planet's tidal forces outweigh the self-attraction of loose material;
almost all dense planetary rings therefore lie within this limit, whose location depends on the
density of the material. For Saturn it is given, with the applicable numbers and their derivation,
under [Tides](thema:gezeiten): about 129,000 km for compact ice, considerably farther out for the
loose, porous particles of the small ring moons and of the outer ring particles themselves. No moon
can permanently coalesce within this limit; ring moons such as Pan and Daphnis, which orbit within
the rings themselves, are more likely to be grown than shrunk bodies – aggregates that could only
compact themselves up to their own critical Roche density
([Porco et al. 2007](literatur:porco-2007)).

## Ring seismology

Cassini's ring observations do not just yield the rings' own properties; they also let Saturn's
interior be heard: stellar occultations in the C ring reveal, at particular radii, faint,
wave-like density disturbances excited by the planet's own oscillations, visible where the ring
particles' orbital frequency stands in Lindblad resonance with the given oscillation frequency;
fundamental modes (f-modes) as well as buoyancy-driven gravity waves (g-modes)
([Mankovich and Fuller 2021](literatur:mankovich-2021)) leave their signature in the ring this way,
long before any direct measurement of the planetary interior would be possible
([Hedman and Nicholson 2013](literatur:hedman-2013)). What this "kronoseismology" reveals about
Saturn's dilute core and its moment of inertia is covered under
[Interior structure](thema:innerer-aufbau).

## Mass, age and origin

Cassini's Grand Finale tracking, supplemented by close-up images of the rings from a few thousand
kilometres' altitude during the same 22 orbits
([Tiscareno et al. 2019](literatur:tiscareno-2019)), gave the rings a total mass of only
$(1.54 \pm 0.49) \cdot 10^{19}\,\mathrm{kg}$, about 0.41 times the mass of Mimas
([Iess et al. 2019](literatur:iess-2019); more under [Saturn](objekt:saturn)) – markedly less than
older models expected for rings as old as the planet. An independent method confirms the "young"
finding: from the flux of exogenous micrometeoroids that continually contaminate the rings with
non-icy material, and from the measured, still-low contamination of the dense rings, follows an
exposure time of no more than a few hundred million years
([Kempf et al. 2023](literatur:kempf-2023)). A critical counter-view, however, holds that neither the
low mass nor the low contamination alone proves that the rings themselves are young: both quantities
change through viscous spreading and ongoing mass loss, so that an originally far more massive, old
ring could have arrived at today's "young"-looking values over time, if the model assumptions about
viscosity and loss rate are chosen differently ([Crida et al. 2019](literatur:crida-2019)). The
dispute thus remains unsettled despite consistent individual measurements (see "Open questions").

Three origin hypotheses are on offer, none of them uncontested. First, the rings could stem from the
tidal disruption of a differentiated, Titan-sized moon that lost its icy shell while migrating
inward toward Saturn, while its rocky core plunged into the planet – a model that explains pure ice
but requires a particular, early migration history ([Canup 2010](literatur:canup-2010)). Second, a
single, large Kuiper belt object could have passed close enough to Saturn (or Uranus) to be torn
apart by tidal force; simulations show that 0.1 to 10% of the passing body's mass is captured in the
process and can form a massive ice ring, plausibly during the Late Heavy Bombardment about
3.8 billion years ago ([Hyodo et al. 2017](literatur:hyodo-2017)). Third, the breakup of a mid-sized
icy moon already orbiting within the Saturn system, in an impact – simulations point to a
comet-sized body – could be markedly younger, within a few hundred million years
([Dubinski 2019](literatur:dubinski-2019)). A fourth, quite differently framed explanation ties the
rings' formation to Saturn's obliquity itself: a now-destroyed moon, "Chrysalis", would have held
Saturn in a spin–orbit resonance with Neptune for a long time, until its breakup around
100 million years ago produced both the axis's present tilt and the rings (details in the text on
Saturn).

## Photometry of the rings

As with any rough surface, the rings' brightness is shaped by the illumination geometry, including
an opposition effect near zero phase angle (see [Albedo and brightness](thema:photometrie) for its
general derivation). A second, purely geometric effect is added for rings: when the Sun, as seen
from the camera, stands almost exactly behind the ring plane, the optically thinner, dustier regions
light up in backlight, because small particles preferentially scatter light forward. Cassini's
mosaic "In Saturn's Shadow" from 15 September 2006, taken during a twelve-hour solar eclipse behind
Saturn, shows this effect in full clarity: alongside the familiar main rings it reveals the faint G
ring and the even fainter E ring, and among them a pale point of light – Earth, from more than a
billion kilometres away ([Photojournal Saturn](quelle:jpl-photojournal-saturn)). A view of the
illuminated edge of the rings in grazing light is shown in the scene
[Rings in grazing light](szene:saturn-streiflicht).

Seen from Earth, the visible ring opening changes with Saturn's own year, because the ring plane and
the equator coincide: twice per orbit, near the equinoxes, the ring appears exactly edge-on. The
edge-on crossing of May and November 1995 was used by the Hubble Space Telescope for spectroscopic
stellar occultations that bounded the F ring's apparent thickness to only 1.2 to 1.5 km and traced
the E ring out to about 15,000 km
([Nicholson et al. 1996](literatur:nicholson-1996)); similar edge-on crossings recurred in 2009 and,
most recently, on 23 March 2025. Seen edge-on, the main rings nearly vanish for an observer on Earth,
while fine structures rising out of the plane – such as the rim of the Keeler gap rippled by Daphnis
– become most conspicuous exactly then. The edge-on crossing itself is shown in the scene
[Saturn's rings edge-on](szene:saturn-ringkante).

## The rings of the other giant planets

**Uranus.** When a star was due to be occulted by Uranus on 10 March 1977, five short, symmetrically
arranged dimmings of the starlight already blocked it minutes before and after the actual
occultation: the chance discovery of at least five narrow, dense rings, named α, β, γ, δ and ε
([Elliot et al. 1977](literatur:elliot-1977)). [Uranus](objekt:uranus)'s rings are narrow, dark, and,
unlike Saturn's, lie exactly in the planet's equatorial plane, which is itself steeply tilted against
its orbit; the outermost and by far the densest, the ε ring, is flanked by the small moons Cordelia
and Ophelia as shepherd moons. Only with deep Hubble images did two further, very diffuse outer dust
rings, ν and μ, turn up in 2003 to 2005, the outer one sharing its orbit with the small moon Mab
([Showalter and Lissauer 2006](literatur:showalter-2006)) – roughly twice as far out as the nine
originally discovered main rings. How strongly tilted the whole system appears from Earth is shown
in the scene [Uranus tilted](szene:uranus-gekippt).

**Neptune.** Voyager 2 confirmed complete, but extremely faint, rings around
[Neptune](objekt:neptune) in 1989, after Earth-based stellar occultations in the 1980s had at first
suggested only incomplete "arcs". In the outermost, the Adams ring, material is in fact concentrated
in several short, dense arcs rather than a uniform ring; a 42:43 corotation resonance with the small
moon Galatea, which orbits just inside the ring, was originally proposed to explain their tight
azimuthal clustering, while a measured radial wave running through the arcs, with an amplitude of
about 30 km, shows that the pattern is not entirely rigid but slowly evolving
([Porco 1991](literatur:porco-1991)); how well the simple resonance actually holds the arcs together
has been disputed ever since (see "Open questions").

**Jupiter.** [Jupiter](objekt:jupiter)'s rings consist, unlike those of the three other giant
planets, mostly of fine, water-free dust rather than larger ice particles. Their source is the four
small inner moons Metis, Adrastea, Amalthea and Thebe: impacts of interplanetary micrometeoroids,
accelerated to high speed by Jupiter's strong gravity, knock dust off their surfaces; the inner main
ring is fed chiefly by Metis and Adrastea, the two outer, much fainter "gossamer" rings each by dust
from Amalthea and Thebe, along their orbits slightly inclined to Jupiter's equator
([Burns et al. 1999](literatur:burns-1999)).

## Rings of small bodies beyond the giant planets

Only since the 2010s has it been known that small bodies beyond the giant planets can carry rings
too. A
multiply observed stellar occultation discovered, in 2013, around the Centaur (10199) Chariklo –
with a radius of about 124 km itself barely larger than a mid-sized moon – two narrow, dense rings at
391 and 405 km distance, 7 and 3 km wide respectively, with optical depths around 0.4 and 0.06 – the
first ring ever found around a body of this size class
([Braga-Ribas et al. 2014](literatur:braga-ribas-2014)). In 2017, again by stellar occultation,
followed a roughly 70-km-wide, dense ring at about 2287 km distance around the dwarf planet
[Haumea](objekt:haumea), in the plane of its equator and its largest moon
([Ortiz et al. 2017](literatur:ortiz-2017)). The most unusual case is the ring discovered in 2023
around the trans-Neptunian object Quaoar: it lies at about 4100 km distance, 7.4 times the Quaoar
radius, well outside the Roche limit that applies for compact material (see
[Tides](thema:gezeiten)) – laboratory experiments on inelastic collisions show that a ring can
persist there if its particles retain little energy on impact, so that despite too weak a tidal
force no moon forms from it ([Morgado et al. 2023](literatur:morgado-2023)). Why this ring has
nonetheless not long since clumped into a moon is among the open questions below.

## Open questions

- **Age and origin of Saturn's rings:** the low mass measured in the Grand Finale
  ([Iess et al. 2019](literatur:iess-2019)) and the short exposure time inferred from the
  micrometeoroid flux ([Kempf et al. 2023](literatur:kempf-2023)) argue for young rings, yet a
  critical counter-calculation shows that viscous spreading and ongoing mass loss could also have
  brought an originally old, massive ring to today's values
  ([Crida et al. 2019](literatur:crida-2019)); which of the three or four origin hypotheses is
  correct – torn moon, Kuiper belt object, more recent collision, or Chrysalis – remains open for
  want of further independent tests.
- **Cause of the arcs in the Adams ring:** the corotation resonance with Galatea originally proposed
  was meant to explain why material stays concentrated at certain longitudes
  ([Porco 1991](literatur:porco-1991)); whether this simple resonance model actually accounts for
  the arcs' observed position and extent, which have measurably changed since their discovery, or
  whether other mechanisms are needed, is open.
- **Stability of the Quaoar ring outside the Roche limit:** inelastic collisions can, according to
  current models, sustain a ring where a moon should classically long since have formed
  ([Morgado et al. 2023](literatur:morgado-2023)); whether the same principle also applies to
  Chariklo and Haumea, or whether other mechanisms are at work there, is not conclusively settled.

## In the model

Saturn's ring is, in the model, a single flat disk from 74,658 to 136,780 km
([Saturn](objekt:saturn)), drawn with `ringGeometrieDaten` (128 segments, radial UV coordinate from
the inner to the outer edge) and aligned to the pole (`ringAusrichtung`); the D, F, G and E rings,
any thickness, any individual particle and any gap other than those of the texture strip are absent,
as are density waves and self-gravity waves. The visible structure carries an image file (see
`ASSETS.md`): its alpha channel supplies the radial banding as opacity, its RGB the colour. A
calculation in the scratchpad gave, for the unlinearised RGB mean over the whole file, about 0.146
linear – the code comment "about 0.15 linear" thus does match the mean of the actual file, even
though Saturn's real ring albedo, at 0.5 to 0.6, lies far above it; the alpha-weighted mean (only the
actually visible, opaque image regions) is somewhat lower still, at about 0.125.

The brightness of a ring fragment (`ringHelligkeit`) results from three terms multiplied by the
texture's albedo value: the direct light (ring normal against the sun direction, taken on both sides
by its absolute value, because a ring has no back face) and the forward scattering in backlight, both
divided by $\pi$ (Lambert weighting, see [Albedo and brightness](thema:photometrie)), plus the
unweighted, artistically set night-side fill without this factor. The forward scattering in
backlight

$$s(\gamma) = 0.85\,\max\left(0,-\cos\gamma\right)^{6}$$

contributes ($\gamma$ the angle between the viewing and the sun direction, `RING_STREUUNG` 0.85,
`RING_SCHAERFE` 6): the term reaches its maximum only in exact backlight ($\gamma = 180^\circ$),
falls to half at $\gamma \approx 153^\circ$ (about 27° from the exact backlit direction), and to a
hundredth at $\gamma \approx 118^\circ$ – a narrow, but not extremely tight, brightening cone, as seen
in Cassini's backlit images. Under default settings (brightness 1, night side 0.25) and Saturn's
semi-major axis, a calculation in the scratchpad gives, for a ring point under everyday illumination
(no backlight), a direct-light share of 53% against 47% night-side fill; near exact backlight this
ratio reverses, and scattering alone contributes up to 44% of the total brightness. The planet's
shadow on its own ring (`RingOkkluder`) removes only the direct light entirely and leaves
`RING_SCHATTEN_RESTLICHT` (0.3) of the night-side fill standing as planetshine (see the text on
Saturn); tracing this back with `ringTreffer` further shows that geometry alone – without any further
model – produces a pronounced seasonal effect: at today's small solar elevation above the ring plane
(about −7.5° on 21 September 2026), the ring's shadow on the planet lies as a narrow band a few
degrees wide near the equator, whereas at the greatest tilt near the solstice (26.7°, the same as the
obliquity itself) it instead reaches as a broad band all the way to the winter polar cap – just as
Cassini images of the ring shadow migrating across the cloud tops showed between 2004 and 2017
([Photojournal Saturn](quelle:jpl-photojournal-saturn)), even though the model has no clouds, only a
textured sphere. This interaction is known neither to the shadow model for sphere occluders nor
otherwise to [Eclipses](thema:finsternis), because only the ring, as an occluder, is a plane rather
than a sphere.

Uranus's rings, by contrast, are not an image but a calculation from measured data
(`ringProfil.ts`): each narrow band gets a display width made of a 260-km base plus six times its
real width, each diffuse component a capped, twelvefold-amplified opacity of at most 0.12; the
physical opacity of the narrow rings instead follows $1 - e^{-\tau}$ and ranges from 10% for the λ
ring to 78% for the dense ε ring. Neptune and Jupiter appear without any rings at all. The data
block shows no values of its own for any ring system. Further simplifications:
[Limits of the model](thema:modell).
