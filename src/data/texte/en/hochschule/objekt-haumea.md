# Haumea

Haumea is the most strongly deformed of the four transneptunian dwarf planets recognized by the
International Astronomical Union, and carries the fastest reliably measured rotation period of any
known body above roughly 100 km in diameter (see Parameters and measurement). Observations from
2003/2004 led to its discovery in 2005; it carries the name of the Hawaiian goddess of fertility,
and its moons Hiʻiaka and Namaka, found in 2005, the names of her mythological daughters
([Haumea at NASA](quelle:nasa-haumea)). No spacecraft has ever visited Haumea, and everything below
rests on lightcurves, stellar occultations, thermal and spectroscopic remote sensing, and the
orbits of these two moons. This text presents parameters, interior, surface, orbit
and formation, and closes with what Orrery models of all this — above all, that the simulation shows
Haumea as a sphere rather than the strongly elongated ellipsoid the measurements reveal. Context
among the other dwarf planets appears under [Dwarf planets](thema:zwergplaneten); Haumea's ring
against Chariklo, Quaoar and Saturn's rings under [Ring systems](thema:ringe).

## Parameters and measurement

A first, unambiguous double-peaked lightcurve gave a rotation period of
$3.9154 \pm 0.0002\,\mathrm{h}$ — at the time already the fastest reliably determined spin of any
solar system body above about 100 km ([Rabinowitz et al. 2006](literatur:rabinowitz-2006)); a more
precise campaign refined this to $3.9155 \pm 0.0001\,\mathrm{h}$ and simultaneously found subtle
color variations recurring with rotation that cannot be explained by rotational deformation alone
([Lacerda et al. 2008](literatur:lacerda-2008); see Surface). The value used in the dataset,
$3.915341\,\mathrm{h}$, comes from a Herschel thermal lightcurve of the same period
([Santos-Sanz et al. 2017](literatur:santos-sanz-2017)).

The centrifugal force of this fast spin deforms Haumea into a pronounced triaxial ellipsoid. A
multi-chord stellar occultation gave semi-axes of $1161 \pm 30$, $852 \pm 4$ and
$513 \pm 16\,\mathrm{km}$; the same measurement showed that this shape is **not** consistent with a
homogeneous body in hydrostatic equilibrium, and yielded an upper density limit of
$1885 \pm 80\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ and a geometric albedo of $0.51 \pm 0.02$ — markedly smaller than
earlier estimates ([Ortiz et al. 2017](literatur:ortiz-2017)). A more recent Bayesian shape fit
combining several occultations with the moons' orbits revises this to smaller, denser semi-axes
$1061^{+87}_{-71}$, $844^{+5}_{-7}$ and $514^{+18}_{-19}\,\mathrm{km}$ with density
$2050^{+157}_{-152}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ ([Proudfoot et al. 2026a](literatur:proudfoot-2026a)) — a
genuine revision computed with more data, as with Eris before. With the system mass from the orbits
of Hiʻiaka and Namaka, $4.006 \times 10^{21}\,\mathrm{kg}$
([Ragozzine and Brown 2009](literatur:ragozzine-2009)), later refined via an orbit solution that
includes Haumea's own nonspherical gravity field to $3.952 \times 10^{21}\,\mathrm{kg}$
([Proudfoot et al. 2024](literatur:proudfoot-2024)), an own calculation
$\bar\rho = 3M/(4\pi abc)$ gives about $1859\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ for the older shape and about
$2050\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ for the newer one (derivation) — both well below rock density and
consistent with a substantial ice fraction (see Interior).

Whether Haumea is a Jacobi ellipsoid — the equilibrium shape of a homogeneous, rigidly rotating
fluid body — has itself become a point of dispute. Before the occultation, a thermal-optical
analysis of the lightcurve fit well to a Jacobi ellipsoid with axes
$1920 \times 1540 \times 990\,\mathrm{km}$ and density $2.6\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ and read this as
evidence for hydrostatic equilibrium ([Lockwood et al. 2014](literatur:lockwood-2014)); the more
precise 2017 occultation shape is precisely not consistent with a homogeneous equilibrium body
([Ortiz et al. 2017](literatur:ortiz-2017)). A differentiated model with a dense core and ice shell
resolves the contradiction, since such a body can remain in equilibrium despite a shape that departs
from the pure Jacobi form ([Dunham et al. 2019](literatur:dunham-2019); see Interior). A more recent
equilibrium calculation instead finds that the hydrostatic models that best match the occultation
shape sit near critical rotation and take on a "pinched" form deviating by up to $110\,\mathrm{km}$
from any ellipsoid, which past occultations and lightcurves could not distinguish from a simple
ellipsoid; only the next occultation, predicted for 4 May 2026, could settle this
([Staelen et al. 2026](literatur:staelen-2026)) — as of September 2026 no analysis of it is yet
available.

The albedo stood markedly higher only a few years earlier: a thermal analysis of Herschel data gave
$0.75$ ([Lellouch et al. 2010](literatur:lellouch-2010)) — an example of how far thermal radiometry
without independent shape knowledge can depart from the geometric albedo later fixed by the
occultation.

## Interior

The mean density of roughly 1900 to 2050 kg/m³ from both shape determinations requires a substantial
rock fraction beneath a comparatively thin ice shell. An equilibrium model that computes a dense core
and a volatile-rich shell separately finds, for the best fit to the occultation shape, semi-axes of
$1050 \times 840 \times 537\,\mathrm{km}$, a mean density of $2018\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, a core
density of $2680\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, and an ice shell that makes up only about 17 volume percent
of Haumea and is between 67 and 167 km thick ([Dunham et al. 2019](literatur:dunham-2019)). A
geophysical evolution model that jointly computes heat sources, rock–ice differentiation and the
original impact links this picture to Haumea's history: after the family-forming impact of two
partially differentiated bodies, the growing rocky core reduced Haumea's moment of inertia and drove
its spin up further, to the point of ejecting icy material — the origin, in this picture, of ring,
moons and family — before core hydration later let the moment of inertia grow again and the spin
slow accordingly. In this model Haumea could have sustained an internal ocean for up to about 250
million years ([Noviello et al. 2022](literatur:noviello-2022)). Both models are computational
reconstructions without any direct measurement of the interior; how well they hold up depends on the
assumed composition and thermal conductivity (see Open questions).

## Surface

Near-infrared spectra show the characteristic absorption bands of crystalline water ice; a fit gives
a mixture of about 81% crystalline water ice and 19% kaolinite
([Trujillo et al. 2007](literatur:trujillo-2007)). An independent analysis of the same spectral range
instead favors a roughly even mixture of crystalline and amorphous water ice at over 90% ice overall,
and finds no measurable variation across rotation — by this analysis the surface appears fairly
homogeneous globally ([Pinilla-Alonso et al. 2009](literatur:pinilla-alonso-2009)); which fraction is
amorphous and which crystalline depends strongly on the model and spectral range used and is not
settled. This apparent homogeneity, however, contradicts the photometric lightcurve directly: its
asymmetric double peak requires a large, darker and redder region near one of the two rotational
maxima, the so-called dark red spot, whose cause (crater ejecta, leftover pre-collision material, or
something else) is open ([Lacerda et al. 2008](literatur:lacerda-2008); see Open questions).

## Orbit, rotation and dynamics

Haumea orbits the Sun with a semi-major axis of $a = 43.06\,\mathrm{AU}$, eccentricity
$e = 0.194$ and orbital inclination $i = 28.2^\circ$. From the third Kepler law with the Sun's mass
and Haumea's own mass follows an orbital period of about $103\,206$ days, or $282.56$ years
(derivation); the Kepler cross-check $P = a^{3/2}$ gives $282.56$ years, agreeing to about
0.0004%. Over billion-year timescales, a weak, fifth-order 12:7 mean-motion resonance with Neptune
can exchange Haumea's eccentricity and inclination against each other, as mean-motion resonances with
Neptune do for other transneptunian bodies too ([Orbital resonances](thema:resonanzen)); whether
Haumea currently sits in this resonance or only passes through it intermittently is part of the same,
still unsettled investigation of the family's origin
([Ragozzine and Brown 2007](literatur:ragozzine-2007)).

In 2017 the same stellar occultation that measured Haumea's shape also discovered a roughly 70-km-
wide, dense ring at about 2287 km distance from the center, in the plane of Haumea's equator and the
orbit of its largest moon Hiʻiaka — the first known ring around a body beyond the Centaurs
([Ortiz et al. 2017](literatur:ortiz-2017); context alongside Chariklo and Quaoar under
[Ring systems](thema:ringe)); its radius lies close to the 3:1 orbital resonance with Haumea's own
rotation period. Hiʻiaka, the outer and brighter moon, orbits Haumea at $49\,371 \pm 45\,\mathrm{km}$
in $49.462 \pm 0.083$ days; the inner, smaller Namaka at $25\,657 \pm 91\,\mathrm{km}$ in
$18.2783 \pm 0.0076$ days, on an orbit inclined by $13.2 \pm 0.2^\circ$ against Hiʻiaka's orbital
plane; a joint, dynamically interacting three-body fit of both orbits yields Haumea's own mass
([Ragozzine and Brown 2009](literatur:ragozzine-2009)).

Haumea's rotation pole itself is not directly measured but inferred from a lightcurve inversion that
allows two mirror-symmetric solutions: right ascension/declination $282.6^\circ/{-13.0}^\circ$ or
$282.6^\circ/{-11.8}^\circ$ ([Kondratyev and Kornoukhov 2018](literatur:kondratyev-2018)). The plane
of the ring and Hiʻiaka's orbit from the occultation gives an independent second pole that agrees
roughly ([Ortiz et al. 2017](literatur:ortiz-2017)); a third, purely dynamical determination from the
non-Keplerian orbit solution of both moons confirms the same pole within the uncertainties
([Proudfoot et al. 2024](literatur:proudfoot-2024)) — unlike for Eris and Makemake, three independent
methods here converge on nearly the same axis direction (counterexample under
[Axial tilt](thema:achsneigung)).

## Formation and evolution

Several smaller bodies with very similar orbits show the same deep water-ice absorption in their
spectra as Haumea itself — the first collisional family found in the Kuiper belt
([Brown et al. 2007](literatur:brown-2007)). A systematic search for further members by their orbits
found a minimum velocity dispersion of about 140 to 150 m/s and, using the slow diffusion of orbits
through resonances as a clock, a minimum age of about a billion years — the family is thus thought to
be probably primordial, nearly as old as the solar system itself
([Ragozzine and Brown 2007](literatur:ragozzine-2007)). Two competing models address the exact
formation mechanism. In one, family and fast rotation arose in one and the same oblique impact:
hydrodynamic simulations of such a "graze-and-merge" event reproduce both the observed velocity
dispersion of the family and the extreme rotation of the surviving primary
([Leinhardt et al. 2010](literatur:leinhardt-2010)). In the other, the family formed only in a second
step: the original impact first formed only Haumea and a single large moon, which tidally evolved
outward until a second, independent collision destroyed it and the family members formed from the
debris ([Schlichting and Sari 2009](literatur:schlichting-2009)). The
geophysical model described above under Interior links both pictures to a longer internal evolution:
in it, ring, moons and family formed together by ejecting icy material during the initial spin-up
after the impact, before Haumea's rotation later slowed again through core growth
([Noviello et al. 2022](literatur:noviello-2022)); whether this picture correctly captures the actual
formation mechanism is not yet reconciled with the two previous models.

## Open questions

- **How exactly did family, ring, moons and fast rotation form — in a single "graze-and-merge"
  impact, or in a second, independent collision on a moon that formed in the original impact?**
  Both mechanisms are consistent with the family's measured velocity dispersion
  ([Leinhardt et al. 2010](literatur:leinhardt-2010);
  [Schlichting and Sari 2009](literatur:schlichting-2009)), but predict different starting states.
- **Is Haumea a Jacobi ellipsoid in hydrostatic equilibrium, and how dense is it really?** Depending
  on the adopted shape determination, the derived density is about 1859 or about
  2050 kg/m³ (derivation); a homogeneous body does not fit the measured shape
  ([Ortiz et al. 2017](literatur:ortiz-2017)), a differentiated one does
  ([Dunham et al. 2019](literatur:dunham-2019)), and a more recent calculation even predicts, for the
  hydrostatic case, a "pinched" shape departing from any ellipsoid that only a future occultation
  could decide ([Staelen et al. 2026](literatur:staelen-2026)).
- **Where does Haumea's ring come from, and how is it stable outside or inside the Roche limit?**
  The general question of origin and stability for such rings around small bodies is also open for
  Chariklo and Quaoar ([Ring systems](thema:ringe)); for Haumea's ring specifically, its location near
  an orbital resonance with the rotation itself adds a further, unconfirmed possible connection.
- **What causes the dark red spot?** Whether crater ejecta, original material left over from the
  rest of the ejected matter, or another cause is responsible remains unclear
  ([Lacerda et al. 2008](literatur:lacerda-2008)).

## In the model

- **Sphere instead of a triaxial ellipsoid:** Orrery shows Haumea as a sphere with `radiusKm`
  $774.1$, the geometric mean of the semi-axes the dataset labels as "newer",
  $1061 \times 844 \times 518\,\mathrm{km}$ — but the actual source of these semi-axes gives
  $514\,\mathrm{km}$, not $518\,\mathrm{km}$, for the shortest axis
  ([Proudfoot et al. 2026a](literatur:proudfoot-2026a); a dataset finding). With the value that matches
  the literature, the geometric mean would be $772.1\,\mathrm{km}$ instead of $774.1\,\mathrm{km}$, a
  difference of about 0.26% that also shifts the density derived from the dataset by about
  0.8% (derivation). Against the true shape, the sphere departs far more: its diameter
  underestimates the silhouette's longest diameter (along $a = 1061\,\mathrm{km}$) by about 27%
  and overestimates the shortest (along $c = 514\,\mathrm{km}$) by about 51% (derivation) — the
  sphere matches neither the long nor the short axis, but sits in between. Neither the ring, nor the
  moons, nor the dark red spot are part of the model; all three appear at most as part of the static
  texture (`textures/haumea/albedo.jpg`, a stylized "fictional" rendering by Solar System Scope
  approximating color and albedo, `ASSETS.md`), not as their own physical element.
- **Mass and density:** `massKg` $3.952 \times 10^{21}$ follows
  [Proudfoot et al. 2024](literatur:proudfoot-2024); together with the sphere this gives a data-panel
  density of about $2034\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ (derivation) — between the two literature values
  derived above, $1859$ and $2050\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, but closer to the newer one.
- **Rotation and pole:** `rotationPeriodH` $3.915341$ follows
  [Santos-Sanz et al. 2017](literatur:santos-sanz-2017); the pole $282.6^\circ/{-13.0}^\circ$ is
  fixed to the first of the two lightcurve solutions
  ([Kondratyev and Kornoukhov 2018](literatur:kondratyev-2018)) rather than the pole from the ring
  plane ([Ortiz et al. 2017](literatur:ortiz-2017)); the two solutions are only $1.2^\circ$ apart, and
  the difference from the ring-plane pole is of similar size. The axial tilt independently recomputed
  with `achsneigungDeg` gives $90.15^\circ$ for this pole against the heliocentric orbital normal
  (with the second lightcurve solution $89.0^\circ$; derivation) — unlike for Eris and Makemake, this
  pole is an actual measurement rather than the orbital normal itself, and it happens to lie almost
  exactly within Haumea's own orbital plane, as extreme as Uranus's axis but for an entirely different
  reason (there, genuine obliquity; here, a measurement that happens to fall near $90^\circ$;
  [Axial tilt](thema:achsneigung)).
- **Elements and scale:** Haumea runs on osculating heliocentric SBDB elements at epoch
  JD 2,461,200.5, with all rates except $\dot L$ zero, as for the other dwarf planets (values as
  in [Orbital elements](thema:bahnelemente)); the orbital period computed from this via
  $360^\circ/\dot L$ gives $103\,208\,\mathrm{d}$, about 0.002% above the Kepler period computed
  above from mass and semi-major axis (derivation). As a heliocentric dwarf planet, Haumea scales
  with the distance compression like a planet, not with `sizeScale` like a satellite
  (`sim/scale.ts`). Further simplifications: [Limits of the model](thema:modell).
