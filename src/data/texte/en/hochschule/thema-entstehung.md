# Formation of the Solar System

The nebular hypothesis, in which the Sun and planets emerged from a rotating cloud of gas and dust,
was formulated by Swedenborg, Kant and Laplace in the 18th century. For a long time the most serious
objection to it was that it did not explain the Sun's small angular momentum compared with the planets;
since the early 1980s, however, observations of young stars have shown the cool discs of dust and gas
that it requires ([Formation and evolution of the Solar System](quelle:wikipedia-en-formation-solar-system)).
Kant had put forward the idea of a primordial cloud in 1755
([Sonnensystem, section Entstehung](quelle:wikipedia-de-entstehung-sonnensystem)). Dynamical models
usually infer the initial state backwards from today's architecture, because the physics of
the disc phase is not known well enough for unique predictions
([Nesvorný 2018](literatur:nesvorny-2018)).

## The protoplanetary disc

Around 200 discs resolved in the millimetre continuum have radii of about 10 to 500 AU. The mass of
solids follows from the millimetre continuum luminosity; according to the distribution for 887 discs, only a
few contain enough material for a Solar System. Its terrestrial planets and giant-planet cores require
at least about 40 Earth masses of solids, and its gas envelopes at least about 3000 Earth masses of gas.
Because the conversion has to assume an opacity and part of the emission may be optically thick, the
solid masses are regarded as lower limits; gas masses from CO isotopologues lie five to ten times below
other estimates ([Andrews 2020](literatur:andrews-2020)).

In the L band at 3.5 µm, over 80 % of the stars in a young cluster initially
carry a disc; half of them lose it in less than about 3 million years, and after about 6 million years
essentially all stars in a cluster have lost it ([Haisch Jr. et al. 2001](literatur:haisch-2001)). For the solar nebula itself there is a direct
marker: volcanic angrites formed $4563.5 \pm 0.1$ million years ago, about 3.8 million years after the start
of the Solar System, in a field below 0.6 µT. The nebular magnetic field, and probably its gas, had
already gone by then ([Wang et al. 2017](literatur:wang-2017)).

The standard reference for calculations is the minimum-mass solar nebula (MMSN): today's planets,
topped up with hydrogen and helium to solar composition and spread over abutting annuli centred on
their orbits. In the version of Chiang and Youdin, with $x$ as the heliocentric distance in AU
([Chiang and Youdin 2010](literatur:chiang-2010), eqs. 1 to 3):

$$\Sigma_\mathrm{g} = 2200\,x^{-3/2}\,\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{cm}^{-2}, \quad \Sigma_\mathrm{p} = 33\,Z_\mathrm{rel}\,x^{-3/2}\,\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{cm}^{-2}, \quad T = 120\,x^{-3/7}\,\mathrm{K}$$

Out to 100 AU this disc contains 0.03 solar masses. $Z_\mathrm{rel}$ scales the condensed material: 1
when all ices have condensed, 0.78 above about 41 K, where methane ice sublimates, and 0.33 above about 182 K, where
water and all other ices are lost as well (condensation data after Lodders, as cited by Chiang and
Youdin). The temperature profile applies to the midplane of a passive disc heated only by the star;
turbulent accretion can make it warmer. Observations determine $T(r)$ from optically thick lines and
from the condensation fronts of individual molecules ([Andrews 2020](literatur:andrews-2020)).

## Snow line

Beyond the snow line, water condenses to ice, and the surface density of solids in the MMSN rises by a
factor of 2.4 ($Z_\mathrm{rel}$ from 0.33 to 0.78). In the passive disc above, water ice already
condenses outside about 0.4 AU ([Chiang and Youdin 2010](literatur:chiang-2010)). As a disc cools over
time, the snow line generally moves inwards ([Raymond and Morbidelli 2022](literatur:raymond-2022)).
In models by Lichtenberg et al., it moves first outwards and then inwards in the early Solar System and
can thereby trigger two separate bursts of planetesimal formation from different source regions
([Lichtenberg et al. 2021](literatur:lichtenberg-2021)).

## From dust to planetesimals

To form planetesimals, sub-micrometre dust grains have to grow by more than twelve orders of magnitude
in size within a few million years ([Andrews 2020](literatur:andrews-2020)). The gas is supported by
its outward-decreasing pressure and orbits the star slower than Keplerian by $\eta\,v_\mathrm{K}$, with
$\eta\,v_\mathrm{K} \sim c_\mathrm{s}^2/v_\mathrm{K}$, about 25 m/s in the MMSN. A particle feels a
headwind, loses angular momentum and drifts inwards
([Chiang and Youdin 2010](literatur:chiang-2010), eqs. 8 and 13):

$$v_r = -\frac{2\,\mathrm{St}}{1 + \mathrm{St}^2}\,\eta\,v_\mathrm{K}, \quad \mathrm{St} = \Omega_\mathrm{K}\,t_\mathrm{s}$$

With the stopping time $t_\mathrm{s}$, the Stokes number $\mathrm{St}$ is dimensionless. The drift is
fastest at $\mathrm{St} = 1$; for bodies of density $1\,\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{cm}^{-3}$ in a disc of twice
the MMSN mass this means 35 cm at 1 AU up to 120 cm at 7 AU, and at 1 AU they drift towards the star on
a timescale of only about 200 years ([Chiang and Youdin 2010](literatur:chiang-2010)). This is the
drift or metre barrier; in addition, such boulders stick together poorly
([Johansen et al. 2007](literatur:johansen-2007)). Observations, above all with the interferometer
ALMA, show clear signs of growth and migration of the solids, but in a smooth gas disc the drift would
be too fast. Rings and gaps probably mark local pressure maxima in which drifting particles are trapped; there the ratio of solids to gas can approach
unity ([Andrews 2020](literatur:andrews-2020)).

In the streaming instability, gas and solids pull on each other through drag and stream against each
other; particle density perturbations then grow without self-gravity being required. They grow fastest for a stopping time close to the dynamical time and a solid-to-gas density
ratio of order unity ([Youdin and Goodman 2005](literatur:youdin-2005)). In simulations, boulders first
gathered in transient high-pressure regions of the turbulence, the streaming instability concentrated
them by a further order of magnitude, and gravitationally bound clusters with masses of dwarf planets
formed; the collapse proceeded much faster than the drift ([Johansen et al. 2007](literatur:johansen-2007)). The Kuiper belt supplies evidence: binaries from the streaming instability should orbit each other
prograde in about 80 % of cases, as observed ([Nesvorný et al. 2019](literatur:nesvorny-2019)). The
contact binary Arrokoth, which New Horizons flew past in January 2019, resulted from a gentle merger of
two bodies that had formed in a collapsing cloud of particles
([McKinnon et al. 2020](literatur:mckinnon-2020)). The cold classical objects with inclinations below
5° are red, often equal-sized binaries and contain no very large bodies; they therefore formed or
evolved differently from the other trans-Neptunian objects ([Nesvorný 2018](literatur:nesvorny-2018)).

## Protoplanets: oligarchic growth or pebble accretion

Classically, planetesimals grow by collisions, first in runaway and then in oligarchic growth:
neighbouring protoplanets of similar mass keep separations of typically ten Hill radii
([Kokubo and Ida 1998](literatur:kokubo-1998)). Once a protoplanet has
swept up the annulus of width $b\,r_\mathrm{H}$ around its orbit, its growth ends at the isolation
mass $M_\mathrm{iso} \simeq 2\pi a\,b\,r_\mathrm{H}\,\Sigma_\mathrm{p}$. With the Hill radius of two
neighbouring protoplanets of equal mass and $b \approx 10$ as in N-body simulations
([Kokubo and Genda 2010](literatur:kokubo-2010)) this gives

$$r_\mathrm{H} = a \left(\frac{2 M_\mathrm{iso}}{3 M_\odot}\right)^{1/3}, \quad M_\mathrm{iso} = (2\pi\,b\,a^2\,\Sigma_\mathrm{p})^{3/2} \left(\frac{2}{3 M_\odot}\right)^{1/2}$$

In the MMSN above this gives 0.18 Earth masses at 1 AU if, like Chiang and Youdin, one calculates without
ice at Earth's orbit ($Z_\mathrm{rel} = 0.33$, which is how they normalise eq. 2; the passive disc would
already be cold enough for ice there), and 2.3 Earth masses at 5.2 AU with water ice
($Z_\mathrm{rel} = 0.78$). For a
Jupiter core of about ten Earth masses this is too little. The core accretion model of Pollack et al. requires a
planetesimal surface density of about $10\,\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$ at Jupiter, by their account
three to four times the MMSN value, and then yields formation times of 1 to 10 million years for
Jupiter and Saturn
([Pollack et al. 1996](literatur:pollack-1996)). With this density, the formula above gives about 23
Earth masses at 5.2 AU.

Pebble accretion changes the calculation. Centimetre-sized pebbles are loosely coupled to the gas; gas drag
increases their accretion cross section dramatically, and they are captured within the Hill sphere of a
core ([Lambrechts et al. 2014](literatur:lambrechts-2014)). Compared with sweeping up planetesimal fragments or km-sized planetesimals respectively,
this shortens the growth time of a core at 5 AU by a factor of 30 to 1000, provided that the
dust-to-gas ratio in the midplane is near unity, particles grow to millimetres and centimetres, and
there are massive planetesimals as seeds or a low pressure support
([Lambrechts and Johansen 2012](literatur:lambrechts-2012)). The rates are orders of magnitude higher
than those of planetesimal accretion and decrease only slowly with distance, so cores can start far out
and migrate while growing ([Johansen and Lambrechts 2017](literatur:johansen-2017)). The growth limits
itself: from the pebble isolation mass $M_\mathrm{PIM}$ onwards, the gas just outside the core's orbit
becomes super-Keplerian, and drifting pebbles are held up there. In a disc with relative scale height
$h$ ([Lambrechts et al. 2014](literatur:lambrechts-2014), eq. 12 and section 4.1)

$$M_\mathrm{PIM} \approx 20\,M_\oplus \left(\frac{h}{0.05}\right)^3, \quad h = \frac{H}{a}$$

## Giant planets and core accretion

In the model of Pollack et al. three phases follow: a solid core grows and depletes its feeding zone,
then solids and gas accrete slowly and almost steadily for a long time, and once both masses are about
equal a self-reinforcing gas accretion sets in. The duration of the second phase determines the total
time ([Pollack et al. 1996](literatur:pollack-1996)). A core of about ten Earth masses has to be complete
before the gas disperses after 1 to 10 million years ([Lambrechts and Johansen 2012](literatur:lambrechts-2012)).
Pebble accretion eases the bottleneck: on reaching $M_\mathrm{PIM}$ the heating by infalling solids
stops, the critical core mass drops and the envelope collapses. In this picture Uranus and Neptune did
not reach this mass before the disc dispersed and remained ice giants with low-mass envelopes rich in
water vapour ([Lambrechts et al. 2014](literatur:lambrechts-2014)). The early Juno gravity data favoured
for [Jupiter](objekt:jupiter) a dilute core, extended over a large fraction of the radius, with 7 to 25
Earth masses of heavy elements, which was hard to reconcile with formation and evolution models ([Wahl et al. 2017](literatur:wahl-2017);
more under [Interior structure](thema:innerer-aufbau)).

## Clocks from meteorites

The zero point of the chronology is the calcium-aluminium-rich inclusions (CAIs), the first solids.
Uranium-corrected lead-lead ages give $4567.30 \pm 0.16$ million years; chondrule formation started
at the same time as the CAIs and lasted about 3 million years
([Connelly et al. 2012](literatur:connelly-2012)). Bouvier and Wadhwa dated one CAI to
$4568.2_{-0.4}^{+0.2}$ million years; they used the recently redetermined value of 137.84 for ²³⁸U/²³⁵U in
natural uranium and estimated the deviation of their CAI from its Th/U ratio
([Bouvier and Wadhwa 2010](literatur:bouvier-2010)). The ratio was long considered uniform at 137.88,
but it varies in CAIs, which can shift lead-lead ages by up to 5 million years; the first dating of a CAI with a measured ratio gave
$4567.18 \pm 0.50$ million years ([Amelin et al. 2010](literatur:amelin-2010)).

Relative ages come from short-lived, now extinct nuclides. If $R$ is the ratio of such a nuclide to a
stable isotope of the same element when a sample closes and $R_0$ its value in the CAIs, the interval
from the zero point is

$$\Delta t = \frac{t_{1/2}}{\ln 2}\,\ln\frac{R_0}{R}$$

For ²⁶Al, with a half-life of about 0.7 million years, the whole-rock isochron of CAIs from the
Allende meteorite gave $R_0 = (5.23 \pm 0.13) \cdot 10^{-5}$ for ²⁶Al/²⁷Al. This confirmed the
canonical value and limits the formation of these CAIs to ±20,000 years
([Jacobsen et al. 2008](literatur:jacobsen-2008)). The same nuclide heated the early planetesimals: the
different thermal histories of the parent bodies mainly reflect their initial abundance of ²⁶Al, which
depends on their accretion age ([Kleine et al. 2009](literatur:kleine-2009)).

The ¹⁸²Hf–¹⁸²W system, with a half-life of $8.90 \pm 0.09$ million years
([Vockenhuber et al. 2004](literatur:vockenhuber-2004)), dates core formation because hafnium stays in
the silicate while tungsten goes into the metal. Accordingly, the parent bodies of magmatic iron
meteorites probably accreted about 0.1 to 0.3 million years after the CAIs and segregated their cores
over about one million years ([Kruijer et al. 2014](literatur:kruijer-2014)). [Mars](objekt:mars),
based on a more precisely determined Hf/W ratio of its mantle, reached half its present size in
$1.8_{-1.0}^{+0.9}$ million years or less, consistent with a stranded embryo
([Dauphas and Pourmand 2011](literatur:dauphas-2011)). For the
[Earth](objekt:earth), tungsten model ages of core formation range from about 30 to more than 100
million years after the CAIs, because they depend sensitively on large impacts and on how far the cores
of the impactors equilibrated with the mantle. From the ¹⁸²W/¹⁸⁴W ratios of the lunar and
terrestrial mantles, then measured as identical, Kleine et al. concluded that the Moon-forming impact
came only after ¹⁸²Hf had become extinct, more than about 50 million years after the CAIs, provided that
the Hf/W ratios of Moon and Earth differ by more than about 10 % ([Kleine et al. 2009](literatur:kleine-2009)).
More precise measurements from 2015 show a lunar ¹⁸²W excess of $20.6 \pm 5.1$ ppm
([Touboul et al. 2015](literatur:touboul-2015)) and $27 \pm 4$ ppm
([Kruijer et al. 2015](literatur:kruijer-2015)) relative to the present-day terrestrial mantle. Both
groups attribute it to disproportional late accretion, before which the values were identical; Kruijer
et al. call it enigmatic that the giant impact left no difference.

## Isotopic dichotomy and Jupiter's early growth

Mass-independent isotopic anomalies divide the meteorites into two groups: in
$\varepsilon^{54}\mathrm{Cr}$ against $\varepsilon^{50}\mathrm{Ti}$ and against $\Delta^{17}\mathrm{O}$,
the carbonaceous chondrites (CC) lie far apart from all other materials (NC). Earth and Mars belong to
the NC group; by the lever rule, Warren estimated a carbonaceous fraction of very roughly 24 % for the
Earth and 9 % for Mars, at most about 32 % and 18 %, without excluding zero. Besides episodic accretion,
he considered, explicitly as speculation, a boundary between the inner and outer Solar System
([Warren 2011](literatur:warren-2011)). More recent estimates arrive at 2 % for the Earth from nitrogen
and hydrogen isotopes, and mixing models for Earth and Mars at 3 to 10 %
([Brasser and Mojzsis 2020](literatur:brasser-2020)).
Molybdenum and tungsten isotopes in iron meteorites show that both reservoirs coexisted from about 1 to
about 3 to 4 million years after the CAIs and remained separate. Kruijer et al. name as the most plausible
cause a gap that the young Jupiter opened in the disc: its core would then have reached about 20 Earth masses in
less than one million years and grown to about 50 Earth masses by at least 3 to 4 million years
([Kruijer et al. 2017](literatur:kruijer-2017)). Whether Jupiter really was the barrier is open (see below).

## Migration in the gas: the Grand Tack

According to hydrodynamic simulations, Jupiter and Saturn can migrate in the gas disc first inwards and
then outwards, on timescales of only about 100,000 years. In the Grand Tack model, Jupiter migrates inwards to 1.5 AU and then turns back together with Saturn. The
planetesimal disc is then truncated at 1 AU, the terrestrial planets grow over the following 30 to 50
million years, and the mass ratio of Earth and Mars fits. Jupiter empties the asteroid belt and refills
it, the inner belt with bodies from 1 to 3 AU and the outer belt with bodies from between and beyond the
giant planets; this explains the marked differences in composition across the belt
([Walsh et al. 2011](literatur:walsh-2011)). It requires the 3:2 resonance of Jupiter and Saturn, into
which both are captured under standard conditions during convergent migration
([Nesvorný 2018](literatur:nesvorny-2018)).

## After the gas: the Nice model

Without gas, the planets exchange angular momentum with the leftover planetesimals: if a planet of mass
$M$ ejects planetesimals of total mass $m$ from the system, its orbit shrinks by
$\delta r/r \simeq -C\,m/M$ with $C$ of order unity. Jupiter mainly ejects and migrates inwards; Saturn,
Uranus and Neptune preferentially scatter inwards and migrate outwards
([Nesvorný 2018](literatur:nesvorny-2018)). How Neptune captured Pluto into the 3:2 resonance in the
process is described under [Orbital resonances](thema:resonanzen).

The Nice model adds an instability. In the original version the giant planets start on nearly circular,
coplanar orbits and, while migrating through a planetesimal disc, Jupiter and Saturn cross their 1:2
resonance. This produces today's semi-major axes, eccentricities and mutual inclinations
([Tsiganis et al. 2005](literatur:tsiganis-2005)). Gomes et al. linked the
instability to the Late Heavy Bombardment (LHB), a spike in the cratering rate about 700 million years
after planet formation inferred from lunar petrology ([Gomes et al. 2005](literatur:gomes-2005)).

The later variants start from a resonant chain left by the gas phase. For the secular resonances not to
excite the terrestrial planets and the asteroid belt too strongly, the period ratio $P_6/P_5$ of Saturn
to Jupiter must have jumped from below 2.1 to above 2.3, perhaps through encounters with an ice giant
("jumping Jupiter"). With an early instability the constraint from the terrestrial planets could be
bypassed; that from the asteroid belt would remain ([Nesvorný 2018](literatur:nesvorny-2018)). In nearly $10^4$ simulations, the
instability with four giant planets and Jupiter and Saturn in 3:2 was mostly too violent and ejected at
least one ice giant. An additional ice giant with the mass of Uranus or Neptune, ejected by Jupiter,
fitted better, best with an outer disc of about 20 Earth masses; even then the result matched today's
Solar System with a probability of only about 5 % ([Nesvorný and Morbidelli 2012](literatur:nesvorny-2012)).
Nesvorný's review assembles, with considerable uncertainties, a sequence from this: Neptune initially at
about 20 to 25 AU, the additional ice giant at about 10 AU, a planetesimal disc out to about 30 AU with
15 to 20 Earth masses and the instability when
Neptune reached about 27.7 AU. The dispersed planetesimal disc populated the Kuiper belt, the scattered disc, the
Oort cloud and the Jupiter Trojans and, with a probability of $5 \cdot 10^{-6}$ per planetesimal, the
asteroid belt as well ([Nesvorný 2018](literatur:nesvorny-2018); see also
[Dwarf planets](thema:zwergplaneten) and [Kirkwood gaps](thema:kirkwood-luecken)).

## Terrestrial planets and the Moon

According to geochemical data and models, the formation of the terrestrial planets probably ended
about 30 to 100 million years after the gas disc dispersed ([Nesvorný 2018](literatur:nesvorny-2018)).
For the Earth it ended with the giant impact from which the [Moon](objekt:moon) emerged
([Maurice et al. 2020](literatur:maurice-2020)); impact models
and isotopic findings are described under [Earth](objekt:earth). Its timing is disputed: zircons from
Apollo 14 samples require a Moon within the first about 60 million years
([Barboni et al. 2017](literatur:barboni-2017)); a long-lived magma ocean instead leads to
$4.425 \pm 0.025$ billion years, about 140 million years after the CAIs
([Maurice et al. 2020](literatur:maurice-2020)).

## Open questions

- **Timing of the instability:** A late instability was supported by the impact spike inferred from
  lunar rocks ([Gomes et al. 2005](literatur:gomes-2005)). An inner extension of the asteroid belt at
  1.7 to 2.1 AU, destabilised by late migration, explains impacts into the Archaean
  ([Bottke et al. 2012](literatur:bottke-2012)), and shock ages of several meteorite classes cluster
  around the time of the LHB ([Nesvorný 2018](literatur:nesvorny-2018)). Against this, argon loss by
  diffusion in the Apollo samples can produce a cluster of ages at 3.9 billion years even from a steadily
  declining impact flux ([Boehnke and Harrison 2016](literatur:boehnke-2016)). The binary Jupiter Trojan
  Patroclus–Menoetius only survived if migrating planets dispersed the planetesimal disc within 100
  million years of the nebula's dispersal ([Nesvorný et al. 2018a](literatur:nesvorny-2018a)). The early
  proposals also lie far apart: the dispersal of the gas disc as trigger gives a few to ten million years
  after the start ([Liu et al. 2022](literatur:liu-2022)); enstatite meteorites, whose fragments the
  instability is thought to have carried from the terrestrial-planet region into the main belt, give 60
  to 100 million years together with the Trojan limit ([Avdellidou et al. 2024](literatur:avdellidou-2024)).
- **Grand Tack versus alternatives:** The Grand Tack explains the small Mars and the mixed composition
  of the asteroid belt in one go ([Walsh et al. 2011](literatur:walsh-2011)). The reversal, however,
  requires a Jupiter-to-Saturn mass ratio of about 2:1 to 4:1, and whether it holds during simultaneous
  gas accretion is open ([Raymond and Morbidelli 2022](literatur:raymond-2022)). Other models do without
  it: a belt that was almost empty from the start and was filled by scattering with S-types from inside
  and C-types from outside ([Raymond and Izidoro 2017](literatur:raymond-2017)), an early instability
  that cuts off Mars' growth ([Clement et al. 2018](literatur:clement-2018)), or a pressure maximum near
  Jupiter's orbit that could explain the low mass of the belt and prevents Jupiter from migrating
  inwards
  ([Brasser and Mojzsis 2020](literatur:brasser-2020)).
- **Cause of the dichotomy:** According to Kruijer et al., the growing Jupiter separated the reservoirs
  ([Kruijer et al. 2017](literatur:kruijer-2017)). According to Brasser and Mojzsis, Jupiter's core did
  not grow fast enough by pebble accretion for this, and a pressure maximum in the disc took over the
  separation ([Brasser and Mojzsis 2020](literatur:brasser-2020)); according to Lichtenberg et al., the
  migration of the snow line suffices ([Lichtenberg et al. 2021](literatur:lichtenberg-2021)).
- **Origin of Earth's water:** The D/H ratio of carbonaceous chondrites, $(1.4 \pm 0.1) \cdot 10^{-4}$,
  is close to Earth's value of $(1.558 \pm 0.001) \cdot 10^{-4}$, whereas that of six Oort cloud comets
  averages $(2.96 \pm 0.25) \cdot 10^{-4}$ ([Hartogh et al. 2011](literatur:hartogh-2011)). Hydrogen and
  nitrogen isotopes suggest CI chondrites as the principal source of Earth's volatiles
  ([Alexander et al. 2012](literatur:alexander-2012)). Comets are not uniform, however: 103P/Hartley 2
  has ocean-like water with $(1.61 \pm 0.24) \cdot 10^{-4}$ ([Hartogh et al. 2011](literatur:hartogh-2011)),
  67P/Churyumov–Gerasimenko about three times the terrestrial value with $(5.3 \pm 0.7) \cdot 10^{-4}$
  ([Altwegg et al. 2015](literatur:altwegg-2015)). A third possibility is material from the inner
  system: enstatite chondrites, isotopically similar to Earth's rocks and long presumed dry, contain
  enough hydrogen for at least three ocean masses, with hydrogen and nitrogen isotopes like those of
  Earth's mantle ([Piani et al. 2020](literatur:piani-2020)).

## In the model

- **Present state, no evolution:** Orrery moves all bodies on Keplerian orbits with linearly
  propagated elements ([Orbital elements](thema:bahnelemente)). There is no gas disc, no migration, no
  instability and no mutual perturbation.
- **Validity of the rates:** The planetary elements come from the JPL table for 1800 to 2050; they are
  fitted to this interval and, according to JPL, not valid outside it. The data block warns outside 1800
  to 2050. Only the eight planets have rates for $a$ and $e$. Propagated linearly, Saturn's
  eccentricity would become zero in the year 12,563 and negative thereafter, and going backwards
  Neptune's in the year −14,828; the Kepler solver rejects such values. Orrery therefore limits time
  to Julian day 0 (4713 BC) through 31 December 9999, and the time-lapse stops at these limits.
  Within this range the planets' eccentricities stay between 0 and 1; the smallest is Venus's,
  0.0035 in the year 9999.
  Propagated back over 4.5 billion years, the rates would give semi-major axes of 5228
  AU for Jupiter and −16 AU for Mercury.
- **Chaos:** Even a complete calculation could not trace the path back uniquely: the motion of the
  planets is chaotic with a maximum Lyapunov exponent of about 1/(5 million years), and the orbits of
  the inner planets are unpredictable over a few tens of millions of years
  ([Laskar 1989](literatur:laskar-1989)).
- **Belts:** The main belt and the Kuiper belt are point clouds with fixed elements drawn from
  prescribed distributions; only the mean anomaly advances. The Kuiper cloud mixes 60 % cold classical
  particles (Rayleigh parameter of the inclination 3°), 25 % hot ones (12°) and 15 % Plutinos. All
  main-belt particles have the [albedo](thema:photometrie) 0.06; there is no division into S and C types. Jupiter Trojans,
  the scattered disc and the Oort cloud are missing. Further simplifications:
  [Limits of the model](thema:modell).
