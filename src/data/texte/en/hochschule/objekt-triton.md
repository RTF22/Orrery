# Triton

Triton is by far the largest moon of [Neptune](objekt:neptune) and, by radius, the seventh-largest
moon in the Solar System – but the only large moon that orbits its planet retrograde: against the
planet's own sense of rotation, on an orbit strongly inclined to Neptune's equator. No formation
disc produces such an orbit; Triton must have been captured, probably from the Kuiper belt, making
it a close relative of [Pluto](objekt:pluto). The retrograde motion has a second consequence: tides
are slowly pulling Triton inward, instead of pushing it outward as they do for most moons. Voyager 2
flew past Triton on 25 August 1989, the only spacecraft ever to do so, and supplied most of what is
known about the moon today; everything since has come from Earth, from stellar occultations, or from
models. This text presents parameters, interior, surface, atmosphere and magnetosphere, and orbit,
rotation and formation, and closes with what Orrery represents of all this.

## Parameters and measurement

Voyager 2 provided Triton's radius through limb fitting of its images, and mass, atmospheric
pressure and surface temperature through radio science (Doppler tracking and a radio occultation)
([Smith et al. 1989](literatur:smith-1989); [Tyler et al. 1989](literatur:tyler-1989)). Jacobson
reanalysed decades of ground-based and Voyager astrometry of Triton's own orbit and redetermined
Neptune's system mass and pole from it ([Jacobson 2009](literatur:jacobson-2009); as with
[Neptune](objekt:neptune)); Triton's own mass then follows as the difference between the system's
total mass carried in the catalogue and Neptune's mass alone.

The mean density derived from catalogue mass and radius,
$\bar\rho = 3\,M / (4\pi R^3) = 2.061\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, matches the round value of about
2.06 g/cm³ often quoted in the literature and lies between the density of the large icy moons –
Titan at 1.881 g/cm³ – and that of Pluto, which for both Triton and Pluto suggests a markedly
higher rock fraction than for most other large icy moons (placement alongside all measured values
under [Interior Structure](thema:innerer-aufbau)). The real scatter lies in the albedo: the
fact-sheet value 0.72 is an integral round number; Nelson et al. find values between 0.59 (UV) and
0.81 (green) depending on the filter, plus a Bond albedo of $0.82 \pm 0.05$ – the differences
mostly reflect Triton's strongly wavelength-dependent reflectance spectrum, not a measurement
uncertainty. Time series from stellar occultations show an unexpected course since 1989: a rise to
about one and a half times the 1989 value by the mid-1990s, followed by a decline back to
approximately the Voyager value by 2017 and a plateau at that lower value since, through 2022 (see
"Atmosphere and magnetosphere" and "Open questions").

## Interior

Radius and mean density together point to a differentiated body with a high rock and metal fraction
in the core, topped by an ice shell with a possible liquid ocean. Two independent heat sources are
candidates. First, radiogenic heat from the decaying rock of the core: for medium-sized icy moons
and large trans-Neptunian objects in general – a class Triton falls into by size – whether an ocean
persists under an insulating, non-convecting ice shell over the lifetime of the Solar System mainly
depends on the radiogenic heating rate of the rock fraction and how effectively heat is carried
through the ice shell ([Hussmann et al. 2006](literatur:hussmann-2006)). For Triton specifically, a
more recent estimate shows that radiogenic heat alone suffices for a long-lived ocean beneath a
heat-conducting, non-convecting ice shell, but not for the convective deformation needed to explain
the young surface (see below). Second, therefore, tidal heat from a small obliquity forced by
Neptune: because Triton's orbit is strongly inclined to Neptune's equator, Neptune's tidal action
maintains a small but non-zero obliquity of the spin axis against Triton's own orbit (about 0.7° in
a stable Cassini state); the heat this dissipates in an ocean can, for an ice shell about 300 km
thick, reach the order of 0.3 TW – enough to make the ice shell flow convectively and yield at the
surface ([Nimmo and Spencer 2015](literatur:nimmo-2015)). Both works draw on the same observation –
a young, sparsely cratered surface (see below) – to conclude that some heat source was active into
the geologically recent past, without the two mechanisms, tidal heat from obliquity and radiogenic
heat, excluding one another; a surviving ocean is plausible but not proven (see "Open questions").

## Surface

Near-infrared spectra from the flyby show nitrogen ice as the dominant component on Triton, along
with methane, carbon monoxide and carbon dioxide ice
([Cruikshank et al. 1993](literatur:cruikshank-1993)). The surface is exceptionally young and
nearly crater-free: models of the impact flux in the outer Solar System place an upper bound of
about 50 million years on the more heavily cratered terrains, and of only about 6 million years on
the Neptune-facing cantaloupe terrain (see below); if planetocentric impact debris rather than
heliocentric comets dominates the source, the whole surface could be younger than 10 million years
– "negligible" compared with the age of the Solar System
([Schenk and Zahnle 2007](literatur:schenk-2007)). Large parts of the southern hemisphere show a
pitted "cantaloupe" terrain with no direct counterpart elsewhere in the Solar System, along with
smooth plains probably flooded by cryovolcanism; a more recent photogrammetric analysis of the
Voyager images produces the first global topographic maps for under 40 % of the surface and confirms
relief differences of several kilometres between basins and highlands
([Schenk et al. 2021](literatur:schenk-2021)).

Over the south polar cap, Voyager 2 discovered at least four active, geyser-like plumes of dark
material that rise to about 8 km and are then carried for kilometres by the wind
([Soderblom et al. 1990](literatur:soderblom-1990)). Two interpretations stand side by side: solar
heating of a thin, semi-transparent nitrogen ice film could vaporise gas trapped beneath it, which
then erupts at weak points (a "solid-state greenhouse" effect); alternatively, internal, endogenic
heat – for instance from the sources named in "Interior" – could release gas at the base of the ice
crust. Solar heating more readily explains the observed clustering of the plumes near the subsolar
latitude; a purely endogenic origin would have to explain this clustering through a coincidental
geographic distribution of geothermal anomalies. Both mechanisms remain open (see "Open questions").

## Atmosphere and magnetosphere

Triton's atmosphere is a thin envelope, mostly nitrogen, with traces of methane and a high, thin
haze layer; the Voyager radio occultation gave a surface temperature of about 38 K and a surface
pressure of about 14 µbar, plus an ionosphere with a maximum electron density at an altitude of
several hundred kilometres ([Tyler et al. 1989](literatur:tyler-1989)). The low surface pressure
means that even small temperature changes of the surface ice – whose vapour pressure keeps the
atmosphere in equilibrium – can change the pressure by a large factor. A series of stellar
occultation measurements spanning 33 years (1989 to 2022) shows exactly that, following an
unexpected course: the pressure rose from about 14 µbar at Voyager to about 19 µbar by the
mid-1990s, fell back to a value near the Voyager level by an occultation in 2017, and has stayed at
that lower level essentially unchanged through the most recent measurement in 2022 – models, by
contrast, predict a steady further decline for the period 2005 to 2060, which this observation does
not confirm ([Sicardy et al. 2024](literatur:sicardy-2024)). The simplest explanation – a seasonal
cycle of nitrogen ice frost, which after the southern summer solstice in the year 2000 should lead
to a continued pressure drop with growing distance from the Sun – therefore does not straightforwardly
explain why the decline has not continued since 2017; more complex surface–atmosphere interactions,
such as an ice–albedo feedback or localised nitrogen frost formation near the equator, are discussed
(see "Open questions").

Triton lies deep inside Neptune's magnetosphere, whose field, strongly tilted against the rotation
axis and offset from the centre, exposes Triton to varying field strength over each orbit. As with
the icy moons of the gas giants, a secondary field induced in a conductive ocean beneath Triton's
ice crust by Neptune's rotating field would be a possible test for an ocean; a future flyby mission
carrying a magnetometer is among the options discussed by the Decadal Survey for a Neptune–Triton
system ([National Academies of Sciences 2022](literatur:national-academies-2022); as with
[Neptune](objekt:neptune)), but no magnetometer measurement at Triton itself exists so far.

## Orbit, rotation and dynamics

Triton orbits Neptune on a nearly circular path ($e = 0.000146$) at $354766\,\mathrm{km}$, which
is $14.41$ Neptune radii, in $5.877$ days – the third Kepler law with Neptune's and Triton's masses
combined gives $5.877084$ days, the dataset's own sidereal motion gives $5.876994$ days, and the
NSSDC fact sheet gives $5.876854$ days, all three agreeing to under 0.02 %. Against Neptune's equator
in the reference frame used here, Horizons gives an orbital inclination of $i = 156.828^\circ$ – over
90°, hence by definition a retrograde orbit: the orbital angular momentum points opposite to
Neptune's rotation pole. Against the more distant Laplace plane, which for most moons lies between a
planet's equatorial and orbital plane, older literature values give about 157°; which reference plane
is meant changes the value by less than a degree, because Triton's orbit lies so close to the planet
that the Laplace plane there practically coincides with the equator
([Moons and the Laplace plane](thema:bahnelemente)). Triton rotates in a bound state; figures on
libration and on the angle between the map centre and the direction to Neptune are given under
[Bound Rotation](thema:gebundene-rotation).

The retrograde motion has an unusual consequence for tidal evolution: while prograde moons such as
Earth's Moon migrate outward under tides, tidal friction removes orbital angular momentum from a
retrograde moon and spirals it inward. For the case of the small obliquity named above (a Cassini
state near zero), a model calculation using the classical Kaula tidal formalism finds that Triton
would reach the Roche limit in about 3.6 billion years, with the orbital inclination declining from
today's value to about 145°; for the second possible Cassini state (obliquity near 100°), the decay
would be considerably faster ([Chyba et al. 1989](literatur:chyba-1989);
[Correia 2009a](literatur:correia-2009a); on the Roche limit and timescales in general,
[Tides and the Roche Limit](thema:gezeiten)). With the rigid-body approximation
$R_\mathrm{N}\,(2\,\rho_\mathrm{N}/\rho_\mathrm{T})^{1/3}$ (subscript N for Neptune, T for Triton),
Neptune's Roche limit for Triton's density is about 28,700 km; with Roche's own fluid approximation
$2.44\,R_\mathrm{N}\,(\rho_\mathrm{N}/\rho_\mathrm{T})^{1/3}$, it is about
55,600 km (both derivations, using Neptune's density of $1.638\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ derived from its
catalogue mass and radius); Triton's present orbital radius is therefore roughly six to twelve times
farther out than this limit, so the decay is a very distant future, not a present condition. Were
Triton to fall within the Roche limit, tidal forces would tear it apart, and it could, similar to
what is suspected for part of the material in Saturn's rings, at least partly form a ring system
around Neptune.

## Formation and evolution

No body can have formed on such an inclined, retrograde orbit within the shared gas and dust disc
from which Neptune and its original moons formed: within today's standard model of planet
formation, such an orbit can only be explained by later capture. The most widely accepted
explanation is a three-body exchange: Triton was originally part of a binary from the trans-Neptunian
belt – as also occurs with Pluto and Charon, or with many smaller Kuiper belt objects – that was
torn apart during a close encounter with young Neptune through Neptune's own tidal action. Triton lost
enough orbital energy to remain bound to Neptune, while its former partner flew off on a now-unbound,
hyperbolic orbit ([Agnor and Hamilton 2006](literatur:agnor-2006)). This mechanism readily explains
why Triton – unlike a body captured by pure gas drag – could have largely kept its original density
and composition typical of Kuiper belt objects, and why capture within the still gas-rich, much
denser protoplanetary environment was not required.

A freshly captured Triton initially moved on a strongly eccentric orbit. With the same tidal
parameters already used by Goldreich et al. 1989, a complete model of the secular tidal evolution –
which, unlike earlier work, also accounts for Triton's own spin instead of assuming it synchronous
from the start – circularises the orbit over roughly a billion years; by the same calculation,
Triton remained largely molten until about a billion years ago, and its interior is still warm
today ([Correia 2009a](literatur:correia-2009a)). This process must have all but destroyed Neptune's
original, presumably coplanar and prograde moon system: an initially strongly eccentric Triton
crossed, on its way to today's orbit, the region between 5 and 23 Neptune radii, where regular
moons similar to today's Uranian moons probably once orbited – with the total mass of this original
system only about 40 % of Triton's own mass, a model calculation finds these moons colliding with
one another within a few centuries into a debris ring around Neptune, which Triton subsequently
swept up; individual debris pieces flung far outward could account for Nereid and the small, outer
moon Halimede ([Ćuk and Gladman 2005](literatur:cuk-2005)). Because of this shared origin and the
comparable size and density, Triton is considered a close dynamical and compositional relative of
[Pluto](objekt:pluto), even though Pluto itself was never captured but migrated into Neptune's
3:2 resonance ([Formation](thema:entstehung)).

## Open questions

- **Ocean.** Radiogenic heat alone would maintain an ocean beneath an insulating ice shell, but
  without the convection needed to explain the observed young surface
  ([Hussmann et al. 2006](literatur:hussmann-2006)); tidal heat from a small forced obliquity could
  supply enough additional heat ([Nimmo and Spencer 2015](literatur:nimmo-2015)). Whether an ocean
  still exists today, how thick it would be, and whether it fully separates the crust from the core
  cannot be decided without a gravity-field or magnetometer measurement at Triton itself.
- **Driver of the geysers.** Solar heating of a thin ice film
  ([Soderblom et al. 1990](literatur:soderblom-1990)) versus endogenic heat from the interior –
  both interpretations explain the observed plumes, and neither has been ruled out.
- **Age of the surface.** Crater counts yield only upper bounds (about 50 million years for the
  most heavily cratered terrains, about 6 million for the cantaloupe terrain) that depend strongly
  on whether heliocentric comets or planetocentric impact debris are mainly responsible for the
  craters; whether the activity continues to this day remains uncertain
  ([Schenk and Zahnle 2007](literatur:schenk-2007)).
- **Capture mechanism.** The three-body exchange from a binary
  ([Agnor and Hamilton 2006](literatur:agnor-2006)) readily explains the orbit and Triton's largely
  unaltered composition; earlier proposals involving pure gas drag in a still extended protoplanetary
  envelope or a direct collision are now less favoured, but not ruled out with final certainty.
- **Course of the atmospheric pressure.** Why the pressure, having risen since Voyager until the
  mid-1990s, has held at a level lower than its peak since 2017 instead of continuing to fall as
  simple seasonal models predict for the period after 2005, is unclear
  ([Sicardy et al. 2024](literatur:sicardy-2024)).

## In the model

Triton is the only moon of [Neptune](objekt:neptune) in the catalogue; Nereid, Proteus and all the
other, considerably smaller and irregular moons are missing, as are Neptune's rings – both already
described at Neptune itself. This makes Triton the automatic sole candidate in every shadow
calculation around Neptune: `waehleOkkluder` collects all sibling moons of a central body before
trimming the list to `MAX_OKKLUDER` (4); since Neptune has only this one moon in the catalogue, the
selection, regardless of distance or angular radius, always ends up with exactly one entry – Triton
casts its shadow in the model without exception.

Orbit and rotation follow the same procedure as for all Neptunian and Plutonian moons: osculating
Horizons elements at epoch J2000 against Neptune's equator, with no precession of node or periapsis
(`nodeDot = lpDot = 0`, as with [Orbital Elements](thema:bahnelemente)). `rotationPeriodH` is
$-141.04786\,\mathrm{h}$ in the dataset; the negative sign comes from the (retrograde) rotation rate
of the same SPICE kernel that also supplies Neptune's own pole, and is checked against the third
Kepler law: $141.04786\,\mathrm{h} / 24 = 5.8770$ days matches the orbital period given above to
$0.028 \cdot 10^{-6}$ relative – the data panel rounds both values independently to one decimal
place and so shows the same figure, 5.9 days, for both orbital period and day length, even though
they come from different calculations (the Kepler formula with the system mass versus the fixed
dataset value).

The pole is fixed in the dataset at $299.36^\circ / 41.17^\circ$ – the constant terms of the IAU
series, without the large periodic terms with which the IAU report describes Triton's actual pole
drift (as with [Axial Tilt](thema:achsneigung) and [Bound Rotation](thema:gebundene-rotation)).
`achsneigungDeg` compares this fixed pole with the orbital normal built from position and velocity
at the epoch, and flips the result by 180° because of the negative rotation period: the
recalculation gives $21.394^\circ$, rounding to the $21.4^\circ$ shown in the data panel – exactly
the libration amplitude in latitude already fact-checked there, because both quantities measure the
same angle between pole and orbital normal. This value is **not** Triton's physical obliquity: the
actual obliquity named in "Interior", the one decisive for tidal heating, is estimated in the
literature at about 0.7° ([Nimmo and Spencer 2015](literatur:nimmo-2015)) – a value that presupposes
a spin axis precessing with the moving orbit, as the IAU report describes with its periodic terms.
Orrery's fixed pole does not capture this precession; the data-panel value of 21.4° is an artefact
of this simplification, not a measurement. Without the 180° flip, the same calculation gives
$158.606^\circ$, not quite identical to Horizons' own $156.828^\circ$ inclination: the difference of
$1.778^\circ$ corresponds almost exactly to the angle between Triton's fixed dataset pole and the
nutation-corrected Neptune pole used for the orbit calculation ($1.781^\circ$, independently
recalculated) – the same kind of reference-plane inconsistency that amounts to about 0.51° for
Neptune's own raw versus corrected pole (noted in a comment in `neptun-monde.ts`), here with
Triton's own, again slightly different pole.

Albedo 0.72 is normalised to the catalogue albedo like every body
([Albedo and Brightness](thema:photometrie)); Orrery's renderer has no concept of atmospheres, haze
or geysers – Triton appears as a sharply bounded, directly lit sphere with the Voyager 2 mosaic
texture (`ASSETS.md`), whose unlit northern hemisphere at the time of the flyby stayed unexposed and
appears black in the model. As a satellite (`isSatellite`, with `parent` set to Neptune, not the
Sun), Triton's orbital radius around Neptune scales, like its own radius and Neptune's, with
`sizeScale`, not with the distance compression applied to the planets' own orbits
(`scaledPositionAt`, [Limits of the Model](thema:modell)). The scene
[Triton's Retrograde Orbit](szene:triton-rueckwaerts) shows the strongly inclined, retrograde orbital
ellipse from a perspective in which Triton itself no longer carries a label, owing to the camera
distance – the subject is the orbit, not a close view of the moon.
