# Enceladus

At 252 km in radius, Enceladus is only Saturn's sixth-largest moon, but one of the most active
bodies in the Solar System: four parallel fracture zones at its south pole shoot out plumes of
water vapour and ice grains that feed Saturn's E ring and have been observed directly by the
Cassini spacecraft since 2005 ([Porco et al. 2006](literatur:porco-2006);
[Hansen et al. 2006](literatur:hansen-2006); [Waite et al. 2006](literatur:waite-2006)). Gravity
and libration measurements point to a global, salty ocean beneath the ice crust
([Iess et al. 2014](literatur:iess-2014); [Thomas et al. 2016](literatur:thomas-2016)), whose floor
is probably hydrothermally active, judging from tiny silicate grains and molecular hydrogen
detected in the plume ([Hsu et al. 2015](literatur:hsu-2015);
[Waite et al. 2017](literatur:waite-2017)). The heat for this comes from a 2:1 orbital resonance
with [Dione](objekt:dione), which keeps Enceladus's orbit permanently slightly eccentric. Enceladus
therefore ranks, like Europa in the Jupiter system, among the most promising targets in the search
for life beyond Earth. This text presents the gravity field and interior, the surface, the
atmosphere and magnetosphere, and the orbit, rotation and formation, and finally describes what
Orrery renders of it.

## Parameters and measurement

Three close Cassini flybys in 2010 and 2012 measured Enceladus's gravity field by radio-Doppler
tracking. Assuming hydrostatic equilibrium, this yielded a model with a porous silicate core of
roughly 180 to 185 km radius, an ocean above it, and, on top, an ice shell 30 to 40 km thick, with a
regional thinning confined to the south pole down to an ocean depth of about 10 km
([Iess et al. 2014](literatur:iess-2014)). McKinnon pointed out that Enceladus rotates too fast to
adopt the hydrostatic-equilibrium assumption unchanged; once corrected, the same gravity field is
also consistent with a smaller, denser core (radius about 190 km, density about 2450 kg m⁻³) and an
ice shell about 50 km thick on global average but only about 30 km thick at the south pole
([McKinnon 2015](literatur:mckinnon-2015)). A joint analysis of gravity, shape and libration data
narrowed the south-polar ice shell further, to under 5 km, with a global average of 18 to 22 km and
a core radius of 180 to 185 km, at a minimum ocean density of 1030 kg m⁻³
([Čadek et al. 2016](literatur:cadek-2016)). That the ocean is truly global rather than merely
regional was shown only by the physical libration: a control-point network built from seven years
of Cassini images gave a forced libration of $0.120^\circ \pm 0.014^\circ$ (2σ) – too large a
swing for the core to be rigidly connected to the surface, and one that rules out a purely local
south-polar sea ([Thomas et al. 2016](literatur:thomas-2016); classification alongside all moons
under [Bound rotation](thema:gebundene-rotation)).

## Interior

The core radius derived from gravity and shape (roughly 180 to 190 km, depending on the analysis)
leaves open whether the body is porous and partially water-saturated or a fully compacted rocky
body; the choice between the two models changes how deep the ocean reaches and how thick the ice
shell is, without changing the measured gravity field itself (see above). The interior of
[Mimas](objekt:mimas) remains similarly unclear: there, one of the amplitudes measured from
libration images fits only a strongly non-hydrostatic body or one with its own, thinner ocean – a
finding that shows how uncertain the separation between a porous core and an ocean remains for the
mid-sized Saturnian moons in general.

Hydrothermal activity itself needs not just an ocean but a heat source at its floor. A model that
places the tidal dissipation not in the ice shell but in a permeable, uncompacted rocky core finds
locally concentrated hydrothermal circulation there, of the kind seen at terrestrial deep-sea
vents, and can thereby explain the heat output observed at the south pole even though the tidal
heating averaged over the whole moon would be too small on its own
([Choblet et al. 2017](literatur:choblet-2017)). Exactly where the tidal heat is released is itself
unclear: a model of the ice shell itself instead suggests that a substantial part of the
dissipation occurs near its base, where the heat loss carried away by convection even exceeds the
heat radiated through the plumes, while remaining overall consistent with a steady state
([Hemingway and Mittal 2019](literatur:hemingway-2019)). Direct observational support for a heat
source at the core floor comes from tiny, crystalline silicate grains in the E ring: they probably
form when hot ocean water, at least 90 °C, reacts with rock at the core and precipitates
nanosilica, which then escapes with the plumes ([Hsu et al. 2015](literatur:hsu-2015)). How much
heat tidal friction supplies overall, and how it is distributed among core, ocean and ice shell, is
presented under [Tides and the Roche limit](thema:gezeiten); more on the moment-of-inertia factor
and core formation in moons in general is under [Interior structure](thema:innerer-aufbau).

## Surface

Four parallel fracture zones – the "tiger stripes" – lie at the south pole, from which plumes of
water vapour and ice grains shoot hundreds of kilometres high at about 400 m/s; their discovery was
first made by Cassini's camera in 2005 ([Porco et al. 2006](literatur:porco-2006)). A thermal
mapping of the same region with the CIRS infrared spectrometer simultaneously found a heat flow of
3 to 7 GW from the trenches themselves, at temperatures up to 145 K
([Spencer et al. 2006](literatur:spencer-2006)); a later analysis over a considerably broader
wavenumber range of the same Cassini/CIRS data found a substantially higher total for the whole
south polar region ([Howett et al. 2011](literatur:howett-2011)) – the peer-reviewed overall range
from several analyses runs from 4 to 19 GW
([Tides and the Roche limit](thema:gezeiten)). At the north pole, Cassini measurements in winter
and in summer additionally show an unexpected heat flux of $46 \pm 4\,\mathrm{mW\,m^{-2}}$; taken
together, this should amount to at most 54 GW, compared with an estimated 50 to 55 GW of tidal
heating in equilibrium, whose uncertainty exceeds 100 %
([Miles et al. 2025](literatur:miles-2025)).

Away from the active south pole, Enceladus shows old, heavily cratered plains as well as young,
practically crater-free terrain; the old plains themselves carry signs of tectonic overprinting and
viscous crater relaxation that complicate any simple age classification from crater counts alone
([Kirchoff and Schenk 2009](literatur:kirchoff-2009)). How old the moon and its ocean actually are
remains disputed (see Open questions).

## Atmosphere and magnetosphere

Enceladus carries no permanent atmosphere, but an extensive, episodically active plume. A stellar
occultation with the ultraviolet spectrograph UVIS confirmed in 2005 a regionally confined
water-vapour plume at the south pole ([Hansen et al. 2006](literatur:hansen-2006)); the mass
spectrometer INMS measured its composition as predominantly water vapour with a few percent of
carbon dioxide, methane and further trace gases ([Waite et al. 2006](literatur:waite-2006)). Plume
activity follows the orbit: its opening and closing follows the changing tidal stress along the
tiger stripes ([Hurford et al. 2007](literatur:hurford-2007)), and a decade-long Cassini record of
the plume's total brightness correlates with orbital phase, with a time lag that is not yet fully
explained ([Hedman et al. 2013a](literatur:hedman-2013a)).

The plume's chemical composition carries direct clues to the ocean beneath: sodium and potassium
salts in E-ring ice grains demonstrate contact between ocean water and rock
([Postberg et al. 2009](literatur:postberg-2009)); macromolecular organic compounds of more than
200 atomic mass units point to complex chemistry in the ocean or at its floor
([Postberg et al. 2018](literatur:postberg-2018)); phosphates, concentrated at least a hundredfold
more than in Earth's oceans, supply a nutrient that is often otherwise limiting for possible life
([Postberg et al. 2023](literatur:postberg-2023)); and molecular hydrogen in the plume is taken as
evidence of ongoing serpentinisation or similar hydrothermal reactions that could supply chemical
energy of the kind used by terrestrial methanogens ([Waite et al. 2017](literatur:waite-2017)). The
ejected material continuously feeds Saturn's E ring ([Spahn et al. 2006](literatur:spahn-2006);
structure and extent under [Ring systems](thema:ringe)); an observation with the James Webb Space
Telescope found in 2023 a far larger water-vapour torus, tracing Enceladus's entire orbit, that the
plume feeds ([Villanueva et al. 2023a](literatur:villanueva-2023a)).

## Orbit, rotation and dynamics

Enceladus and [Dione](objekt:dione) stand in a 2:1 orbital resonance with the resonant angle
$2\lambda_\mathrm{Di} - \lambda_\mathrm{En} - \varpi_\mathrm{En}$, whose libration stays below
$1^\circ$ ([Ćuk et al. 2024](literatur:cuk-2024); derivation and classification alongside all
orbital resonances in the Solar System under [Orbital resonances](thema:resonanzen)). The resonance
keeps Enceladus's orbit permanently slightly eccentric: for their resonance argument, Ćuk et al.
adopt an eccentricity of about $0.0047$, held in balance by resonance forcing and tidal damping
([Ćuk et al. 2024](literatur:cuk-2024)); without this constant drive, tidal friction would
circularise the orbit within a short time and switch off the heating itself – just as at Europa,
whose forced eccentricity is sustained by the resonance with Io and Ganymede.

Enceladus rotates synchronously, with a measured libration of $0.120^\circ$ (see above) that
requires an ice shell decoupled from the core above a global ocean. Because the resonance drives
only the eccentricity, not the rotation itself, the same tidal friction that keeps the ocean warm
also brakes the spin in parallel towards the synchronous state – details and comparison with all
other moons under [Bound rotation](thema:gebundene-rotation).

## Formation and evolution

How old Enceladus and its ocean are is one of the biggest open questions in the Saturn system.
Numerical integrations of the present-day orbital resonances of the mid-sized Saturnian moons,
including Enceladus–Dione, favour a comparatively young system: the moons may have rearranged
themselves only about 100 million years ago after a preceding collision
([Ćuk et al. 2016](literatur:cuk-2016)). Set against this are the heavily cratered old plains
described above, which would rather suggest an age close to that of the Saturn system itself if
their crater density had grown undisturbed
([Kirchoff and Schenk 2009](literatur:kirchoff-2009)); independently of this, how long the ocean
itself has already been liquid remains its own, unresolved question.

Two mission concepts aim to reach the ocean directly in the future. The Orbilander concept,
developed as part of a planetary-science decadal study, would first orbit Enceladus and then land
at the south pole, to study plume material from orbit and directly from the surface over an
extended mission – in the study team's assessment, the best balance between scientific return and
available resources ([MacKenzie et al. 2021](literatur:mackenzie-2021)). Until such a mission
flies, [Titan](objekt:titan), with the Dragonfly mission planned for 2028, remains the next target
in the Saturn system.

## Open questions

- **How old are Enceladus and its ocean?** The present-day orbital resonances of the mid-sized
  moons favour a young system no more than a few hundred million years old
  ([Ćuk et al. 2016](literatur:cuk-2016)); the heavily cratered but tectonically overprinted old
  plains would also be compatible with a much older moon
  ([Kirchoff and Schenk 2009](literatur:kirchoff-2009)). Both lines of evidence rest on different
  clocks (orbital dynamics versus crater counts) and cannot yet be decided against each other.
- **Is tidal heating today in balance with heat loss?** The observed heat radiated from the south
  and north poles together is of the same order of magnitude as the modelled equilibrium heating,
  but with an uncertainty exceeding 100 %
  ([Miles et al. 2025](literatur:miles-2025); [Tides and the Roche limit](thema:gezeiten)); whether
  activity is currently increasing or decreasing therefore remains open.
- **Why are the tiger stripes located exactly at the south pole?** That plume activity follows the
  changing tidal stress is well established
  ([Hurford et al. 2007](literatur:hurford-2007)); why the active zone formed there and not
  elsewhere on the ice shell depends on the particular thermal-orbital evolution model adopted and
  is not conclusively settled ([Nimmo et al. 2018](literatur:nimmo-2018)).
- **A porous or a fully differentiated core?** Without an independent measurement of the moment of
  inertia, gravity data alone cannot decide between a porous, partially water-saturated core and a
  compacted rocky one (see Parameters and measurement); the answer also determines how long
  hydrothermal circulation at the floor can be sustained.
- **Is Enceladus habitable?** Liquid water, chemical energy from serpentinisation, and a nutrient
  that is otherwise often limiting, in the form of phosphates, would coincide there
  ([Waite et al. 2017](literatur:waite-2017); [Postberg et al. 2023](literatur:postberg-2023));
  whether the conditions are actually sufficient is a question only a future mission such as
  Orbilander can settle ([MacKenzie et al. 2021](literatur:mackenzie-2021)).

## In the model

- **Orbital reference:** Like the other Saturnian moons, Enceladus moves in the `parentEquator`
  reference on Saturn's equatorial plane, with osculating Horizons elements at epoch J2000
  ($a = 238420\,\mathrm{km}$, $e = 0.00635$). `nodeDot` is fixed at 0 because the JPL mean-elements
  table gives no precession rate for Enceladus's node (nodal period 0 years) – the same special
  case as for Io among the Jovian moons.
- **Momentary value versus forced eccentricity:** The catalogue carries, with $e = 0.00635$,
  Horizons's osculating eccentricity at the single epoch J2000, not the value $0.0047$ held in
  balance by the resonance mentioned above: the snapshot adds to the forced component a free
  oscillation, normally damped by tidal friction, which `eDot = 0` freezes forever instead of
  letting it decay slowly, as it really does. JPL's own orbital-element table, averaged over
  decades, in turn gives $e = 0.005$ for the same quantity, a value between both timescales.
- **Apsidal period:** From `lpDot` ($12345.679^\circ$ per century) follows
  $360/12345.679 \times 100 = 2.916$ years – exactly the apsidal precession period JPL's
  orbital-element table gives for Enceladus, so this mainly confirms the calculation by which
  `lpDot` itself was derived from that table.
- **Orbital period of the data set:** From $\dot L$ ($9596283.219167^\circ$ per century) follows
  $36525/(\dot L/360) = 1.370218$ days – exactly the value given by JPL's orbital-element table.
  The data panel instead shows the Kepler orbital period computed from the semi-major axis and the
  masses (`umlaufzeitTage`), $1.374613$ days, about 3207 ppm longer.
- **Rotation ratio:** `rotationPeriodH` ($32.88523\,\mathrm{h}$) matches the sidereal period
  computed from $\dot L$ to $-0.061 \cdot 10^{-6}$ relative; the angle between the map centre and
  the direction to Saturn is $136.2^\circ$ at the epoch and $135.8^\circ$ after a hundred years
  (figures as in [Bound rotation](thema:gebundene-rotation)).
- **Axial tilt:** Recomputing with `achsneigungDeg` gives $0.019^\circ$ – Enceladus's orbit thus
  lies almost exactly in Saturn's equatorial plane.
- **Pole:** $40.66^\circ / 83.52^\circ$ from the IAU rotation elements, fixed at the epoch.
- **Albedo, texture and map:** The catalogue albedo of 1.0 is the NSSDC fact sheet's geometric
  albedo and therefore lies below the more recent measurements mentioned above (1.24); the texture
  itself averages only 0.172 linear – normalised by the factor 5.80, the rendered surface reaches
  the catalogue albedo on average ([Albedo and brightness](thema:photometrie)). Plumes, the E ring
  and a dedicated heat map are entirely absent from the model.
- **Dampened brightness, cause found:** The comment on the scene
  [Enceladus in bright light](szene:enceladus-hell) in `scenes.ts` notes an "overall dampened
  brightness" of the Saturnian moons without naming the cause. It lies neither in the texture nor
  in the lighting direction, but in the scattering model itself: Lambertian scattering times
  $1 - F$ (the Fresnel factor) reaches, recomputed without specular highlight and fill light, only
  the geometric albedo $0.640\,p$ instead of $p$ – for Enceladus with $p = 1.0$, thus 0.64 instead
  of 1.0 (see above); the same factor affects every body in the catalogue equally, not just
  Enceladus or the other Saturnian moons.
- **Shadows:** Enceladus is not among the four occluders of Saturn chosen by `waehleOkkluder`
  (Titan, Tethys, Dione and Rhea have a larger angular radius as seen from Saturn); in the model it
  therefore never casts a shadow on Saturn, although its real orbit would leave room for this,
  computationally, with a threshold of
  $\arcsin(R_\mathrm{Saturn}/a_\mathrm{Enceladus}) = 14.14^\circ$ – well below Saturn's axial tilt
  of $26.73^\circ$ (figures as in [Eclipses](thema:finsternis)). Seen from Enceladus itself,
  however, Saturn is always used as an occluder, together with up to three of the other Saturnian
  moons and Saturn's ring: Enceladus is indeed shadowed, it simply never casts a shadow of its own.
- **Scale:** Enceladus is a satellite (`isSatellite`, its `parent` is Saturn, not the Sun); its
  orbital radius around Saturn therefore scales, like its own and Saturn's radius, with
  `sizeScale` rather than with the distance compression applied to planetary orbits
  (`scaledPositionAt`).
- **Data panel:** The mass carried in the data set ($1.08 \cdot 10^{20}\,\mathrm{kg}$), multiplied
  by $G$ from CODATA 2018, gives a $GM$ of $7.2082\,\mathrm{km^3\,s^{-2}}$, about 295 ppm below the
  value measured by orbit tracking in the table above. Further simplifications:
  [Limits of the model](thema:modell).
