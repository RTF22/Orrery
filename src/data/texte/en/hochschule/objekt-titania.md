# Titania

At 788.9 km in radius, Titania is the largest of the five large moons of Uranus, and at
3.42·10²¹ kg also the most massive – ahead of the only slightly smaller [Oberon](objekt:oberon)
(761.4 km) and well ahead of the three inner moons Miranda, Ariel, and Umbriel. During Voyager 2's
single flyby on 24 January 1986, the probe saw only Titania's sunlit southern hemisphere as well
([Smith et al. 1986](literatur:smith-1986)); since then, ground- and space-based telescopes, most
recently Hubble and the James Webb Space Telescope, together with a single stellar occultation,
have provided the only new data. This text covers parameters and measurement, interior, surface,
atmosphere and magnetosphere, and orbit, rotation, and formation, and finally describes what
Orrery models of it.

## Parameters and measurement

Like Umbriel and Oberon, Titania appears spherical within measurement uncertainty in the
Voyager 2 limb-coordinate surveys ([Thomas 1988](literatur:thomas-1988)); a single radius value
suffices. The catalogue value of 788.9 km comes from this photogrammetric analysis and was later
checked independently by the only stellar occultation by Titania observed to date: on
8 September 2001, the moon occulted the Hipparcos star SAO 164538, observed from more than a
hundred stations on three continents; of the 57 chords observed, the best 27 were used to derive
the radius, yielding 788.4 ± 0.6 km
([Widemann et al. 2009](literatur:widemann-2009)) – 0.5 km smaller than the catalogue value, but
not statistically significant given the combined uncertainties of 1.8 km (Voyager) and 0.6 km
(occultation). As with the other moons, Titania's mass parameter comes from a single flyby
trajectory plus decades of ground-based astrometry ([Jacobson 2014](literatur:jacobson-2014)).

With the catalogue mass ($3.42 \cdot 10^{21}\,\mathrm{kg}$) and the radius of 788.9 km, the density
works out to about $1.663\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ (derivation) – matching the NSSDC fact-sheet value;
the density computed from $GM$ and radius in the table above, $1.653\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, rests
on a slightly different, more recent mass solution, as with [Ariel](objekt:ariel) (1.54 versus
1.59) and [Umbriel](objekt:umbriel) (1.52 versus 1.46 g/cm³). Titania is thus the densest of the
five large Uranian moons, just ahead of Ariel (1587 kg/m³), Oberon (1558 kg/m³), and Umbriel
(1457 kg/m³), and well ahead of Miranda (1204 kg/m³) – a hint at the highest rock fraction among
the five (see Interior). With a visual albedo of 0.27, Titania sits between the brighter Ariel
(0.39) and the darker Umbriel (0.21) – the same ranking that HST multi-band photometry confirms
independently ([Karkoschka 2001](literatur:karkoschka-2001)). Because
Voyager 2 saw only the southern hemisphere, all surface values apply to a good half of the moon.

## Interior

In the same simplified two-component model of water ice ($917\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$) and silicate
rock ($3000\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, as for [Miranda](objekt:miranda), [Ariel](objekt:ariel),
[Umbriel](objekt:umbriel), and [Iapetus](objekt:iapetus)), Titania's catalogue density corresponds
to a rock mass fraction of about 65% (derivation) – the highest among the five large Uranian moons
(Miranda one third, Umbriel 54%, Ariel about 60%). Whether the body is fully differentiated cannot be
decided without a dedicated gravity-field measurement (see
[Interior structure](thema:innerer-aufbau)). Interior-structure models for all five large Uranian
moons rank Titania together with Oberon as the two most promising candidates for a residual ocean,
mainly for their size and correspondingly greater heat-retention capacity
([Castillo-Rogez et al. 2023](literatur:castillo-rogez-2023)); an independent heat budget agrees:
unlike Ariel and Umbriel, radiogenic heating alone suffices for Titania and Oberon to keep enough
heat for a long-lived ocean over billions of years
([Bierson and Nimmo 2022](literatur:bierson-2022)). A present-day ocean is thus more likely at
Titania than at the three smaller moons, though unconfirmed.

One possible direct test would be measuring the secondary field induced by Uranus's time-varying
magnetic field during a flyby, as computed jointly for all five large moons
($>2\,\mathrm{S\,m^{-1}}$ conductivity, layer thickness of 0.4 to 6 km, even under an ice shell up
to 50 km thick, a signal above the roughly 1 nT sensitivity typical of today's magnetometers,
[Weiss et al. 2021](literatur:weiss-2021)). A newer, independent approach looks for the same
signal instead in libration and precession with non-vanishing obliquity of a synchronously
rotating moon, both measurably larger for a liquid interior layer than for a fully rigid body –
equally applicable to Titania once a spacecraft measures its rotation at close range
([Baland et al. 2025](literatur:baland-2025)). Direct evidence of past interior heat comes from the
surface itself: flexure at the margins of Messina Chasmata (see Surface) implies a heat flux of
5 to 12 mW/m², assuming a lithosphere of pure, pore-free water ice – lower than comparable
graben-derived values at Miranda or Ariel, pointing to overall lower, but not vanishing, interior
activity; if Messina is indeed geologically young, as cross-cutting relationships with older
craters suggest, that points to an additional heat source besides radiogenic heating, possibly
tidal heat from an earlier episode (see Orbit, rotation and dynamics)
([Beddingfield et al. 2023](literatur:beddingfield-2023)).

## Surface

Titania's surface shows both old, densely cratered terrain and extensive tectonic overprinting:
its most striking feature is Messina Chasmata, a system of broad graben bounded by scarps that
extends across a substantial part of the imaged hemisphere
([Smith et al. 1986](literatur:smith-1986)). One puzzle in the crater statistics is a marked lack
of large craters (over 30 km in diameter); a recent reprocessing of the Voyager 2 images, which
substantially reduces the motion blur of the original frames, additionally reveals a network of
finer fractures, a possible multi-ring structure, and previously unmapped crater chains, and
attributes the missing large craters to viscous relaxation, tectonic overprinting, or infalling
planetocentric debris, without requiring cryovolcanism
([Nathan et al. 2024](literatur:nathan-2024)). Crater number densities rank Titania together with
Miranda, Umbriel, and Oberon among the older surfaces in the system, markedly older than
neighbouring Ariel ([Kirchoff et al. 2022](literatur:kirchoff-2022)); an earlier analysis of the
same Voyager images, based on Umbriel, Titania, and Oberon alone, already reached the same
conclusion ([Plescia 1987](literatur:plescia-1987)) – the tectonic overprinting above thus affects
only part of the surface, not its overall age.

Spectroscopically, Titania carries less CO2 ice than Umbriel and markedly less than Ariel, while
Oberon shows none ([Cartwright et al. 2015](literatur:cartwright-2015)); as with Ariel and Umbriel,
it concentrates on the trailing hemisphere, while the leading hemisphere shows deeper water-ice
bands ([Grundy et al. 2006](literatur:grundy-2006)).

## Atmosphere and magnetosphere

There is no evidence for an atmosphere on Titania: the same 2001 stellar occultation that yielded
the radius sets an upper limit on the surface pressure of about 10 to 20 nanobar for CO2 and
related gases – roughly a hundred-millionth of Earth's atmospheric pressure
([Widemann et al. 2009](literatur:widemann-2009)); as for the other four large Uranian moons, mass
and surface temperature are insufficient to retain even CO2 as a gas over the long term.

With a mean orbital radius of 436,293 km, Titania orbits relatively close to Uranus's magnetopause,
but how close depends strongly on the solar-wind pressure. The only measurement comes from
Voyager 2 in January 1986; a more recent reanalysis shows the probe happened to encounter Uranus
during an unusually compressed state (dayside shrunk by about 78%, occurring only about 4% of the
time): the subsolar magnetopause distance measured then was about 17.3 Uranus radii (roughly
442,000 km, precision range 17.17 to 17.51 Uranus radii, or roughly 439,000 to 448,000 km) –
leaving only about 2,600 km of margin over Titania's own orbital radius at the low end of that
range, and about 5,900 km at the midpoint. Under typical
conditions, the magnetopause distance instead averages 22.2 Uranus radii (roughly 567,000 km,
middle range 20 to 25 Uranus radii) – considerably farther out
([Jasinski et al. 2024](literatur:jasinski-2024)). Titania therefore orbits inside the
magnetosphere even during the unusual Voyager encounter, though with a much narrower safety margin
than under typical conditions.

## Orbit, rotation and dynamics

Titania's osculating orbit, with $i = 179.899^\circ$, lies only $0.101^\circ$ from the degeneracy
at $180^\circ$ – closer to Uranus's equatorial plane than Miranda (4.34°), though not quite as
close as Ariel (0.003°) or Umbriel (0.056°); the NSSDC fact sheet instead gives an orbital
inclination of 0.08°, a small, unresolved discrepancy of 0.02° between the sources, smaller than
for Umbriel (0.07°) or Miranda (0.09°) (convention as under
[Orbital elements](thema:bahnelemente)). Like all 21 moons covered by the model, Titania rotates
synchronously, with a residual deviation of only $0.019 \cdot 10^{-6}$ between rotation and orbital
period; the direction to Uranus consequently drifts from 31.9° at epoch to 31.6° after a hundred
years – one of the smaller librations among the five (numbers as under
[Tidal locking](thema:gebundene-rotation)).

Titania is not currently in a mean-motion resonance with a neighbouring moon; unlike the three
inner moons, it was also not directly involved in a resonance crossing under either of the two
competing pictures of the system's orbital history. Both the classical sequence
Miranda–Umbriel, Miranda–Ariel, and Ariel–Umbriel
([Tittemore and Wisdom 1990](literatur:tittemore-1990)) and the newer, system-wide modelling that
places the Ariel–Umbriel 5:3 resonance at the centre of events
([Ćuk et al. 2020](literatur:cuk-2020)) confine the actual resonance crossings to the four inner
moons; only Oberon's spin axis, under the newer modelling, lies close to its own spin–orbit
resonance with Umbriel's orbital plane, with no role for Titania
([Ćuk et al. 2020](literatur:cuk-2020); basics under
[Orbital resonances](thema:resonanzen)). If Titania's own, geologically younger surface features
(see Surface) are indeed due to tidal heating, they would need a different or additional episode
than the one reconstructed for Ariel and Umbriel (see
[Tides and the Roche limit](thema:gezeiten)).

## Formation and evolution

Like the other regular moons of the giant planets, Titania probably formed in a debris disk around
Uranus, whether after a giant impact that could also explain the planet's extreme axial tilt, or
through orderly co-accretion with the planet itself (numbers and discussion under
[Uranus](objekt:uranus) and [Axial tilt](thema:achsneigung)). The resonance crossings reconstructed
for the inner moons (see Orbit, rotation and dynamics) do not directly explain Titania's present
state; but its size and higher rock fraction (see Interior) suggest radiogenic heat alone could
have sufficed for early interior activity and the later tectonic overprinting by Messina Chasmata,
without requiring a resonance ([Bierson and Nimmo 2022](literatur:bierson-2022)). Uranus and its
moon system formed within the broader formation history of the outer Solar System, in which
Neptune also migrated outward (context under
[Formation of the Solar System](thema:entstehung)).

## Open questions

- **Does Titania have a liquid ocean today?** Interior and heat-budget models rank Titania with
  Oberon as the most promising candidate among the five large Uranian moons
  ([Castillo-Rogez et al. 2023](literatur:castillo-rogez-2023);
  [Bierson and Nimmo 2022](literatur:bierson-2022)); whether an original ocean is still liquid or
  has long since frozen is undecided.
- **How old is Messina Chasmata?** Cross-cutting relationships with older craters suggest a
  relatively young structure, but no absolute age has been determined
  ([Beddingfield et al. 2023](literatur:beddingfield-2023); [Nathan et al. 2024](literatur:nathan-2024)).
- **Northern hemisphere unknown.** Voyager 2 saw only the sunlit southern hemisphere in 1986; no
  close-up images exist of the composition, crater density, or possible further graben systems of
  the northern hemisphere.
- **Would a present-day ocean be detectable by a future orbiter mission?** Both magnetic induction
  ([Weiss et al. 2021](literatur:weiss-2021)) and libration and obliquity measurements
  ([Baland et al. 2025](literatur:baland-2025)) are considered promising, but as yet untested,
  detection methods.

## In the model

- **Radius:** The data panel shows `radiusKm: 788.9`, the photogrammetric Voyager 2 value
  ([Thomas 1988](literatur:thomas-1988), also carried in the NSSDC fact sheet), not the smaller,
  more precise occultation value (788.4 km, [Widemann et al. 2009](literatur:widemann-2009), see
  Parameters and measurement) – kept so that all five large Uranian moons share the same source;
  the two values agree within their combined uncertainty, so volume and density barely change.
- **Orbital reference:** Like the other four large Uranian moons, Titania orbits in the
  `parentEquator` reference on [Uranus's](objekt:uranus) equatorial plane, with osculating Horizons
  elements at epoch J2000 ($a = 436292.68\,\mathrm{km}$, $e = 0.00248$, $i = 179.899^\circ$). The
  resulting distance from the degeneracy at $180^\circ$ (0.101°) departs slightly from the NSSDC
  fact-sheet value (0.08°) – a small, unresolved discrepancy of 0.02° between the sources, smaller
  than for the other moons (see above).
- **Node rate:** `nodeDot` is set to zero – as with the other three larger Uranian moons, a case of
  numerical degeneracy of the node at $i \approx 180^\circ$. Unlike Umbriel and Oberon, whose
  osculating Horizons node drifts steadily over the decades, Titania's reverses direction after
  about 25 years (numbers as under [Orbital elements](thema:bahnelemente)) – the model holds it at
  the mean of this oscillation with `nodeDot = 0`, rather than extrapolating either direction.
- **Apsidal rate:** `lpDot` ($40.1875^\circ$ per century) follows the two-term project formula
  $(360/P_\mathrm{apsis} - 360/P_\mathrm{node}) \cdot 100$ with the JPL table values
  $P_\mathrm{apsis} = 579.928\,\mathrm{a}$ and $P_\mathrm{node} = 1644.649\,\mathrm{a}$:
  $(360/579.928 - 360/1644.649) \cdot 100 = 40.1875$ (recalculation matches the dataset value
  exactly).
- **Orbital period:** From `LDot`, $360^\circ/\dot L = 8.705869\,\mathrm{d}$; the data panel shows
  the Kepler orbital period computed from the semi-major axis and both masses, about
  $8.70631\,\mathrm{d}$, roughly 0.0051% longer. `rotationPeriodH` ($-208.94086\,\mathrm{h}$)
  matches $360^\circ/\dot L \cdot 24$ almost exactly – the synchronous rotation from Orbit, rotation
  and dynamics above.
- **Pole:** $257.43^\circ / -15.10^\circ$ is fixed in the dataset and is – as for Ariel, Umbriel,
  and Oberon – a simplification; only Miranda carries a slightly different declination. The data
  panel's axial tilt (`achsneigungDeg`, the angle between this pole and the orbital normal built
  from position and velocity at epoch, flipped for a negative rotation period) is $0.137^\circ$
  (recalculation) – between Ariel's $0.140^\circ$ and Umbriel's $0.182^\circ$, well below Miranda's
  $4.334^\circ$.
- **Texture:** Titania carries no texture, but a fallback colour approximating the fact-sheet
  albedo: as for the other four large Uranian moons, the only available USGS/JPL map mosaic
  consists, because of the one-sided Voyager illumination, to a substantial extent of unlit area
  (`ASSETS.md`); Messina Chasmata itself is not rendered as a separate feature.
- **Shadows:** Titania is among the up to four spherical occluders drawn simultaneously
  (`MAX_OKKLUDER`) when it has the largest rendered angular radius among the Uranian moons; among
  the five large moons, Oberon, being the smallest, is always the one dropped (numbers as under
  [Eclipses](thema:finsternis)).
- **Scale:** Titania is a satellite (`isSatellite`, `parent` is Uranus, not the Sun); its orbital
  radius therefore scales like its own and Uranus's radius with `sizeScale`, not with the distance
  compression used for planetary orbits. Further simplifications:
  [Limits of the model](thema:modell).
