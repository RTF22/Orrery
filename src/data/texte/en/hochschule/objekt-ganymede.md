# Ganymede

Ganymede is the third of the four Galilean moons by distance and the largest, and, at a volumetric mean radius of
2631.2 km, the largest moon in the Solar System, larger than the planet [Mercury](objekt:mercury)
(derivation below). Together with [Io](objekt:io) and [Europa](objekt:europa) it stands in the
Laplace resonance around [Jupiter](objekt:jupiter); as the outermost link of the chain, the resonance
barely heats it today. Instead, Ganymede is the only known moon with its own magnetic field generated
by a dynamo, embedded in Jupiter's far larger field, and a salty ocean is suspected beneath its ice
crust. This text presents the gravity field and interior, the surface, the atmosphere and
magnetosphere, and the orbit, rotation and formation, and finally describes what Orrery renders of it.

## Parameters and measurement

Two flybys of the Galileo spacecraft delivered Ganymede's first own gravity field in 1996 from the
Doppler tracking of its orbit; assuming hydrostatic equilibrium, this yielded a layered structure of
a dense, metallic core, a rock mantle and an outer, water- and ice-rich shell
([Anderson et al. 1996](literatur:anderson-1996)). How pronounced this layering actually is remained
open until two close flybys of the Juno spacecraft during its extended mission were reanalysed
together with the Galileo data: a three-layer model of shells of equal volume gives a
moment-of-inertia factor of $C/(Ma^2) = 0.3159 \pm 0.0052$ (16th to 84th percentile), markedly higher
than the earlier value of $0.3105 \pm 0.0028$ estimated from Galileo data alone with the forced
hydrostatic relation $J_2/C_{22} = 10/3$ and Radau-Darwin; the higher mean points to a somewhat less
differentiated Ganymede, while the larger uncertainty accounts for non-hydrostatic contributions
([Gomez Casajus et al. 2022](literatur:gomez-casajus-2022)).

With this volumetric mean radius, Ganymede is the largest moon in the Solar System
([Jupiter moons at NASA](quelle:nasa-jupitermonde)): its diameter of 5262.4 km exceeds that of the
planet [Mercury](objekt:mercury), 4879.4 km at a radius of 2439.7 km, by nearly 8%. Its mass, however,
reaches only about 45% of Mercury's mass; the resulting mean density of 1941.6 kg/m³ is markedly lower
than Mercury's and fits a body made of roughly equal parts rock and water ice.

## Interior

The 1996 Doppler orbit data require, for a model in hydrostatic equilibrium, the layered structure of
core, mantle and ice shell ([Anderson et al. 1996](literatur:anderson-1996)). In the same year, the
magnetometer on the Galileo spacecraft discovered that Ganymede, still the only known moon to do so,
carries its own magnetic field generated by a dynamo
([Kivelson et al. 1996](literatur:kivelson-1996)); it sits inside Jupiter's far larger field and
produces aurorae of its own (see below). A dynamo requires motion in an electrically conducting,
at least partly liquid core; a model in which iron crystallises in the outer core and sinks as snow
could supply the convection needed for this without the whole core having to be liquid
([Christensen 2015](literatur:christensen-2015)). Juno flybys have since refined the derived moments
of the dipole field, still best described by a simple dipole
([Weber et al. 2022](literatur:weber-2022)); how this dynamo is actually driven, and how long-lived
it is, remains open (see below).

Several Galileo flybys crossed Jupiter's background field in different orientations; the resulting
time-varying additional, induced field component requires a well-conducting, contiguous layer near
the surface — evidence for an ocean sandwiched between two ice layers, the outer ordinary ice I and a
deeper high-pressure ice, in which dissolved salts make the water conductive
([Kivelson et al. 2002](literatur:kivelson-2002)), much as at [Europa](objekt:europa). Independent
confirmation came from the Hubble Space Telescope in 2015: Ganymede's auroral ovals rock back and
forth with the daily change in Jupiter's field direction, but an ocean damps this rocking because it
shields part of the external forcing. The observed amplitude was $2.0^\circ \pm 1.3^\circ$, close to
the $2.2^\circ \pm 1.3^\circ$ predicted with an ocean and well below the $5.8^\circ \pm 1.3^\circ$
predicted without one; this implies a minimum conductivity of $0.09\,\mathrm{S\,m^{-1}}$ and an
ocean top no deeper than 330 km, consistent with a depth between 150 and 250 km
([Saur et al. 2015](literatur:saur-2015)). General thermal modelling of medium-sized icy moons
predicts similar oceans beneath ice shells elsewhere in the outer Solar System as well
([Hussmann et al. 2006](literatur:hussmann-2006)).

Unlike the similarly sized but only partially differentiated Saturnian moon [Titan](objekt:titan)
(moment-of-inertia factor $0.343 \pm 0.001$), Ganymede's lower value points to markedly stronger
differentiation; the classification of all measured moment-of-inertia factors is given under
[Interior structure](thema:innerer-aufbau).

## Surface

Ganymede's surface falls into two major landscape types: dark, old and heavily cratered terrain such
as Galileo Regio on one side, and brighter, younger terrain crossed by parallel grooves and ridges on
the other, formed by stretching of the ice crust and regionally overprinting the dark terrain; the
global geological map relies on crater densities to order the relative ages of both units
([Patterson et al. 2010](literatur:patterson-2010)). Photogeological analyses of individual bright
regions also show signs of smooth, low-viscosity flooding more consistent with flowing water ice than
with crustal stretching alone ([Schenk et al. 2001](literatur:schenk-2001)); what ultimately triggered
the formation of the bright terrain is still not conclusively settled (see below).

Bright frost material covers the polar caps: energetic charged particles from Jupiter's magnetosphere
preferentially strike the surface where Ganymede's own field does not shield it (see below). In 2024
the James Webb Space Telescope detected carbon dioxide in the near infrared in Ganymede's thin
exosphere, with the highest column density found over the north polar cap
([Bockelée-Morvan et al. 2024](literatur:bockelee-morvan-2024)). On 7 June 2021 the Juno spacecraft
flew past Ganymede at a distance of about 1050 km, the closest flyby since Galileo, returning new
close-up images of the surface.

## Atmosphere and magnetosphere

Ganymede carries an extremely thin oxygen atmosphere produced by radiolysis, as does
[Europa](objekt:europa): energetic particles and photons split water ice at the surface, the light
hydrogen escapes and the heavier oxygen accumulates. High-resolution Hubble Space Telescope spectra
also revealed water vapour: the ratio of the observed oxygen emission lines requires a contribution
from the dissociative excitation of H₂O near the subsolar point, with a column density of about
$10^{15}$ molecules per cm²; away from there, a pure O₂ atmosphere fits alone. Over the warmer
trailing hemisphere the H₂O/O₂ ratio is higher than over the colder leading hemisphere, and the
concentration near the subsolar point points to sublimation of the surface ice as the source of the
water vapour ([Roth et al. 2021](literatur:roth-2021)).

Because Ganymede generates a dipole field of its own, it carves out its own small magnetosphere
within Jupiter's vast one — the only moon in the Solar System to do so. Closed field lines shield part
of the surface around the equator from bombardment by Jupiter's plasma, while at the open field lines
of the poles particles penetrate unhindered; it is precisely there, where this shielding is absent,
that both the bright frost material and the carbon dioxide detected by JWST are most concentrated
(see above). The auroral ovals responding to Jupiter's field, and their damped oscillation, at the
same time provide the evidence for the ocean beneath the crust (see above).

## Orbit, rotation and dynamics

Io, Europa and Ganymede satisfy, on average, the Laplace resonance with the mean-motion ratio 4:2:1;
the derivation, the two-body angles and the forced orbital eccentricity of Io are
presented under [Orbital resonances](thema:resonanzen), that of Europa under [Bound rotation](thema:gebundene-rotation). As the outermost link of the chain, Ganymede
carries the smallest of the three forced eccentricities and is correspondingly barely heated by tides
today; unlike [Io](objekt:io) with its ongoing volcanism, this shows up on Ganymede's surface only as
a past episode (see below). How stable the whole system remains over the long term is presented under
[Tides and the Roche limit](thema:gezeiten). Ganymede also appears in the scene
[Galilean shadow play](szene:galileisches-schattenspiel), where the three inner Galilean moons eclipse
and occult one another.

How the resonance itself arose is an open question of consequence for Ganymede's history: Showman and
Malhotra modelled the tidal evolution into the resonance and showed that passage through earlier
two-body resonances, in particular the 2:1 resonance between Ganymede and Europa, temporarily raised
Ganymede's orbital eccentricity to values that released enough tidal heat to melt and reshape the
bright, grooved terrain ([Showman and Malhotra 1997](literatur:showman-1997)). A coupled orbital-
thermal model linking this heating to the global expansion the icy body undergoes on melting confirms
that such a resonance passage can explain the observed expansion and tectonics of the bright terrain
([Bland et al. 2009](literatur:bland-2009)).

Ganymede rotates in a [tidally locked](thema:gebundene-rotation) state: the rotation period equals the
sidereal orbital period, so Ganymede permanently shows Jupiter the same face; figures for the model
are given below.

## Formation and evolution

The Galilean moons probably formed in a "starved" circumjovian gas disc that contained little solid
mass at any one time and was replenished over millions of years by inflowing material; this picture
fits best with the ice fraction that increases from the inside outward across the four moons and with
[Callisto](objekt:callisto)'s presumably only partially differentiated interior
([Nimmo et al. 2026](literatur:nimmo-2026)). Within this disc, Ganymede formed farther from Jupiter
than [Io](objekt:io) or [Europa](objekt:europa), cold enough for lasting water ice, and grew into the
largest of the four moons. The broader context of planet formation is presented under
[Formation of the Solar System](thema:entstehung).

**The Ganymede-Callisto dichotomy.** Why Ganymede is fully differentiated — iron in the core, rock in
the mantle, ice in the shell — while the only slightly smaller and similarly composed Callisto, judged
by its moment-of-inertia factor, probably remains only partially differentiated, is one of the major
open questions about the Galilean moons. Three explanations stand side by side. According to the
first, only the later capture into the Laplace resonance released enough tidal heat to melt Ganymede
completely, while Callisto, orbiting outside the resonance, stayed cold
([Showman and Malhotra 1997](literatur:showman-1997)). According to the second, the Late Heavy
Bombardment delivered different amounts of impact heat to the two moons: model calculations show that
the impact energy of that time sufficed to push Ganymede past the threshold for complete
differentiation, but not Callisto ([Barr and Canup 2010](literatur:barr-2010)). According to the
third, no additional event is needed at all: an accretion model that assumes a slow build-up lasting
more than two million years for both moons finds that differing disc temperatures and final radii at
their respective formation sites explain why Callisto can remain unmelted despite an impactor
contribution of up to 30% of its accreted mass, while Ganymede could still fully differentiate
([Bennacer et al. 2025](literatur:bennacer-2025)). Which of these explanations is correct — or whether
several acted together — is not decided.

Since April 2023 the European spacecraft JUICE has been under way to Jupiter, designed primarily for
Ganymede; after arriving in 2031 and a series of flybys of all three icy moons, current planning has
it enter orbit around Ganymede in 2034 — the first spacecraft to orbit a moon other than Earth's own
([Grasset et al. 2013](literatur:grasset-2013); [Juice (ESA)](quelle:esa-juice)).

## Open questions

- **The Ganymede-Callisto dichotomy:** resonance passage
  ([Showman and Malhotra 1997](literatur:showman-1997)), impacts during the Late Heavy Bombardment
  ([Barr and Canup 2010](literatur:barr-2010)), and differing disc temperatures and final radii under
  an otherwise similar, slow accretion history ([Bennacer et al. 2025](literatur:bennacer-2025)) stand
  side by side as explanations (see above).
- **The origin of the bright, grooved terrain:** the global geological map explains it mainly through
  tectonic stretching of the crust ([Patterson et al. 2010](literatur:patterson-2010)); photogeological
  evidence from individual terrains instead points to additional cryovolcanic flooding by low-viscosity
  water ice ([Schenk et al. 2001](literatur:schenk-2001)). Whether and where both processes acted
  together is open.
- **What drives the dynamo, and is it still active?** A model with iron crystallising and sinking in
  the outer core could supply the convection a dynamo needs, even if the core is not entirely liquid
  ([Christensen 2015](literatur:christensen-2015)); an independent estimate of the electrical
  conductivity of a molten iron-sulphur core, by contrast, finds ordinary thermal convection possible
  as well ([Littleton et al. 2021](literatur:littleton-2021)). Whether the dynamo is still active today
  or represents a field frozen in from an earlier time is likewise open.
- **How deep is the ocean, and how thick is the ice shell above it?** The damped oscillation of the
  auroral ovals gives an upper limit of 330 km for the top of the ocean, consistent with a depth
  between 150 and 250 km ([Saur et al. 2015](literatur:saur-2015)); a thermal model of ice-shell
  dynamics instead arrives at thicker ice shells of 50 to 200 km
  ([Rückriemen-Bez and Plesa 2025](literatur:rueckriemen-bez-2025)). An independent geodetic
  measurement of the ice-shell thickness, of the kind available for [Europa](objekt:europa), is still
  missing for Ganymede.

## In the model

- **Orbital reference:** Like the other Jovian moons, Ganymede moves in the `parentEquator` reference
  on Jupiter's equatorial plane (pole 268.057°/64.495° from [Jupiter](objekt:jupiter)). Unlike
  [Io](objekt:io) ($i = 0.0^\circ$), the orbital inclination $i = 0.2^\circ$ is not negligible:
  recomputing `positionInParentFrame` at the epoch shows that the node value (58.5°) genuinely
  determines the orientation — substituting a trial node of 0° shifts the position by about 3565 km,
  of which 3565 km is in $z$ but only 1.6 km in $x$ and 5.3 km in $y$. The eccentricity is fixed at
  $e = 0.001$ (`eDot` 0).
- **Nodal period:** From `nodeDot` ($-261.2254^\circ$ per century) follows a period of
  $360/261.2254 \times 100 = 137.812$ years, regressing; this matches exactly the "node precession
  period" of the underlying element table (header comment in `jupiter-monde.ts`), so it mainly
  confirms the calculation by which `nodeDot` itself was derived from that table.
- **Perijove period:** From $\dot\varpi$ ($265.8532^\circ$ per century) follows a period of
  $360/265.8532 \times 100 = 135.413$ years; this is considerably longer than the reciprocal of the
  table's separately listed "periapsis precession period" (68.301 years) would suggest, because the
  longitude of the perijove carries both nodal and apsidal precession together (figures as in
  [Orbital resonances](thema:resonanzen)).
- **Orbital period of the data set:** From $\dot L$ (1,837,850.666562° per century) follows
  $36525/(\dot L/360) = 7.154553$ days — exactly the value with which Ganymede actually orbits in the
  model, and which the fact sheet also gives. Unlike for Io and Europa, the table's column P is not
  flagged as wrong for Ganymede (header comment in `jupiter-monde.ts`); an independent Kepler
  calculation with Jupiter's `GM` and Ganymede's mass (`umlaufzeitTage` in `sim/orbit.ts`) gave
  7.154882 days, only 0.0046% (about 28 s) above the model value — much closer than for Io (0.043%) or
  Europa.
- **Rotation ratio:** `rotationPeriodH` (171.70927 h) matches the sidereal period computed from
  $\dot L$ to $-0.012 \cdot 10^{-6}$ relative; the angle between the map centre and the direction to
  Jupiter is 39.0° at the epoch and 39.1° after a hundred years (figures as in
  [Bound rotation](thema:gebundene-rotation)).
- **Axial tilt:** Recomputing with `achsneigungDeg` gives $0.210^\circ$, slightly above the orbital
  inclination $i = 0.2^\circ$: Ganymede's own rotation pole (268.20°/64.57°) does not exactly coincide
  with the orbit normal derived from Jupiter's pole and the orbital inclination. The data block rounds
  this to one decimal and therefore likewise shows 0.2°.
- **Pole:** 268.20°/64.57° from the IAU rotation elements (SPICE kernel), fixed at the epoch; close
  both to Jupiter's own pole and to Ganymede's Laplace pole listed separately in the orbital-element
  table (268.2°/64.6°, tilt 0.1°, recomputed as 0.122°).
- **Albedo and texture:** The albedo 0.44 is the fact sheet's geometric albedo, matched exactly. The
  texture is a USGS/NASA mosaic (`ASSETS.md`) and is normalised to the catalogue albedo like every
  body's ([Albedo and brightness](thema:photometrie)); neither the two terrain types nor the bright
  polar caps are drawn separately in it, `appearance` in `jupiter-monde.ts` carries only an albedo
  texture.
- **Shadows:** Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto are simultaneously occluders of Jupiter and of one
  another (`MAX_OKKLUDER` 4 in `render/shadows.ts`); each of the four moons is therefore shadowed by
  Jupiter and the three others. A moon at distance $r$ casts its shadow onto a sphere of radius $R$
  only if the solar elevation above the orbital plane stays below $\arcsin(R/r)$; with Jupiter's
  radius of 69,911 km ($R$) and Ganymede's orbital radius of 1,070,400 km ($r$) this gives about
  $3.74^\circ$ — just above Jupiter's recomputed axial tilt of 3.1200°. Ganymede therefore always
  casts a shadow, like Io and Europa, unlike Callisto with only 2.1° of permissible solar elevation.
- **Scale:** Ganymede is a satellite (`isSatellite`, its `parent` is Jupiter, not the Sun); its
  orbital radius around Jupiter therefore scales, like its own and Jupiter's radius, with
  `sizeScale` rather than with the distance compression applied to planetary orbits
  (`scaledPositionAt`).
- **Data panel:** The mass carried in the data set ($1.4819 \cdot 10^{23}\,\mathrm{kg}$), multiplied
  by $G$ from CODATA 2018, gives a $GM$ of $9890.645\,\mathrm{km^3\,s^{-2}}$, about 284 ppm above the
  value measured by orbit tracking in the table above. The data panel computes the orbital period
  from Kepler's law with $a$ and $G\,(M_\mathrm{Jup} + M)$ (`umlaufzeitTage`) as 7.154882 days,
  0.0046% above the sidereal period with which Ganymede actually moves in the model. Further
  simplifications: [Limits of the model](thema:modell).
